# Capitals, Realms and Borders

A map is not only towns and roads. It is also a pattern of power: who rules where, from which city, and where one ruler's land stops and another's begins. This chapter explains where capitals were built and why, what you find in and around them, how big kingdoms, duchies and counties were, and what borders really looked like in the Middle Ages, including why language lines rarely match them. It ends with practical advice for drawing realms, capitals and borders on your map.

## What makes a capital

> **Rule of thumb:** A capital sits in the richest, most loyal region of the realm, on navigable water, at a place that is easy to defend. Centrality matters less than you think.

### The five things a capital needs

| Need | Why | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| A rich core region | The court, officials and garrison must be fed. The ruler's own estates (the *royal domain*) supply food and money. | Paris sits in the grain lands of the Île-de-France. London sits in the Thames valley. Prague sits in the middle of the fertile Bohemian basin. |
| River or sea access | A capital grows into a big city. Big cities need grain by water (see [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md)). | Paris (Seine), London (Thames), Prague (Vltava), Kraków (Vistula), Buda (Danube), Cairo (Nile), Constantinople (Bosporus). |
| A defensible core site | The ruler's family, treasure and archives must be safe. | Kraków's Wawel hill, Prague Castle on its ridge, Buda's Castle Hill, Paris's island (Île de la Cité), Toledo in a deep loop of the Tagus river. |
| Loyalty | The capital is in the dynasty's home region, where the ruler owns land and the local nobles are relatives or clients. | The Capetian kings of France ruled from the Paris–Orléans region, their old family land. |
| Legitimacy | People must accept the ruler. Old sacred places, saints' tombs and old royal sites give that acceptance. | Westminster Abbey (coronations since 1066); Reims (French coronations); Aachen (Charlemagne's church). |

### Centre, fortress or frontier?

A ruler choosing a capital balances three pulls:

| Type | Where | Strength | Weakness | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central capital | Near the middle of the realm | Fair to all regions; short messenger times | Often not on the best river; no natural defences | Madrid, where Philip II settled his court in 1561. Its position near the centre of the Iberian peninsula is often given as one reason, though historians debate the others. |
| Core-region capital | In the dynasty's richest home region, which may be off-centre | Rich, loyal, easy to feed | Far from outer provinces | Paris, London, Kraków |
| Forward capital | Close to a frontier the ruler wants to push or defend | The ruler is near the army and the conquests | Exposed to attack | Ottoman capitals: Bursa (taken 1326), then Edirne (1360s), then Constantinople (1453), each closer to the conquests in Europe |

"Forward capital" is a modern geographers' term, but the pattern is old. The Castilian kings moved their main residences south as they conquered: Toledo (taken in 1085), then Seville (taken in 1248). The Babenberg rulers of Austria moved their seat east to Vienna in 1145, closer to the frontier with Hungary. (Austria became a duchy in 1156.)

> **Rule of thumb:** Expanding realms move their capital toward the frontier. Defensive or settled realms keep it in the old core.

### Two capitals in one: the palace and the merchant city

Many capitals were really two towns side by side: a palace quarter and a merchant city.

- **London and Westminster:** London was the merchant city. Westminster, about 3 km (2 mi) upstream, had the royal palace, the abbey and the law courts.
- **Prague, Kraków, Buda:** a royal castle on a hill (Hradčany, Wawel, Castle Hill) above or across the river from the merchant town (Prague's Old Town, Kraków's market town, Pest).
- **Islamic capitals:** rulers often built a new palace city beside the old one. The Fatimids founded al-Qahira (Cairo) in 969 next to the older city of Fustat. The caliph Abd al-Rahman III of Córdoba founded the palace city of Madinat al-Zahra in 936 (main building work perhaps from 940), about 5 km (3 mi) west of Córdoba.

> **Map tip:** On a local or regional map, draw the capital as two linked parts: a fortified palace on the high ground and a larger merchant town by the river, often with a bridge between them. On a kingdom map, one capital symbol is enough.

### Capital is not always the biggest city

The capital is usually the biggest city in a strong, centralised kingdom (London, Paris). In other realms it is not:

- The Holy Roman Empire (HRE) had no capital at all for most of the Middle Ages.
- The Valois dukes of Burgundy (1363–1477) moved their court between Dijon, Brussels, Lille and Bruges. Dijon, the old ducal capital, was much smaller than the great Flemish cloth cities of Ghent and Bruges.
- In Castile, the court moved between several cities, and the biggest city, Seville, was only one of its stops.

> **Map tip:** In a strong, centralised kingdom, make the capital the biggest city on the map. In a loose realm (an elective empire, a group of duchies under one ruler), put the court in a middling town and let a trading port or cloth city be bigger. That contrast tells the viewer how the realm works.

> **Fantasy twist:** A capital can also be chosen for magic: a ley-line crossing, a dragon-proof mountain, the only place a sacred tree grows. Keep the other four needs, though. Even a magical capital needs food, water transport and loyal land around it, unless you explain how magic replaces them. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

## Travelling courts and fixed capitals

> **Rule of thumb:** Before about 1200, most western kings had no fixed capital. The court travelled. Fixed capitals appear when government needs paper, courts and coin more than it needs to eat the king's food on the spot.

### Why courts travelled

- **The court went to the food.** Taxes and rents were often paid in food. Carting food was expensive (see [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)), so the king and several hundred followers moved from one royal estate to the next and ate what was stored there.
- **Presence was power.** A king who was seen, held court, judged disputes and received oaths in each region kept the loyalty of the nobles there.
- **Elections, not inheritance (in Germany).** HRE kings were elected. They came from different regions, so no single city became the permanent base.

### How it worked

| Feature | Numbers | Source or example |
|---|---|---|
| Spacing of German royal palaces (*Pfalzen*) and royal manors | About 30 km (19 mi), one day's travel for the royal train | Wikipedia *Kaiserpfalz* |
| Spacing of royal farms on routes | 24–31 km (15–19 mi) | Wikipedia *Itinerant court* |
| Length of stay | Rarely more than a few weeks; a few days at transit stops | German royal itineraries |
| Late example | Emperor Charles V (reigned 1519–56) made 40 journeys and spent about a quarter of his reign on the road | Wikipedia *Itinerant court* |

Famous Pfalzen include Aachen (Charlemagne's favourite, 790s), Ingelheim (c. 790), Paderborn and Goslar (built 1030–1055 near the silver mines and hunting forests of the Harz).

### When and why capitals became fixed

| Realm | Fixed capital | Date | What made it fixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Byzantium | Constantinople | From 330 | Salaried officials, coin taxes, huge walls. Never itinerant in the western sense. |
| Islamic caliphates | Baghdad (founded 762), Córdoba, Cairo (969) | 8th–10th centuries | Same: paid bureaucracy and coin taxes. New capitals were often planned from scratch. |
| France | Paris | From the 1190s, firmly by the mid-1300s | In July 1194 Philip II lost his travelling archives, seal and treasure to Richard the Lionheart at the battle of Fréteval. After that, the king kept his records in a fixed archive in Paris (the *Trésor des Chartes*) instead of carrying them around. The Parlement (high court, 1302) and the Chambre des comptes (audit office, c. 1303) sat in the royal palace on the Île de la Cité. |
| England | Westminster / London | From the 12th century, firmly by the mid-1300s | The Exchequer (treasury office) was based at Westminster by the reign of Henry II (1154–89). Magna Carta (1215), clause 17, said common pleas (ordinary lawsuits) must be held "in some fixed place". In practice that place became Westminster Hall. The Chancery (writing office) followed. |
| Poland | Kraków | Seat of government from 1038; coronations from 1320 | Royal castle and cathedral on Wawel hill. |
| Bohemia | Prague | Always the main seat; imperial capital under Charles IV (1346–78) | Archbishopric 1344, New Town and university 1348, Charles Bridge begun 1357. |
| Hungary | Buda (with moves) | Buda until 1315; Temesvár 1315–23; Visegrád 1323–1408; Buda again from 1408 | The seat followed the king's power base and his building projects. |
| Castile / Spain | Madrid | 1561 (Valladolid briefly 1601–06) | Before 1561 the court moved between Toledo, Valladolid, Burgos, Seville and others. |
| Holy Roman Empire | None | — | Remained itinerant. Dynasties used their own home cities (Prague, Vienna). |

The forces that fixed capitals were:

1. **Paperwork.** Archives, account rolls and registers became too bulky and too valuable to move.
2. **Law courts.** People with lawsuits needed a court they could find.
3. **Money taxes.** When taxes came in coin, food could be bought anywhere, so the court no longer had to travel to eat.
4. **A bigger staff.** Clerks, judges and accountants needed offices and houses.

> **Map tip:** If your kingdom is in an "early medieval" stage, do not draw one capital. Draw 5–15 royal residences scattered across the royal lands, about a day's travel apart along the main routes, and mark one or two as favourites. If your kingdom is "late medieval", draw one capital and a ring of royal residences around it (see the next section).

> **Later era (1500s+):** Fixed capitals win everywhere. Philip II fixed Madrid in 1561 and built the Escorial palace-monastery nearby (1563–84). Sigismund III moved the Polish court from Kraków to Warsaw in 1596, closer to Lithuania and the Baltic. German princes settled into fixed *Residenzstädte* (residence towns). See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Fast flying mounts, teleport circles or magical messages reduce the need to travel. A realm with them can have a fixed capital much earlier and govern a bigger area. A realm of nomadic or migrating people (or a king who must personally renew a magical oath in every province) stays itinerant.

## What is in and around a capital

> **Rule of thumb:** A capital is not one building. It is a cluster: palace, great church, treasury, writing office, law courts, mint, and later a university, surrounded within a day's ride by royal residences and hunting forests.

### Inside the capital

| Institution | What it is | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Palace or royal castle | Where the ruler lives and holds court; often on high ground or an island | Palais de la Cité and the Louvre (Paris); Westminster Palace; Prague Castle; Wawel (Kraków); Buda Castle (first royal residence built 1247–65) |
| Cathedral or great abbey | The ruler's main church, often a bishop's or archbishop's seat | Notre-Dame (Paris); St Vitus (Prague); Wawel Cathedral; Hagia Sophia (Constantinople) |
| Treasury | Stores coin, plate and crown jewels | The Paris house of the Knights Templar (the Temple) managed the French royal money from the 12th century until Philip IV moved it to the Louvre in 1307. Charles IV kept the imperial regalia (crown, sceptre and other symbols of rule) in Karlštejn castle, founded in 1348 about 30 km (19 mi) from Prague. |
| Chancery and archives | Writes and stores charters, letters and laws | Trésor des Chartes in Paris (set up after 1194) |
| Law courts | The high court of the realm | Westminster Hall; the Parlement in the Palais de la Cité |
| Mint | Makes the royal coinage | The Tower of London mint; the Paris mint |
| University (later) | Trains lawyers, clerks and priests for government and church | Many capitals founded one: Prague 1348, Kraków 1364, Vienna 1365 |
| Royal chapel or relic church | Holds relics that give the king holy status | Sainte-Chapelle, Paris (1248), built for the Crown of Thorns |

### How many mints?

> **Rule of thumb:** Early medieval kingdoms struck coins in many towns. Later, strong kings closed most mints and kept a few they could control. In loose realms, the opposite happened: more and more lords got the right to mint.

A mint was often a small workshop of a few moneyers (coin makers), not a big building. England is the best-recorded case:

| England | Number of mints | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Æthelred II (978–1016) | About 70 (one standard catalogue lists 73) | Mostly in boroughs (fortified towns, *burhs*), from London and York down to small places such as Lydford in Devon. A single coin type was struck at 60 or more places. |
| Henry II, 1154–58 | Nearly 50 | — |
| Henry II's recoinage of 1158 | 30 during the recoinage, then only 12 kept open | A *recoinage* is when old coins are called in and struck again as new ones. |
| Henry II's death, 1189 | 9 | — |
| Henry III (1216–72), between recoinages | 4: London, Canterbury, Bury St Edmunds and Durham | The capital plus three old church centres. For a big recoinage (1247), many old mints were briefly reopened. |

The Holy Roman Empire went the other way. From the 10th century, kings granted the right to mint to bishops, from the 11th century to lay princes, and later to towns. By the 12th century the empire was split into many regional coin areas, each with its own penny (*Pfennig*).

- **Early medieval or newly united realm:** a mint in most fortified towns.
- **Centralised kingdom of the High or Late Middle Ages:** a mint in the capital, one to three church or regional centres, and one at each big silver mine (see [Industry, Resources and Special Towns](07-industry-and-resources.md#mining-and-metals)).
- **Loose realm (an empire of princes, a land of city-states):** a mint in every princely seat, many bishops' cities and the big free towns.

> **Map tip:** Mints are too small for a symbol on a kingdom map, but they are good detail for a town description or a regional map. Use the number of mints to show what kind of realm it is: many mints for an old or loose realm, three or four for a strong, centralised one.

### Ceremonial cities: coronation and burial

The city where a ruler is **crowned** or **buried** is often not the city where he governs. The ceremonial city is usually an older place, where the dynasty or the church first became strong. It keeps that role for centuries, even after the government has moved elsewhere.

| Realm | Coronation | Burial | Government |
|---|---|---|---|
| France | Reims (nearly all kings; about 130 km / 80 mi from Paris) | Saint-Denis abbey, just north of Paris | Paris |
| England | Westminster Abbey (since 1066) | Westminster Abbey (rebuilt by Henry III from 1245) | Westminster / London (Winchester was an older royal and treasury centre) |
| Holy Roman Empire | Aachen (about 30 kings crowned 936–1531), then Rome for the imperial crown | Speyer cathedral (Salian emperors) and others | No capital. The Golden Bull (1356) fixed elections at Frankfurt, coronation at Aachen and the first diet (assembly) of each reign at Nuremberg. |
| Hungary | Székesfehérvár (about 38 monarchs crowned, 1038–1527) | Székesfehérvár | Esztergom, Visegrád, Buda (about 60 km / 37 mi from Székesfehérvár) |
| Poland | Gniezno (early), then Kraków's Wawel (from 1320) | Wawel Cathedral | Kraków |
| Scotland | Scone | Dunfermline abbey | Edinburgh, from the 15th century |
| Aragon | Zaragoza | Poblet monastery | Barcelona, Zaragoza, Valencia (itinerant) |

> **Rule of thumb:** Governments move; sacred sites stay. A small town with a coronation church or royal tombs can be more important on your map than a large trading city.

Legitimacy can also be borrowed. After the Mongols destroyed Baghdad in 1258, the Mamluk sultans of Egypt brought a member of the Abbasid family to Cairo (1261) and kept him there as a "shadow caliph" to bless their rule.

### Around the capital: the royal landscape

Within about one day's ride of a fixed capital you usually find:

| Feature | Distance from capital | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| A ring of royal residences: smaller palaces, manors and castles | 5–60 km (3–37 mi) | London: Eltham and Sheen (~13 km / 8 mi), Windsor (~35 km / 22 mi). Paris: Vincennes (~7 km / 4 mi), Saint-Germain-en-Laye (~20 km / 12 mi), Fontainebleau (~55 km / 34 mi). Buda: Visegrád (~35 km / 22 mi). Prague: Karlštejn (~30 km / 19 mi). Straight-line distances, rounded. |
| Royal hunting forests and lodges | 10–100 km (6–60 mi) | England: the New Forest (c. 1079); Windsor Forest; Woodstock and Clarendon hunting palaces. Kraków: Niepołomice hunting castle (built by Casimir the Great in the mid-1300s). |
| The ruler's favourite abbeys and burial church | 0–20 km (0–12 mi) | Westminster, Saint-Denis |
| Estates of courtiers and officials | Within 1–2 days' ride | Nobles' town houses in the capital and country manors in the home counties |

**Royal forests** were legal hunting zones that could contain villages, fields and heath as well as woods (see [Royal forests](06-villages-and-countryside.md#royal-forests)). Kings used them for hunting and to raise money from fines and licences.

> **Map tip:** Around your capital, draw 3–6 small royal residences within about 60 km (37 mi), at least one big royal forest with a hunting lodge, a great abbey or burial church close by, and, if you want, a separate coronation town within 50–150 km (30–90 mi). Label royal forests in the same colour as the crown's land. These details make a capital feel real.

## Political units and their sizes

> **Rule of thumb:** Kingdom > duchy (or principality) > county > barony or lordship > manor. (A *fief* is any land held from a lord in return for loyalty and service; a *vassal* is the person who holds it.) Each level is usually several times to a few dozen times smaller in area than the one above. But medieval units overlapped, were scattered and changed constantly. Do not expect a neat pyramid.

### The units, top to bottom

| Unit | Typical area | Typical population (c. 1300) | Seat | Notes and examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Empire | 0.8–1.7 million km² (300,000–650,000 sq mi) | 5–15 million | One great capital, or none (HRE) | HRE 0.8–1.1m km²; Carolingian Empire c. 800 ~1.2m km²; Byzantium in 1025, 1.675m km² |
| Kingdom | 10,000–450,000 km² (4,000–175,000 sq mi) | 0.1–17 million | Capital or itinerant court | Navarre ~10,000–12,000 km² (~100,000 people c. 1300); Scotland ~79,000; Portugal ~89,000; England 130,000; Hungary 280,000–325,000; France was the giant |
| Duchy or principality (great fief) | 10,000–50,000 km² (4,000–19,000 sq mi) | 0.5–2 million (estimates) | A ducal city with castle, cathedral and court | Rough areas, based on the matching modern regions: Normandy ~30,000 km²; Brittany ~34,000 km²; Flanders (a county, but as powerful as a duchy) ~10,000 km² |
| County (shire) | 1,000–10,000 km² (400–4,000 sq mi); England averaged ~3,300 km² | 20,000–300,000; England averaged ~120,000 | County town with castle, sheriff and county court | 39 English shires: Rutland 394 km² (152 sq mi) to Yorkshire ~15,700 km² (6,067 sq mi). Hungary had ~70 counties in the 15th century, averaging ~4,000–4,500 km². |
| Hundred or wapentake (English sub-county district) | Average ~130–150 km² (50–60 sq mi) in 1086, ~150–200 km² (60–80 sq mi) by 1316; range 4.6–982 km² (2–380 sq mi) | 2,000–10,000 | A meeting place, often open-air: a mound, a ford, a crossroads, a stone | 812 hundreds can be traced in Domesday Book (1086), from Worth in Kent (4.6 km²) to Salford in Lancashire (982 km²); roughly 10–12 parishes each in 1086; after mergers about 630 named hundreds remained by 1316, with 10–15 parishes each |
| Barony, lordship, honour (a great lord's group of estates) | Very variable: a few manors to 100+ | — | The *caput* (head manor), usually with a castle | A great baron's manors were often scattered across several counties |
| Knight's fee (land expected to support one knight) | 1–2 manors; no fixed size | — | A manor house | England had only about 5,000–6,000 knights' fees (1166 survey) |
| Manor | 2–10 km² (one village, part of one, or several) | 50–500 | Manor house, often moated | The village of Elton (Huntingdonshire) had 37 recorded households in 1086 (roughly 170–190 people), split among three manors |
| Parish | ~14 km² in the lowlands; 50–100+ km² in uplands | 75–500 | Parish church | ~9,500 parishes in England c. 1300 |

### Units do not nest neatly

- **Manors and villages often differed.** In a 1279 survey of 134 Leicestershire villages, only 37 had a single lord. 63 villages (47%) were split among three or more lords.
- **Baronies were scattered.** In 1086 the people who held land directly from the king were called *tenants-in-chief*. A small group of great barons held a large share of the country; one common estimate is that about a dozen men held around a quarter of it. Their manors lay in many counties.
- **Counties had detached pieces.** Many English counties had small "islands" of land inside neighbouring counties. An Act of 1844 removed many of them, but some survived into the 20th century.
- **Church lands cut across everything.** Bishops and abbots held roughly a quarter of England in 1086 (estimates vary), and a bishop's lands did not match his diocese.

### Who sits where

| Lord | Seat | What the seat looks like |
|---|---|---|
| King | Capital or main palaces | See above |
| Duke or great count | Main city of the duchy | Big castle or palace, cathedral, mint, the duke's own abbey and burial church. Example: Rouen for Normandy, Dijon for Burgundy, Ghent and Bruges for Flanders. |
| Count or sheriff (royal officer) | County town | Royal castle with the sheriff's office and gaol, the county court, a market. In England many shires are named after their county town: Warwickshire, Oxfordshire, Bedfordshire. In Hungary each county head (*ispán*) sat in a royal castle. |
| Baron | Caput of the barony | A castle (see [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md)), often with a small borough and a priory founded by the family |
| Knight or manor lord | Manor | Manor house with hall, barns and often a moat, next to the parish church |

### How many units in one kingdom?

| Realm | Count | Source |
|---|---|---|
| England c. 1300 | 1 kingdom; 39 shires (plus special zones such as the Palatinate of Durham); about 630 hundreds (628 named in a 1316 inquest; 812 can be traced in Domesday Book, 1086); ~9,500 parishes; 5,000–6,000 knights' fees | Domesday; 1166 survey; 1316 inquest; baseline |
| France c. 1200 | Royal domain plus 6 great lay fiefs: the duchies of Burgundy, Normandy and Aquitaine and the counties of Flanders, Champagne and Toulouse (the six lay "peers of France", fixed c. 1180), plus many lesser counties | Peerage of France |
| Hungary | ~37 counties named for 1038 (many of them disputed), ~45–50 (c. 1074), ~70 (late 14th and 15th centuries) | Wikipedia *Administrative divisions of the Kingdom of Hungary* |
| Carolingian Empire c. 800 | Somewhere between about 110 and 600 counties (estimates vary widely) | Wikipedia *Carolingian Empire* |
| Holy Roman Empire 1521 | About 400 *imperial estates* (territories and cities with a place in the imperial diet). About 53 were ecclesiastical principalities (archbishops and bishops, three of the archbishops being electors) and 87 were free and imperial cities. The rest were secular princes, counts and lords, abbots and abbesses. | Worms register of 1521 |
| China (for comparison) | About 1,230 counties (*xian*) under the Song (960–1279); about 1,385 under the Ming (1368–1644) | Skinner 1977 |

> **Map tip:** For a kingdom the size of England (130,000 km², 50,000 sq mi), draw 30–40 counties, each about 50–70 km (30–45 mi) across, each with a county town near its middle. Group them into 4–8 bigger regions held by great lords or old duchies. Draw duchy borders with a thicker line than county borders. Leave 1–3 special zones (a march, a palatinate, a church territory) with their own colour or hatching.

### A different model: the bureaucratic empire (China)

> **Rule of thumb:** An empire run by salaried officials gives a much more regular map than feudal Europe: county seats of similar size, spread fairly evenly, each one a walled government town, with market towns between them and few private castles.

**Why it is different.** In imperial China the basic unit was the county (*xian*). A magistrate ran it. He was an official appointed by the central government, not a lord who inherited the land. A magistrate could not serve in his home county (the "rule of avoidance", strictly enforced from the 6th century), and he was moved to a new post after a few years (in Ming times usually two or three). Nobody owned a county, so counties were not split by inheritance, scattered by marriage or turned into private lordships. Their number also stayed surprisingly steady from one dynasty to the next.

| Feature | Numbers | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Number of counties | Song: about 1,230. Ming: about 1,385. A recent study argues that the Song also had hundreds of "administrative towns" with officials doing county-like work. | Skinner 1977; Han and Sng 2023 |
| Average county size | About 2,000–2,500 km² (770–970 sq mi) in Song China: about 1,230 counties on 2.5–3.1 million km² (derived; area estimates vary). That is a little smaller than an average English shire (~3,300 km²). | Derived |
| Spacing of county seats | About 45–55 km (28–34 mi) apart on average (derived from the area above). Closer on crowded river plains, much farther apart in hills and on the frontier. | Derived |
| City walls | Of all administrative cities (county seats and above): 62% walled in 1368, 70% in 1393, 80% in 1469, 90% in 1540 and 95% in 1576 | Xue et al. 2021 |
| Below the county seat | Periodic market towns. In Skinner's model, each "standard market town" served about 18 villages (6 close by, 12 farther out) and roughly 6,000–12,000 people. Markets met on fixed days of a ten-day cycle, for example days 1, 4 and 7. A bigger market nearby used other days (for example 2, 5 and 8), so traders could visit both. | Skinner 1964–65 |

Be careful with Skinner's market figures. They come from the 19th and 20th centuries, when China was far more crowded than medieval Europe. Use them for the pattern, not for exact numbers in 1000–1500.

> **Map tip:** For a bureaucratic empire, draw county seats as walled towns of similar size, spread fairly evenly about 45–55 km (28–34 mi) apart on good farmland, closer on crowded plains and farther apart in the hills. Give each one a regular wall, often a rectangle on flat land. Group every few counties into a prefecture, with a bigger city as its seat. Fill the gaps with unwalled market towns, each in the middle of its ring of villages. Draw few or no private castles: in this kind of state, the walls belong to the government's towns.

> **Fantasy twist:** Other peoples may divide land differently. Dwarf holds might be counted by mountain or mine, not area; elves by forest; a sea people by islands or harbours. Keep the same rule: one seat per unit, one unit per day or two of travel.

## Borders and frontiers

> **Rule of thumb:** In settled farmland, borders were known lines, walked and argued over field by field. In empty land (mountains, forest, marsh, desert, steppe), borders were wide zones. Draw lines where people live and bands where they do not.

### What borders follow

| Feature | How often used | Why | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old boundaries | Very often | Borders are "sticky". Once fixed, they last for centuries. | Many French dioceses follow Roman city territories. Many English shire borders lasted from the 10th or 11th century until 1974. |
| Watersheds and mountain crests | Often, but not always | Mountains are empty, so a crest line divides little. | Much of the Pyrenees. But the Val d'Aran, on the French side of the crest, belonged to Aragon, and Lower Navarre lay north of the mountains. |
| Pass states (an exception) | In the Alps | Whoever holds a pass wants both sides of it. | Savoy, Tyrol and the Swiss canton of Uri straddled the main Alpine passes instead of stopping at the crest. |
| Rivers | Sometimes | Easy to name in a treaty and easy to see. But a river valley is one economic unit, so peoples on both banks are often the same. | Tweed (England–Scotland, Treaty of York 1237); Scheldt (France–Empire, from the partition of 843); the Danube (old Roman frontier) |
| Forests, marshes, heaths | Often in early times | Thin population; hard to cross. | The "Wildnis" (wilderness) left between the Teutonic Order's Prussia and Lithuania |
| Empty wasteland | Frontier wars | Raiding emptied the land between enemies. | The Castile–Granada frontier (1246–1492); the steppe "Wild Fields" north of the Black Sea |
| Man-made dykes and walls | Rare but striking | Mark a claim or block raids | Offa's Dyke on the Welsh border (traditionally credited to King Offa of Mercia, who reigned 757–796, though recent dating suggests parts are older); the Danevirke in Denmark; the Scots' Dike (1552) |

### Frontier zones vs border lines

- **Line borders** existed in settled land. Parish and manor bounds were marked with stones, crosses, ditches and old trees, and villagers walked them every year ("beating the bounds"). Treaties named rivers and landmarks. The Anglo-Scottish border fixed by the Treaty of York (1237) changed very little afterwards; the only later changes were Berwick and the Debatable Lands.
- **Zone frontiers** were the norm on the edges of Christian Europe, in mountains and in war zones. Here power faded out over 20–100+ km (12–60+ mi). People paid tribute to both sides, or to neither.

### Marches: militarised border provinces

A **march** (German *Mark*, Italian *marca*) was a border province given to a strong lord to defend. The **margrave** (march count) or marcher lord had more military power than an ordinary count.

| March | Dates | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish March (Marca Hispanica) | From c. 795; Barcelona taken 801 | A ring of counties (Barcelona, Girona, Urgell, Cerdanya and others) facing al-Andalus. The count of Barcelona was effectively independent by the late 980s; the march became Catalonia. |
| Welsh March | From the 1070s to 1536 | Dozens of small Norman lordships between England and Wales. Marcher lords ruled "like kings": own courts, own castles, private war, their own markets and boroughs. Britain's densest cluster of motte-and-bailey castles (castles on a man-made earth mound with a fenced yard). The lords' special powers were abolished in 1536 by the first of the Laws in Wales Acts (1535–42). |
| Anglo-Scottish Marches | 13th century to 1603 | Three marches (East, Middle, West) on each side, each under a royal Warden. A special border law, first written down in 1249 by six English and six Scottish knights. Raiding clans ("reivers"). |
| Brandenburg | From 1134 | Albert the Bear was made margrave of the Northern March in 1134 and called himself Margrave of Brandenburg from 1157. It grew into a major principality. |
| Austria | From the 10th century | The eastern march of Bavaria; Vienna became its seat in 1145, and Austria became a duchy in 1156. |

Other frontier words that survive as place names: *Extremadura* (often explained as "the far edge of the Duero" lands), *Marche* (France and Italy), *Mark*, and *Krajina* (Slavic for "borderland").

> **Rule of thumb:** A march is bigger, emptier and more fortified than an inland county of the same importance. Expect noticeably more castles per area (the Welsh March has Britain's densest cluster of earth-and-timber castles) and fewer, smaller towns.

### Buffer states, enclaves, exclaves and contested land

| Thing | What it is | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Buffer state | A small realm between two big ones. Each big neighbour prefers it independent rather than in the other's hands. | Navarre between France, Castile and Aragon (its southern part was conquered by Ferdinand of Aragon in 1512 and later joined to Castile); Savoy on the Alpine passes; Wallachia and Moldavia between Hungary, Poland and the Ottomans; Andorra (co-ruled since 1278 by the bishop of Urgell and the count of Foix; 468 km² / 181 sq mi) |
| Enclave / exclave | A piece of one realm surrounded by another (an enclave from the outside view, an exclave from the owner's view) | Baarle (Low Countries): medieval land deals between the dukes of Brabant and the lords of Breda left dozens of tiny pockets that still exist (22 Belgian pieces inside the Netherlands today). Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin: papal land inside France from 1274 (Venaissin) and 1348 (Avignon) until 1791. |
| Overseas bridgehead | A defended exclave across a sea | The Pale of Calais, English from 1347 to 1558. It ran roughly from Gravelines to Wissant, about 35 km (22 mi) of coast, with marshy, often-changing borders inland. It was supplied by sea. |
| Palatinate | A border county where a great lord holds royal powers | The prince-bishops of Durham guarded England against Scotland |
| Contested land | Claimed by both sides, ruled by neither | The Debatable Lands on the Anglo-Scottish border: about 16 × 6.4 km (10 × 4 mi), disputed for about three centuries and a refuge for raiders, split in 1552 by the Scots' Dike. Berwick-upon-Tweed changed hands more than a dozen times before becoming English for good in 1482. |

### Border markers and crossings

- **Markers:** boundary stones, crosses, ditches, banks, marked trees and posts. Treaty commissioners rode the line and ordered markers to be set up. For the Scots' Dike (1552), an earth bank a little over 5 km (3 mi) long, they ordered a stone with a cross at each end.
- **Crossings:** bridges, fords and passes on main roads, with a toll station, often a border castle on each side, and an inn or hospice. Tolls and customs could be collected at sea straits too: Denmark's Sound Toll at Helsingør (from 1429).
- **Meeting places:** on the Anglo-Scottish border, wardens met on fixed "truce days" at traditional spots on the line to settle complaints.

> **Map tip:** Draw lines where settled farmland meets, and a hatched band (a "march") across mountains, forests and war zones. Put paired castles facing each other where a main road crosses the border. Add one or two odd features: an enclave, a tiny buffer state, a disputed strip. These make borders look historical rather than planned. Castle spacing along frontiers is covered in [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Monsters behave like an extra enemy. A monster-haunted forest or mountain becomes a frontier zone even between friendly realms, with marcher lords, watchtowers and wide empty bands. A magical barrier (a ward line, a cursed river) can create a sharp line border even in wilderness.

## Language lines are not borders

> **Rule of thumb:** A language line is not a political border. It is usually older and it moves much more slowly. Land changes hands across a language line many times while the line itself stays put. When a language line does move, it creeps a few villages at a time over centuries.

### Why they differ

- **Villagers stay put.** When the lord or king changes, the villagers keep speaking what their grandparents spoke. Language follows who settled and farmed the land long ago, not who rules it now.
- **Realms are built across language lines.** Marriage, inheritance and war join lands with different languages into one realm, and split lands with one language between several.
- **Lines move by slow pressure.** A language spreads when towns, lords, the church and trade use it. Its rival survives longest in remote villages. A language can also jump in all at once, when a ruler brings in settlers.

### Real examples

| Example | What happened | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| The Romance–Germanic line in today's Belgium | It took shape as Frankish settlers moved in, roughly between the 4th and 10th centuries. After about the 10th century it hardly moved in what is now Belgium. (Farther west, the coast from Boulogne to Dunkirk became French-speaking only in the later Middle Ages.) Medieval states sat across it: the County of Flanders held Romance-speaking (Picard) Lille and Douai, and the Prince-Bishopric of Liège held Romance-speaking (Walloon) Liège and the Dutch-speaking County of Loon. The line became a legal boundary only in 1962–63. | A stable line, with realms on both sides of it |
| Breton in Brittany | Breton reached its farthest east in the 9th century, roughly from the bay of Mont-Saint-Michel to Donges on the Loire (the "Loth line", named after the linguist Joseph Loth). Even then the region's chief cities, Rennes and Nantes, stayed Romance-speaking. After that the line moved west: to about Dinan–Pornichet by the 12th century, and to about Binic–Guérande by 1588. That is roughly 30–80 km (20–50 mi) in seven centuries (derived from these places). | A slow retreat inside one realm, with the capital on the "wrong" side of the line |
| Transylvanian Saxons (a language island) | King Géza II of Hungary (1141–62) began bringing German-speaking settlers to the thinly settled south-eastern edge of his kingdom, to farm it and defend it. The *Diploma Andreanum* (1224) gave them self-government on their own "Royal Land". Their chief towns included Hermannstadt (Sibiu), Kronstadt (Brașov) and Bistritz (Bistrița), and they built about 300 villages with fortified churches. In 1848 the Royal Land still held 271 villages, market towns and towns. | A language island planted by a ruler, lasting for centuries inside another language area |
| Mudéjar villages in Valencia | After James I of Aragon conquered Valencia (1230s–40s), whole villages of Muslims (*Mudéjars*) kept their Arabic speech and their faith under Christian lords. In the late 1300s they may still have been a majority of the countryside. See [Religious, Cultural, Legal and Ancient Sites](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#muslims-under-christian-rule). | The border moved at once; the language of the villages did not |

> **Map tip:** Draw languages or cultures as a separate layer: a soft tint or a thin dotted coloured line, never the same line as a border. Let it cross borders, and let one realm hold two or three language areas. Sometimes put the capital on the "wrong" side of the line, like Rennes. Change the place-name endings across the line, for example German-style *-ingen* and *-heim* on one side and French-style *-ville* and *-court* on the other (see [Naming settlements realistically](01-settlement-placement.md#naming-settlements-realistically)). Add one to three language islands where a ruler planted settlers: on an empty frontier, in a mining district or around a new town.

> **Fantasy twist:** Long-lived peoples change their speech slowly, so elf or dwarf language lines can be even older and more stable than human ones. A dwarf hold or an elven wood inside a human kingdom is a language island: give it its own place names, even if a human king rules the land around it.

## Kinds of states and how they look on a map

> **Rule of thumb:** Do not colour all of Europe-like land in big, solid blocks. Medieval maps were patchworks: kingdoms, duchies, free cities, church lands, leagues and empires overlapped and interlocked.

| Kind of state | How it works | How it looks on a map | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feudal kingdom | A king over dukes, counts and barons who hold land in return for loyalty and service. The royal domain is only one part. | One outline, but inside it a patchwork of great fiefs. The royal domain is the core around the capital. Some fiefs are nearly independent. | France c. 1200: royal domain in the Île-de-France, surrounded by Normandy, Flanders, Champagne, Burgundy, Aquitaine and Toulouse |
| City-state and its *contado* | A self-governing city rules the countryside around it (the *contado*, often the same area as its diocese) and later conquers neighbouring cities. | A city with a territory of roughly 20–50 km (12–30 mi) radius, many such states side by side, with borders that move often. | Florence (took Arezzo in 1384, conquered Pisa in 1406). In 1427 the Florentine state had roughly 260,000 people, of whom the city held only about 37,000–38,000 (the *catasto* tax survey, studied by Herlihy and Klapisch-Zuber). Also Milan, Siena, Genoa. |
| League of towns | Towns cooperate for trade and defence but keep their own small territories. | A network, not an area: dots linked by sea routes, plus trading posts in foreign cities. | Hanseatic League: between about 70 and 170 member towns at different times, and nearly 200 places linked to it overall. Its power peaked around 1370 (Peace of Stralsund). Main trading posts (*Kontore*) in Novgorod, Bruges, London (the Steelyard) and Bergen; meetings (diets) usually in Lübeck |
| Confederation | Rural valleys and towns ally for defence and rule conquered land together. | A cluster of small units; some areas shown as joint subject lands. | Swiss Confederacy: 8 cantons by 1353, 13 by 1513. Aargau (1415) and Thurgau (1460) were ruled jointly as subject lands. |
| Composite empire | Many realms and hundreds of lords under one elected ruler with weak central power. | A big outer border with hundreds of small internal units. | Holy Roman Empire: ~400 imperial estates in 1521, including 87 imperial cities and ~50 prince-bishops and archbishops |
| Church state | A bishop, abbot or the Pope rules land as a prince. The territory is not the same as the diocese. | Separate colour; often scattered pieces. | Papal States in central Italy; prince-bishoprics of Cologne, Mainz, Trier, Liège, Münster, Würzburg, Salzburg; Durham in England |
| Military-order state | A crusading order of knights rules conquered land. | A frontier territory with many castles and commanderies (houses of the order). | Teutonic Order in Prussia, seat at Marienburg (Malbork) from 1309 |
| Maritime empire | A trading city holds ports and islands along a sea route, later also land behind it. | A home city plus a chain of coastal dots and islands, not a solid area. | Venice: Crete (c. 1210–1669), Negroponte (to 1470), Modon and Coron (1207–1500), Corfu (from 1386), Cyprus (1489–1570), Dalmatian ports; mainland expansion from 1337–39 (Mestre, Treviso) to Brescia and Bergamo (1420s) |
| Nomadic empire | Herders on seasonal pastures; settled peoples nearby pay tribute. | A huge, vague area: core pastures, a few capital cities, and a ring of tributary states around them. Borders are wide zones. | Mongols: Karakorum walled in 1235; the Golden Horde's Sarai on the lower Volga (founded by Batu in the 1240s–1250s), destroyed by Timur in 1395–96. Khans also kept moving camps and tent palaces. Rus' princes paid tribute but kept their own cities. |

### Drawing the unusual kinds

- **City-states:** many small territories, each 1–3 days across, centred on rival walled cities about 50–90 km (30–55 mi) apart.
- **Leagues:** do not colour an area. Put a shared symbol on each member town (members can sit inside other rulers' kingdoms) and dashed sea routes between them.
- **Nomadic empires:** show summer and winter pastures, one or two built capitals on rivers or trade routes, and the tributary ring in a lighter tint.
- **Maritime empires:** colour only the islands and harbour towns along the sea route; the land behind them keeps the local ruler's colour.

> **Map tip:** For a continent map, use at least three kinds of state: one or two big feudal kingdoms, one fragmented zone (city-states or a composite empire) and one unusual kind (a league, a church state, a maritime empire or nomads). This mix is what makes a medieval map look medieval.

> **Fantasy twist:** Different peoples suggest different state types. A dwarven realm may be a league of mountain holds linked by tunnels (a network, like the Hanse). An elven forest realm may look like a nomadic or church state with vague borders. A wizards' city may rule a contado like Florence.

## How big can a realm be

> **Rule of thumb:** A ruler can govern directly up to roughly 500–700 km (300–450 mi) from the capital. At a messenger's normal pace of about 50–65 km (30–40 mi) a day, that is about 8–12 days' travel. Beyond that, the ruler needs viceroys (governors who rule in the king's name), great vassals, sea routes or a relay post, or the realm splits. This is a guide's heuristic, not a measured law.

### Why distance limits power

- Orders, tax money and news travel at the speed of a horse (see the travel table in [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)).
- A rebellion far away can grow for weeks before the king even hears of it.
- Feudal armies often owed only about 40 days of service, so a host raised in the core could not campaign for long on a far frontier.

### Distance from the capital and what it means

Speeds from the shared baseline: royal messenger 50–90 km (30–56 mi) a day; army with baggage 13–20 km (8–12 mi) a day.

| Distance from capital | Royal messenger | Army | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 km (60 mi) | 1–2 days | 5–8 days | Core: royal domain, residences, hunting forests. Direct control. |
| 300 km (190 mi) | 3–6 days | 15–23 days | Normal kingdom: counties ruled by royal officers (sheriffs, bailiffs, *ispáns*). |
| 600 km (370 mi) | 7–12 days | 30–45 days | Edge of a large kingdom: needs great lords, marcher lords or a royal governor. |
| 1,000+ km (600+ mi) | 11–20 days | 50–75+ days | Only possible for empires with sea routes, relay posts or semi-independent governors. |

A real measure comes from news reaching Venice around 1500 (after Sardella and Braudel). Venice had one of the best news networks in Europe. Typical times were:

| From | Time for news to reach Venice (c. 1500) |
|---|---|
| Milan, Genoa, Florence | About 1 week |
| Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Vienna, Buda, Naples | About 2 weeks |
| Antwerp, Cologne, Barcelona | About 3 weeks |
| London | 3–4 weeks |
| Castile (Burgos, Valladolid), Constantinople | 4–5 weeks |
| Lisbon | About 6 weeks |

### Realistic realm sizes

| Realm | Area | Population | Farthest point from the capital (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Andorra (micro-state) | 468 km² | tiny | Under 1 day |
| Navarre | ~10,000–12,000 km² | ~100,000 (c. 1300) | 2–3 days |
| Scotland | ~79,000 km² | 0.5–1m (c. 1300; estimates vary) | 5–8 days |
| Portugal (border almost unchanged since 1297) | ~89,000 km² | ~1.0m (1500) | 4–7 days |
| England | 130,000 km² | 4.75m (1290) | London to Berwick ~500 km (310 mi) in a straight line, ~550 km (340 mi) by road: 6–11 days |
| Poland (1370) | ~240,000 km² | ~2m | 6–10 days |
| Hungary | 280,000–325,000 km² | 2–3m | 8–12 days |
| France (1328) | smaller than modern France's 551,000 km² | 16–17m | 10–14 days to the Mediterranean or the Pyrenees |
| Holy Roman Empire | 0.8–1.1m km² | 11–14m (c. 1300) | Weeks; no single capital |
| Byzantium (1025) | 1.675m km² | ~12m | Weeks, but sea routes helped |

Around 1500, Europe held roughly 500 more or less independent political units (Tilly 1975), from tiny lordships to great kingdoms. Most were small.

Realms that grew too big had three choices. They split (the Carolingian Empire at Verdun in 843; the Mongol Empire into separate khanates by the 1260s). They were run through regents and governors (Charles V). Or they held their distant parts by sea (Venice, Byzantium).

> **Map tip:** Measure from your capital along the roads, not in a straight line. Mark the 5-day and 12-day messenger lines in pencil (about 250–300 km and 600–700 km, or 150–190 mi and 370–430 mi, at a normal messenger pace). Put direct royal counties inside the first line, great vassals and marches between the lines, and tributary or semi-independent lands beyond the second. If your kingdom is much bigger than that, add a second-tier capital (a viceroy's seat) or make the outer parts nearly independent.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Organised postal relays (Taxis, from about 1500, up to ~150 km or 95 mi a day) and faster shipping roughly double the range a capital can control. Empires like Spain and the Ottomans govern huge areas through viceroys and provincial governors. Borders are increasingly surveyed, mapped and marked by boundary commissions. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Every faster messenger extends the realm. A griffon post at 300 km (190 mi) a day is 3–6 times faster than a royal messenger, so orders reach 3–6 times farther in the same time. Instant magical messages remove the communication limit but not the army limit. Armies with baggage still march at 13–20 km (8–12 mi) a day unless magic moves them too.

## Drawing realms, borders and capitals

### Step by step

1. **Choose the realm type** and **find the core:** the best farmland on a navigable river, with a defensible hill or island for the capital.
2. **Add the royal landscape:** 3–6 residences, a royal forest, the burial church, perhaps a separate coronation town.
3. **Draw the outer border:** lines in settled land, march bands across mountains, forests, marshes and war zones.
4. **Divide** into 4–8 duchies or great fiefs, then counties 50–70 km (30–45 mi) across, each with a seat near its middle. Add a few oddities (church land, an enclave, a free city, a disputed strip).
5. **Check distance:** anything more than about 12 days' messenger travel (roughly 700 km or 430 mi by road) from the capital needs a viceroy or becomes semi-independent.

### Symbols and line styles

| Feature | Suggested symbol |
|---|---|
| Capital | Star, or a crowned dot, with the name in bold capitals |
| Coronation or burial city | Small crown or cross beside the town symbol |
| Duchy or regional seat | Smaller star or castle-in-circle |
| County town | Double circle |
| Royal residence or hunting lodge | Small crown or tower symbol |
| Realm border | Thick solid line, or a coloured band along the inside edge |
| Duchy border | Medium dashed line |
| County border | Thin dotted line (only on regional maps) |
| Disputed border | Two parallel lines, or alternating colours |
| March or frontier zone | Hatching, or a fading colour band 10–50 km (6–30 mi) wide |
| Enclave | Same colour as its owner, with a thin outline |

### Colour and labels

- Use pale fills for realms, so roads and towns stay readable. Give vassal duchies a lighter shade of their king's colour.
- Give church lands, free cities and leagues their own colours or symbols. Do not merge them into the surrounding kingdom.
- Write realm names in widely spaced capitals across the whole territory. Write duchies in smaller italic capitals and counties in lower case.
- Label marches and frontier zones by name ("the West March", "the Debatable Land"). Named empty spaces make a map feel lived-in.

> **Map tip:** On a continent map, show only realms and great fiefs. On a kingdom map, add counties and the capital's royal landscape. On a local map, add hundreds, manors, parish bounds and boundary stones. Do not show every level at every scale.

## Quick summary

- A capital needs a rich core region, navigable water, a defensible site, loyal land around it and a source of legitimacy. Centrality matters less.
- Expanding realms move their capital toward the frontier ("forward capital"); settled realms keep the old core.
- Before c. 1200 most western courts travelled. German kings moved between royal palaces and manors about 30 km (19 mi) apart. Fixed capitals came with archives, law courts and money taxes: Paris and Westminster in the 12th–14th centuries, Madrid only in 1561. The Holy Roman Empire never had one.
- Coronation and burial cities are often different from the governing city, and keep their role for centuries.
- Around a capital, draw 3–6 royal residences and hunting forests within about 60 km (37 mi). At their peak (late 12th and early 13th centuries), royal forests covered about one-third of southern England.
- Mints shrink as kings get stronger: about 70 in late Anglo-Saxon England, 9 in 1189, normally 4 under Henry III. Loose realms like the Holy Roman Empire had more and more.
- Typical sizes: kingdom 10,000–450,000 km²; duchy 10,000–50,000 km²; county 1,000–10,000 km² (England averaged ~3,300 km² and ~120,000 people); hundred ~130–150 km² in 1086 (~150–200 km² by 1316); manor 2–10 km².
- A bureaucratic empire (China) looks more regular: about 1,230–1,385 counties, each with a walled seat roughly 45–55 km (28–34 mi) from the next, and periodic market towns between them.
- Borders are lines in settled land and wide zones (marches) in empty land. They follow old boundaries, crests, rivers, forests and wasteland. Marches, buffer states, enclaves and contested strips were normal: add some.
- Language lines are older and slower than borders. Draw them as a separate layer that crosses borders, and add a few language islands of planted settlers.
- Medieval political maps are patchworks: kingdoms, city-states, leagues, church states, maritime chains and nomadic empires side by side. Europe c. 1500 had about 500 political units.
- Direct rule reaches roughly 500–700 km (300–450 mi), about 8–12 days' messenger travel. Beyond that, rulers need viceroys, vassals, sea routes or relays.
