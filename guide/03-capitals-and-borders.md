# Capitals, Realms and Borders

A map is not only towns and roads. It is also a pattern of power: who rules where, from which city, and where one ruler's land stops and another's begins. This chapter explains where capitals were built and why, what you find in and around them, how big kingdoms, duchies and counties were, and what borders really looked like in the Middle Ages. It ends with practical advice for drawing realms, capitals and borders on your map.

## What makes a capital

> **Rule of thumb:** A capital sits in the richest, most loyal region of the realm, on navigable water, at a place that is easy to defend. Centrality matters less than you think.

### The five things a capital needs

| Need | Why | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| A rich core region | The court, officials and garrison must be fed. The ruler's own estates (the *royal domain*) supply food and money. | Paris sits in the grain lands of the Île-de-France. London sits in the Thames valley. Prague sits in the middle of the fertile Bohemian basin. |
| River or sea access | A capital grows into a big city. Big cities need grain by water (see [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md)). | Paris (Seine), London (Thames), Prague (Vltava), Kraków (Vistula), Buda (Danube), Cairo (Nile), Constantinople (Bosporus). |
| A defensible core site | The ruler's family, treasure and archives must be safe. | Kraków's Wawel hill, Prague Castle on its ridge, Buda's Castle Hill, Paris's island (Île de la Cité), Toledo in a deep loop of the Tagus river. |
| Loyalty | The capital is in the dynasty's home region, where the ruler owns land and the local nobles are relatives or clients. | The Capetian kings of France ruled from the Paris–Orléans region, their old family land. |
| Legitimacy | People must accept the ruler. Old sacred places, saints' tombs and old royal sites give that acceptance. | Westminster Abbey (coronations since 1066); Reims (French coronations); Aachen (Charlemagne's church). |

### Centre, fortress or frontier?

A ruler choosing a capital balances three pulls:

| Type | Where | Strength | Weakness | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central capital | Near the middle of the realm | Fair to all regions; short messenger times | Often not on the best river; no natural defences | Madrid, chosen in 1561 because it was in the geometric centre of Spain |
| Core-region capital | In the dynasty's richest home region, which may be off-centre | Rich, loyal, easy to feed | Far from outer provinces | Paris, London, Kraków |
| Forward capital | Close to a frontier the ruler wants to push or defend | The ruler is near the army and the conquests | Exposed to attack | Ottoman capitals: Bursa (taken 1326), then Edirne (1360s), then Constantinople (1453), each closer to the conquests in Europe |

"Forward capital" is a modern geographers' term, but the pattern is old. The Castilian kings moved their main residences south as they conquered: Toledo (taken in 1085), then Seville (taken in 1248). The Babenberg dukes moved their seat to Vienna in the 1150s, on the edge facing Hungary.

> **Rule of thumb:** Expanding realms move their capital toward the frontier. Defensive or settled realms keep it in the old core.

### Two capitals in one: the palace and the merchant city

Many capitals were really two towns side by side: a palace quarter and a merchant city.

- **London and Westminster:** London was the merchant city. Westminster, about 3 km (2 mi) upstream, had the royal palace, the abbey and the law courts.
- **Prague, Kraków, Buda:** a royal castle on a hill (Hradčany, Wawel, Castle Hill) above or across the river from the merchant town (Prague's Old Town, Kraków's market town, Pest).
- **Islamic capitals:** rulers often built a new palace city beside the old one. The Fatimids founded al-Qahira (Cairo) in 969 next to the older city of Fustat. The caliphs of Córdoba built the palace city of Madinat al-Zahra (from 936) a few kilometres outside Córdoba.

> **Map tip:** On a local or regional map, draw the capital as two linked parts: a fortified palace on the high ground and a larger merchant town by the river, often with a bridge between them. On a kingdom map, one capital symbol is enough.

### Capital is not always the biggest city

The capital is usually the biggest city in a strong, centralised kingdom (London, Paris). In other realms it is not:

- The Holy Roman Empire (HRE) had no capital at all for most of the Middle Ages.
- The Burgundian dukes (1363–1477) moved their court between Dijon, Brussels, Lille and Bruges. Ghent and Bruges were bigger than the court towns.
- In Castile, the court moved between several cities, and the biggest city, Seville, was only one of its stops.

> **Fantasy twist:** A capital can also be chosen for magic: a ley-line crossing, a dragon-proof mountain, the only place a sacred tree grows. Keep the other four needs, though. Even a magical capital needs food, water transport and loyal land around it, unless you explain how magic replaces them. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

## Travelling courts and fixed capitals

> **Rule of thumb:** Before about 1200, most western kings had no fixed capital. The court travelled. Fixed capitals appear when government needs paper, courts and coin more than it needs to eat the king's food on the spot.

### Why courts travelled

- **The court went to the food.** Taxes and rents were often paid in food. Carting food was expensive (see [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)), so the king and several hundred followers moved from one royal estate to the next and ate what was stored there.
- **Presence was power.** A king who was seen, held court, judged disputes and received oaths in each region kept the loyalty of the nobles there.
- **Elections, not inheritance (in Germany).** HRE kings were elected. They came from different regions, so no single city became the permanent base.

### How it worked

| Feature | Numbers | Source or example |
|---|---|---|
| Spacing of German royal palaces (*Pfalzen*) and royal manors | About 30 km (18 mi), one day's travel for the royal train | Wikipedia *Kaiserpfalz* |
| Spacing of royal farms on routes | 24–31 km (15–19 mi) | Wikipedia *Itinerant court* |
| Length of stay | Rarely more than a few weeks; a few days at transit stops | German royal itineraries |
| Late example | Emperor Charles V (reigned 1519–56) made 40 journeys and spent about a quarter of his reign on the road | Wikipedia *Itinerant court* |

Famous Pfalzen include Aachen (Charlemagne's favourite, 790s), Ingelheim (c. 790), Paderborn and Goslar (built 1030–1055 near the silver mines and hunting forests of the Harz).

### When and why capitals became fixed

| Realm | Fixed capital | Date | What made it fixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Byzantium | Constantinople | From 330 | Salaried officials, coin taxes, huge walls. Never itinerant in the western sense. |
| Islamic caliphates | Baghdad (founded 762), Córdoba, Cairo (969) | 8th–10th centuries | Same: paid bureaucracy and coin taxes. New capitals were often planned from scratch. |
| France | Paris | From the 1190s, firmly by the mid-1300s | In 1194 Philip II lost his travelling archives to Richard the Lionheart at the battle of Fréteval. From 1195 royal records were kept in Paris. The Parlement (high court) and Chambre des comptes (audit office) settled in Paris during the 13th–14th centuries. |
| England | Westminster / London | From the 12th century, firmly by the mid-1300s | The Exchequer (treasury office) settled at Westminster in the 12th century. Magna Carta (1215), clause 17, said common pleas (ordinary lawsuits) must be held "in some fixed place", which meant Westminster Hall. The Chancery (writing office) followed. |
| Poland | Kraków | Seat of government from 1038; coronations from 1320 | Royal castle and cathedral on Wawel hill. |
| Bohemia | Prague | Always the main seat; imperial capital under Charles IV (1346–78) | Archbishopric 1344, New Town and university 1348, Charles Bridge begun 1357. |
| Hungary | Buda (with moves) | Buda until 1315; Temesvár 1315–23; Visegrád 1323–1408; Buda again from 1408 | The seat followed the king's power base and his building projects. |
| Castile / Spain | Madrid | 1561 (Valladolid briefly 1601–06) | Before 1561 the court moved between Toledo, Valladolid, Burgos, Seville and others. |
| Holy Roman Empire | None | — | Remained itinerant. Dynasties used their own home cities (Prague, Vienna). |

The forces that fixed capitals were:

1. **Paperwork.** Archives, account rolls and registers became too bulky and too valuable to move.
2. **Law courts.** People with lawsuits needed a court they could find.
3. **Money taxes.** When taxes came in coin, food could be bought anywhere, so the court no longer had to travel to eat.
4. **A bigger staff.** Clerks, judges and accountants needed offices and houses.

> **Map tip:** If your kingdom is in an "early medieval" stage, do not draw one capital. Draw 5–15 royal residences scattered across the royal lands, about a day's travel apart along the main routes, and mark one or two as favourites. If your kingdom is "late medieval", draw one capital and a ring of royal residences around it (see the next section).

> **Later era (1500s+):** Fixed capitals win everywhere. Philip II fixed Madrid in 1561 and built the Escorial palace-monastery nearby (1563–84). Sigismund III moved the Polish court from Kraków to Warsaw in 1596, closer to Lithuania and the Baltic. German princes settled into fixed *Residenzstädte* (residence towns). See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Fast flying mounts, teleport circles or magical messages reduce the need to travel. A realm with them can have a fixed capital much earlier and govern a bigger area. A realm of nomadic or migrating people (or a king who must personally renew a magical oath in every province) stays itinerant.

## What is in and around a capital

> **Rule of thumb:** A capital is not one building. It is a cluster: palace, great church, treasury, writing office, law courts, mint, and later a university, surrounded within a day's ride by royal residences and hunting forests.

### Inside the capital

| Institution | What it is | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Palace or royal castle | Where the ruler lives and holds court; often on high ground or an island | Palais de la Cité and the Louvre (Paris); Westminster Palace; Prague Castle; Wawel (Kraków); Buda Castle (first royal residence built 1247–65) |
| Cathedral or great abbey | The ruler's main church, often an archbishop's seat | Notre-Dame (Paris); St Vitus (Prague); Wawel Cathedral; Hagia Sophia (Constantinople) |
| Treasury | Stores coin, plate and crown jewels | The Paris Temple held the French royal treasure in the 13th century. Charles IV kept the imperial regalia in Karlštejn castle, about 30 km (19 mi) from Prague. |
| Chancery and archives | Writes and stores charters, letters and laws | Trésor des Chartes in Paris (from 1195) |
| Law courts | The high court of the realm | Westminster Hall; the Parlement in the Palais de la Cité |
| Mint | Makes the royal coinage | The Tower of London mint; the Paris mint |
| University (later) | Trains lawyers, clerks and priests for government and church | Many capitals founded one: Prague 1348, Kraków 1364, Vienna 1365 |
| Royal chapel or relic church | Holds relics that give the king holy status | Sainte-Chapelle, Paris (1248), built for the Crown of Thorns |

### Ceremonial cities: coronation and burial

The city where a ruler is **crowned** or **buried** is often not the city where he governs. The ceremonial city is usually older, where the dynasty or the church first became strong, and keeps its role for centuries.

| Realm | Coronation | Burial | Government |
|---|---|---|---|
| France | Reims (nearly all kings; about 130 km / 80 mi from Paris) | Saint-Denis abbey, just north of Paris | Paris |
| England | Westminster Abbey (since 1066) | Westminster Abbey (rebuilt by Henry III from 1245) | Westminster / London (Winchester was an older royal and treasury centre) |
| Holy Roman Empire | Aachen (30 kings crowned 936–1531), then Rome for the imperial crown | Speyer cathedral (Salian emperors) and others | No capital. The Golden Bull (1356) fixed elections at Frankfurt, coronation at Aachen and the first diet (assembly) of each reign at Nuremberg. |
| Hungary | Székesfehérvár (38 kings crowned, 1038–1527) | Székesfehérvár | Esztergom, Visegrád, Buda (about 60 km / 37 mi from Székesfehérvár) |
| Poland | Gniezno (early), then Kraków's Wawel (from 1320) | Wawel Cathedral | Kraków |
| Scotland | Scone | Dunfermline abbey | Edinburgh, from the 15th century |
| Aragon | Zaragoza | Poblet monastery | Barcelona, Zaragoza, Valencia (itinerant) |

> **Rule of thumb:** Governments move; sacred sites stay. A small town with a coronation church or royal tombs can be more important on your map than a large trading city.

Legitimacy can also be borrowed. After the Mongols destroyed Baghdad in 1258, the Mamluk sultans of Egypt brought a member of the Abbasid family to Cairo (1261) and kept him there as a "shadow caliph" to bless their rule.

### Around the capital: the royal landscape

Within about one day's ride of a fixed capital you usually find:

| Feature | Distance from capital | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| A ring of royal residences: smaller palaces, manors and castles | 10–60 km (6–37 mi) | London: Eltham and Sheen (~13 km), Windsor (~35 km). Paris: Vincennes (~7 km), Saint-Germain-en-Laye (~20 km), Fontainebleau (~55 km). Buda: Visegrád (~35 km). Prague: Karlštejn (~30 km). |
| Royal hunting forests and lodges | 10–100 km (6–60 mi) | England: the New Forest (c. 1079); Windsor Forest; Woodstock and Clarendon hunting palaces. Kraków: Niepołomice hunting castle. |
| The ruler's favourite abbeys and burial church | 0–20 km (0–12 mi) | Westminster, Saint-Denis |
| Estates of courtiers and officials | Within 1–2 days' ride | Nobles' town houses in the capital and country manors in the home counties |

**Royal forests** were a legal zone, not just woodland. Inside them, deer and trees were protected by special forest law, and villages, fields and heath could all lie inside a "forest". At their peak, in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, royal forests covered about one-third of southern England. Kings used them for hunting and to raise money from fines and licences.

> **Map tip:** Around your capital, draw 3–6 small royal residences within about 60 km (37 mi), at least one big royal forest with a hunting lodge, a great abbey or burial church close by, and, if you want, a separate coronation town within 50–150 km (30–90 mi). Label royal forests in the same colour as the crown's land. These details make a capital feel real.

## Political units and their sizes

> **Rule of thumb:** Kingdom > duchy (or principality) > county > barony or lordship > manor. Each level is usually several times to a few dozen times smaller in area than the one above. But medieval units overlapped, were scattered and changed constantly. Do not expect a neat pyramid.

### The units, top to bottom

| Unit | Typical area | Typical population (c. 1300) | Seat | Notes and examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Empire | 0.8–1.7 million km² (300,000–650,000 sq mi) | 5–15 million | One great capital, or none (HRE) | HRE 0.8–1.1m km²; Byzantium in 1025, 1.675m km² |
| Kingdom | 10,000–450,000 km² (4,000–175,000 sq mi) | 0.1–17 million | Capital or itinerant court | Navarre ~12,000 km²; Scotland ~79,000; Portugal ~89,000; England 130,000; Hungary 280,000–325,000; France was the giant |
| Duchy or principality (great fief) | 10,000–50,000 km² (4,000–19,000 sq mi) | 0.5–2 million (estimates) | A ducal city with castle, cathedral and court | Normandy ~30,000 km²; Brittany ~34,000 km²; Flanders (a county, but as powerful as a duchy) ~10,000 km² |
| County (shire) | 1,000–10,000 km² (400–4,000 sq mi); England averaged ~3,300 km² | 20,000–300,000; England averaged ~120,000 | County town with castle, sheriff and county court | 39 English shires: Rutland 394 km² (152 sq mi) to Yorkshire ~15,700 km² (6,067 sq mi). Hungary had ~70 counties in the 15th century, averaging ~4,000–4,500 km². |
| Hundred or wapentake (English sub-county district) | Average ~130–150 km² (50–60 sq mi); range 4.6–982 km² | 2,000–10,000 | A meeting place, often open-air: a mound, a ford, a crossroads, a stone | 812 hundreds recorded in Domesday Book (1086); about 10–12 parishes each |
| Barony, lordship, honour | Very variable: a few manors to 100+ | — | The *caput* (head manor), usually with a castle | A great baron's manors were often scattered across several counties |
| Knight's fee | 1–2 manors; no fixed size | — | A manor house | England had only about 5,000–6,000 knights' fees (1166 survey) |
| Manor | 2–10 km² (one village, part of one, or several) | 50–500 | Manor house, often moated | Elton (Huntingdonshire) had 1,872 acres (~760 ha) of arable in 1279 |
| Parish | ~14 km² in the lowlands; 50–100+ km² in uplands | 75–500 | Parish church | ~9,500 parishes in England c. 1300 |

### Units do not nest neatly

- **Manors and villages often differed.** In a 1279 survey of 134 Leicestershire villages, only 37 had a single lord. 63 villages (47%) were split among three or more lords.
- **Baronies were scattered.** In 1086 England had about 1,100 tenants-in-chief (people holding land directly from the king), but a dozen or so great barons held about a quarter of the country. Their manors lay in many counties.
- **Counties had detached pieces.** Many English counties had small "islands" of land inside neighbouring counties; many of these were removed only by an Act of 1844.
- **Church lands cut across everything.** Bishops and abbots held about a quarter of England in 1086, and a bishop's lands did not match his diocese.

### Who sits where

| Lord | Seat | What the seat looks like |
|---|---|---|
| King | Capital or main palaces | See above |
| Duke or great count | Main city of the duchy | Big castle or palace, cathedral, mint, the duke's own abbey and burial church. Example: Rouen for Normandy, Dijon for Burgundy, Ghent and Bruges for Flanders. |
| Count or sheriff (royal officer) | County town | Royal castle with the sheriff's office and gaol, the county court, a market. In England many shires are named after their county town: Warwickshire, Oxfordshire, Bedfordshire. In Hungary each county head (*ispán*) sat in a royal castle. |
| Baron | Caput of the barony | A castle (see [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md)), often with a small borough and a priory founded by the family |
| Knight or manor lord | Manor | Manor house with hall, barns and often a moat, next to the parish church |

### How many units in one kingdom?

| Realm | Count | Source |
|---|---|---|
| England c. 1300 | 1 kingdom; 39 shires (plus special zones such as the Palatinate of Durham); ~800+ hundreds; ~9,500 parishes; 5,000–6,000 knights' fees | Domesday; 1166 survey; baseline |
| France c. 1200 | Royal domain plus 6 great lay fiefs: the duchies of Burgundy, Normandy and Aquitaine and the counties of Flanders, Champagne and Toulouse (the six lay "peers of France"), plus many lesser counties | Peerage of France |
| Hungary | ~37 counties (1038), ~45–50 (c. 1074), ~70 (15th century) | Wikipedia *Administrative divisions of the Kingdom of Hungary* |
| Carolingian Empire c. 800 | A few hundred counts (estimates vary; ~300 is often quoted) | — |
| Holy Roman Empire 1521 | ~400 imperial estates: 7 archbishops (3 of them electors) and 46 bishops; 83 lesser prelates (abbots and abbesses); 180 secular lords; 87 free and imperial cities | Worms register of 1521 |

> **Map tip:** For a kingdom the size of England (130,000 km², 50,000 sq mi), draw 30–40 counties, each about 50–70 km (30–45 mi) across, each with a county town near its middle. Group them into 4–8 bigger regions held by great lords or old duchies. Draw duchy borders with a thicker line than county borders. Leave 1–3 special zones (a march, a palatinate, a church territory) with their own colour or hatching.

> **Fantasy twist:** Other peoples may divide land differently. Dwarf holds might be counted by mountain or mine, not area; elves by forest; a sea people by islands or harbours. Keep the same rule: one seat per unit, one unit per day or two of travel.

## Borders and frontiers

> **Rule of thumb:** In settled farmland, borders were known lines, walked and argued over field by field. In empty land (mountains, forest, marsh, desert, steppe), borders were wide zones. Draw lines where people live and bands where they do not.

### What borders follow

| Feature | How often used | Why | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old boundaries | Very often | Borders are "sticky". Once fixed, they last for centuries. | Many French dioceses follow Roman city territories. Many English shire borders lasted from the 10th or 11th century until 1974. |
| Watersheds and mountain crests | Often, but not always | Mountains are empty, so a crest line divides little. | Much of the Pyrenees. But the Val d'Aran, on the French side of the crest, belonged to Aragon, and Lower Navarre lay north of the mountains. |
| Pass states (an exception) | In the Alps | Whoever holds a pass wants both sides of it. | Savoy, Tyrol and the Swiss canton of Uri straddled the main Alpine passes instead of stopping at the crest. |
| Rivers | Sometimes | Easy to name in a treaty and easy to see. But a river valley is one economic unit, so peoples on both banks are often the same. | Tweed (England–Scotland, Treaty of York 1237); Scheldt (France–Empire); the Danube (old Roman frontier) |
| Forests, marshes, heaths | Often in early times | Thin population; hard to cross. | The "Wildnis" (wilderness) left between the Teutonic Order's Prussia and Lithuania |
| Empty wasteland | Frontier wars | Raiding emptied the land between enemies. | The Castile–Granada frontier (1246–1492); the steppe "Wild Fields" north of the Black Sea |
| Man-made dykes and walls | Rare but striking | Mark a claim or block raids | Offa's Dyke (built 757–796) on the Welsh border; the Danevirke in Denmark; the Scots' Dike (1552) |

### Frontier zones vs border lines

- **Line borders** existed in settled land. Parish and manor bounds were marked with stones, crosses, ditches and old trees, and villagers walked them every year ("beating the bounds"). Treaties named rivers and landmarks. The Anglo-Scottish border fixed by the Treaty of York (1237) changed very little afterwards.
- **Zone frontiers** were the norm on the edges of Christian Europe, in mountains and in war zones. Here power faded out over 20–100+ km. People paid tribute to both sides, or to neither.

### Marches: militarised border provinces

A **march** (German *Mark*, Italian *marca*) was a border province given to a strong lord to defend. The **margrave** (march count) or marcher lord had more military power than an ordinary count.

| March | Dates | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish March (Marca Hispanica) | From c. 795; Barcelona taken 801 | A ring of counties (Barcelona, Girona, Urgell, Cerdanya and others) facing al-Andalus. The count of Barcelona was effectively independent by 985; the march became Catalonia. |
| Welsh March | From the 1070s to 1536 | Dozens of small Norman lordships between England and Wales. Marcher lords ruled "like kings": own courts, own castles, private war, their own markets and boroughs. Britain's densest cluster of motte-and-bailey castles (castles on a man-made earth mound with a fenced yard). Abolished by the Laws in Wales Acts (1535–42). |
| Anglo-Scottish Marches | 13th century to 1603 | Three marches (East, Middle, West) on each side, each under a royal Warden. A special border law (from 1249). Raiding clans ("reivers"). |
| Brandenburg | From 1134 | Northern March given to Albert the Bear; it grew into a major principality. |
| Austria | From the 10th century | The eastern march of Bavaria; Vienna became its seat in the 1150s. |

Other frontier words that survive as place names: *Extremadura* (often explained as "the far edge of the Duero" lands), *Marche* (France and Italy), *Mark*, and *Krajina* (Slavic for "borderland").

> **Rule of thumb:** A march is bigger, emptier and more fortified than an inland county of the same importance. Expect noticeably more castles per area (the Welsh March has Britain's densest cluster of earth-and-timber castles) and fewer, smaller towns.

### Buffer states, enclaves, exclaves and contested land

| Thing | What it is | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Buffer state | A small realm between two big ones. Each big neighbour prefers it independent rather than in the other's hands. | Navarre between France, Castile and Aragon (its southern part was taken by Castile in 1512); Savoy on the Alpine passes; Wallachia and Moldavia between Hungary, Poland and the Ottomans; Andorra (co-ruled since 1278 by the bishop of Urgell and the count of Foix; 468 km²) |
| Enclave / exclave | A piece of one realm surrounded by another (an enclave from the outside view, an exclave from the owner's view) | Baarle (Low Countries): a feudal split from c. 1198 left dozens of tiny pockets that still exist. Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin: papal land inside France from 1274 (Venaissin) and 1348 (Avignon) until 1791. |
| Overseas bridgehead | A defended exclave across a sea | The Pale of Calais, English from 1347 to 1558, about 18 mi (29 km) of coast and 8–10 mi (13–16 km) inland, supplied by sea |
| Palatinate | A border county where a great lord holds royal powers | The prince-bishops of Durham guarded England against Scotland |
| Contested land | Claimed by both sides, ruled by neither | The Debatable Lands on the Anglo-Scottish border: about 16 × 6.4 km (10 × 4 mi), lawless for over three centuries, split in 1552 by the Scots' Dike. Berwick-upon-Tweed changed hands many times before becoming English for good in 1482. |

### Border markers and crossings

- **Markers:** boundary stones, crosses, ditches, banks, marked trees and posts. Treaty commissioners rode the line and set stones at each end (as with the Scots' Dike in 1552).
- **Crossings:** bridges, fords and passes on main roads, with a toll station, often a border castle on each side, and an inn or hospice. Tolls and customs could be collected at sea straits too: Denmark's Sound Toll at Helsingør (from 1429).
- **Meeting places:** on the Anglo-Scottish border, wardens met on fixed "truce days" at traditional spots on the line to settle complaints.

> **Map tip:** Draw lines where settled farmland meets, and a hatched band (a "march") across mountains, forests and war zones. Put paired castles facing each other where a main road crosses the border. Add one or two odd features: an enclave, a tiny buffer state, a disputed strip. These make borders look historical rather than planned. Castle spacing along frontiers is covered in [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Monsters behave like an extra enemy. A monster-haunted forest or mountain becomes a frontier zone even between friendly realms, with marcher lords, watchtowers and wide empty bands. A magical barrier (a ward line, a cursed river) can create a sharp line border even in wilderness.

## Kinds of states and how they look on a map

> **Rule of thumb:** Do not colour all of Europe-like land in big, solid blocks. Medieval maps were patchworks: kingdoms, duchies, free cities, church lands, leagues and empires overlapped and interlocked.

| Kind of state | How it works | How it looks on a map | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feudal kingdom | A king over dukes, counts and barons who hold land in return for loyalty and service. The royal domain is only one part. | One outline, but inside it a patchwork of great fiefs. The royal domain is the core around the capital. Some fiefs are nearly independent. | France c. 1200: royal domain in the Île-de-France, surrounded by Normandy, Flanders, Champagne, Burgundy, Aquitaine and Toulouse |
| City-state and its *contado* | A self-governing city rules the countryside around it (the *contado*, often the same area as its diocese) and later conquers neighbouring cities. | A city with a territory of roughly 20–50 km (12–30 mi) radius, many such states side by side, with borders that move often. | Florence (bought Arezzo in 1384, conquered Pisa in 1406); in 1427 the Florentine state had about 260,000 people, of whom the city held about 38,000. Also Milan, Siena, Genoa. |
| League of towns | Towns cooperate for trade and defence but keep their own small territories. | A network, not an area: dots linked by sea routes, plus trading posts in foreign cities. | Hanseatic League: 70 or more core towns at its height (1350s–1370s), up to about 200 loosely linked; main trading posts (*Kontore*) in Novgorod, Bruges, London (the Steelyard) and Bergen; meetings in Lübeck |
| Confederation | Rural valleys and towns ally for defence and rule conquered land together. | A cluster of small units; some areas shown as joint subject lands. | Swiss Confederacy: 8 cantons by 1353, 13 by 1513. Aargau (1415) and Thurgau (1460) were ruled jointly as subject lands. |
| Composite empire | Many realms and hundreds of lords under one elected ruler with weak central power. | A big outer border with hundreds of small internal units. | Holy Roman Empire: ~400 estates in 1521, including 87 imperial cities and ~50 prince-bishops and archbishops |
| Church state | A bishop, abbot or the Pope rules land as a prince. The territory is not the same as the diocese. | Separate colour; often scattered pieces. | Papal States in central Italy; prince-bishoprics of Cologne, Mainz, Trier, Liège, Münster, Würzburg, Salzburg; Durham in England |
| Military-order state | A crusading order of knights rules conquered land. | A frontier territory with many castles and commanderies (houses of the order). | Teutonic Order in Prussia, seat at Marienburg (Malbork) from 1309 |
| Maritime empire | A trading city holds ports and islands along a sea route, later also land behind it. | A home city plus a chain of coastal dots and islands, not a solid area. | Venice: Crete (c. 1210–1669), Negroponte (to 1470), Modon and Coron (1207–1500), Corfu (from 1386), Cyprus (1489–1570), Dalmatian ports; mainland expansion from 1337–39 (Treviso) to Bergamo and Brescia |
| Nomadic empire | Herders on seasonal pastures; settled peoples nearby pay tribute. | A huge, vague area: core pastures, a few capital cities, and a ring of tributary states around them. ### Drawing the unusual kinds

- **City-states:** many small territories, each 1–3 days across, centred on rival walled cities about 50–90 km (30–55 mi) apart.
- **Leagues:** do not colour an area. Put a shared symbol on each member town (members can sit inside other rulers' kingdoms) and dashed sea routes between them.
- **Nomadic empires:** show summer and winter pastures, one or two built capitals on rivers or trade routes, and the tributary ring in a lighter tint.
- **Maritime empires:** colour only the islands and harbour towns along the sea route; the land behind them keeps the local ruler's colour.

> **Map tip:** For a continent map, use at least three kinds of state: one or two big feudal kingdoms, one fragmented zone (city-states or a composite empire) and one unusual kind (a league, a church state, a maritime empire or nomads). This mix is what makes a medieval map look medieval.

> **Fantasy twist:** Different peoples suggest different state types. A dwarven realm may be a league of mountain holds linked by tunnels (a network, like the Hanse). An elven forest realm may look like a nomadic or church state with vague borders. A wizards' city may rule a contado like Florence.

## How big can a realm be

> **Rule of thumb:** A ruler can govern directly up to about 10–15 days' messenger travel from the capital: roughly 500–700 km (300–450 mi). Beyond that, the ruler needs viceroys, great vassals, sea routes or a relay post, or the realm splits.

### Why distance limits power

- Orders, tax money and news travel at the speed of a horse (see the travel table in [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)).
- A rebellion far away can grow for weeks before the king even hears of it.
- Feudal armies often owed only about 40 days of service, so a host raised in the core could not campaign for long on a far frontier.

### Distance from the capital and what it means

Speeds from the shared baseline: royal messenger 50–90 km (30–56 mi) a day; army with baggage 13–20 km (8–12 mi) a day.

| Distance from capital | Royal messenger | Army | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 km (60 mi) | 1–2 days | 5–8 days | Core: royal domain, residences, hunting forests. Direct control. |
| 300 km (190 mi) | 4–6 days | 15–23 days | Normal kingdom: counties ruled by royal officers (sheriffs, bailiffs, *ispáns*). |
| 600 km (370 mi) | 7–12 days | 30–45 days | Edge of a large kingdom: needs great lords, marcher lords or a royal governor. |
| 1,000+ km (600+ mi) | 11–20 days | 50–75+ days | Only possible for empires with sea routes, relay posts or semi-independent governors. |

A real measure: news reaching Venice around 1500 (after Sardella and Braudel) took about 1 week from Milan, Genoa or Florence; about 2 weeks from Paris, Lyon, Vienna, Buda or Naples; about 3 weeks from Antwerp, Cologne or Marseille; 3–4 weeks from London; 4–5 weeks from Castile or Constantinople; about 6 weeks from Lisbon. That was a city with one of the best news networks in Europe.

Around 1500, Europe held roughly 500 more or less independent political units (Tilly 1975), from tiny lordships to great kingdoms. Most were small.

Realms that grew too big either split (the Carolingian Empire at Verdun in 843; the Mongol Empire into separate khanates by the 1260s), were run through regents and governors (Charles V), or held their distant parts by sea (Venice, Byzantium).

> **Map tip:** Measure from your capital along the roads, not in a straight line. Mark the 5-day and 12-day travel lines in pencil. Put direct royal counties inside the first line, great vassals and marches between the lines, and tributary or semi-independent lands beyond the second. If your kingdom is much bigger than that, add a second-tier capital (a viceroy's seat) or make the outer parts nearly independent.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Organised postal relays (Taxis, from about 1500, up to ~150 km or 95 mi a day) and faster shipping roughly double the range a capital can control. Empires like Spain and the Ottomans govern huge areas through viceroys and provincial governors. Borders are increasingly surveyed, mapped and marked by boundary commissions. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Every faster messenger extends the realm. A griffon post at 300 km (190 mi) a day lets a capital govern roughly 3–5 times farther; instant magical messages remove the communication limit but not the army limit. Armies still march at 15–20 km a day unless magic moves them too.

## Drawing realms, borders and capitals

### Step by step

1. **Choose the realm type** and **find the core:** the best farmland on a navigable river, with a defensible hill or island for the capital.
2. **Add the royal landscape:** 3–6 residences, a royal forest, the burial church, perhaps a separate coronation town.
3. **Draw the outer border:** lines in settled land, march bands across mountains, forests, marshes and war zones.
4. **Divide** into 4–8 duchies or great fiefs, then counties 50–70 km (30–45 mi) across, each with a seat near its middle. Add a few oddities (church land, an enclave, a free city, a disputed strip).
5. **Check distance:** anything more than 12–15 days' messenger travel from the capital needs a viceroy or becomes semi-independent.

### Symbols and line styles

| Feature | Suggested symbol |
|---|---|
| Capital | Star, or a crowned dot, with the name in bold capitals |
| Coronation or burial city | Small crown or cross beside the town symbol |
| Duchy or regional seat | Smaller star or castle-in-circle |
| County town | Double circle |
| Royal residence or hunting lodge | Small crown or tower symbol |
| Realm border | Thick solid line, or a coloured band along the inside edge |
| Duchy border | Medium dashed line |
| County border | Thin dotted line (only on regional maps) |
| Disputed border | Two parallel lines, or alternating colours |
| March or frontier zone | Hatching, or a fading colour band 10–50 km wide |
| Enclave | Same colour as its owner, with a thin outline |

### Colour and labels

- Use pale fills for realms, so roads and towns stay readable. Give vassal duchies a lighter shade of their king's colour.
- Give church lands, free cities and leagues their own colours or symbols. Do not merge them into the surrounding kingdom.
- Write realm names in widely spaced capitals across the whole territory. Write duchies in smaller italic capitals and counties in lower case.
- Label marches and frontier zones by name ("the West March", "the Debatable Land"). Named empty spaces make a map feel lived-in.

> **Map tip:** On a continent map, show only realms and great fiefs. On a kingdom map, add counties and the capital's royal landscape. On a local map, add hundreds, manors, parish bounds and boundary stones. Do not show every level at every scale.

## Quick summary

- A capital needs a rich core region, navigable water, a defensible site, loyal land around it and a source of legitimacy. Centrality matters less.
- Expanding realms move their capital toward the frontier ("forward capital"); settled realms keep the old core.
- Before c. 1200 most western courts travelled between royal palaces about 30 km (18 mi) apart. Fixed capitals came with archives, law courts and money taxes: Paris and Westminster in the 12th–14th centuries, Madrid only in 1561. The Holy Roman Empire never had one.
- Coronation and burial cities are often different from the governing city, and keep their role for centuries.
- Around a capital, draw 3–6 royal residences and hunting forests within about 60 km (37 mi); royal forests covered up to one-third of southern England c. 1200.
- Typical sizes: kingdom 10,000–450,000 km²; duchy 10,000–50,000 km²; county 1,000–10,000 km² (England averaged ~3,300 km² and ~120,000 people); hundred ~130–150 km²; manor 2–10 km².
- Borders are lines in settled land and wide zones (marches) in empty land. They follow old boundaries, crests, rivers, forests and wasteland.
- Marches, buffer states, enclaves and contested strips were normal. Add some.
- Medieval political maps are patchworks: kingdoms, city-states, leagues, church states, maritime chains and nomadic empires side by side. Europe c. 1500 had about 500 political units.
- Direct rule reaches about 10–15 days' messenger travel (500–700 km, 300–450 mi). Beyond that, rulers need viceroys, vassals, sea routes or relays.
