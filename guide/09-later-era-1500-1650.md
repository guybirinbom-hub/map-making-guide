# What Changes After 1500

Your map may be set a little later than the classic Middle Ages: a world with cannon, printed books and ships that cross oceans. This chapter explains what changed on the ground between about 1500 and 1650, and how to show it on a map. Most rules in the rest of this guide still hold, because villages, market towns and travel speeds barely change. What changes is the top of the system: bigger capitals, new kinds of fortresses, ocean ports, post roads, canals, drained land and a new religious map.

## What changes and what stays the same

> **Rule of thumb:** After 1500 the bottom of the map stays medieval and the top changes. Villages, fields and market towns look much the same. Capitals, ports, fortresses and long-distance links are new or much bigger.

**Why:** Most people (80–90% almost everywhere) still farm with the same tools and animals. On land, goods still move by ox, horse and mule. So everything that depends on walking distance stays the same: how far apart villages are, how far apart market towns are, and how much land feeds a town. What changes is what rich states and merchants can now pay for: cannon and new walls, ocean ships, standing armies, big courts, printing presses and engineering projects.

### Timeline of the changes

| Date | What happens | What it means on the map |
|---|---|---|
| 1490s | Taxis relay post (from 1490); French siege guns in Italy (1494); Columbus (1492) and da Gama (1498) | Post stations along main roads; old walls suddenly weak; new ocean routes |
| 1500–1540s | Bastioned fortifications develop in Italy and spread north in the 1530s–1540s | Star-shaped walls around key towns |
| 1500–1600 | Population recovers and grows; Atlantic ports boom | Bigger capitals and ports; Seville, Lisbon and Antwerp, then Amsterdam and London |
| 1517–1560s | Protestant Reformation; English monasteries closed 1536–41 | Abbey ruins, abbeys turned into houses, religious borders |
| 1560s–1600 | Fixed capitals (Madrid 1561); printed atlases and county maps; Spanish rules for planting colonial towns (1573) | Palace complexes; more accurate maps; grid towns around a plaza in the colonies |
| 1600–1650 | Chartered trading companies (English 1600, Dutch 1602); lakes drained (Beemster 1612); Japan's one-castle order (1615); French edict against interior castles (1626); Thirty Years' War (1618–48) | Trading posts overseas; new polder landscapes; far fewer working castles in strong states; devastated regions |

### What does not change

| Feature | Value (same as the medieval baseline) |
|---|---|
| Village spacing, lowland | 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) |
| Market towns in practice | 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart; England had ~650 working markets in the late 1500s |
| Walking traveller | 25–35 km (15–22 mi) a day |
| Horse cart | 30–40 km (20–25 mi) a day |
| Water vs land costs | Bulk goods still go by water; land haulage stays several times dearer |
| Share living in towns of 10,000+ | Still under 10% in most countries; the big exceptions are the Netherlands (24% in 1600, 32% in 1650) and Belgium and Italy (15–19%) |

> **Map tip:** Do not redraw the whole countryside for a later setting. Keep your medieval village and market-town pattern. Then change the top layer: enlarge the capital and the main ports, add new forts, post stations, canals and drained land, and turn some castles and abbeys into ruins or country houses.

## Population and bigger cities

> **Rule of thumb:** Europe regains its 1300 peak during the 1500s and keeps growing until about 1600–1620. Almost all the extra growth in towns goes to capitals and ocean ports.

### How many people

- **Europe:** Jan de Vries's estimates for Europe without Russia and the Ottoman lands are roughly 62 million (1500), 70 million (1550), 78 million (1600) and 75 million (1650). Counting Russia and the Ottoman Balkans, the guide's baseline is about 100 million by 1600. Other historians give somewhat different totals, but the shape is the same: growth in the 1500s, a halt in the 1600s.
- **England:** about 2.35 million (1522), 4.1 million (1600) and 5.3 million (1650). England only passed its 1348 peak around 1600–1630.
- **The 1600s are harder.** Growth stops or reverses after about 1620. The Thirty Years' War (1618–48) devastated parts of Germany. Italian plagues in 1629–31 and 1656–57 each killed 20% or more of the people in the areas they hit. Milan lost about 60,000 of its 130,000 people in the plague of 1629–31. Naples lost 150,000 or more people, about half the city, in the 1656 plague.

### The big cities

Figures are estimates in thousands. Sources disagree, so treat each range as "somewhere in here".

| City | c. 1500 | c. 1600 | c. 1650 | Why it grew or shrank |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Istanbul | 100–280 (Ottoman count of 1478: 80–100) | 400–700 | 400–700 | Ottoman capital, fed by sea from Egypt and the Black Sea |
| Paris | 95–250 (often ~200) | 220–300 | 400–500 | Capital of the largest western kingdom |
| Naples | 100–150 | 250–280 | ~300 before the 1656 plague, then about half | Spanish viceroy's capital; nobles and migrants from the whole kingdom |
| London | ~50 | ~200 | 375–400 | Capital and chief port; took over much of Antwerp's trade |
| Amsterdam | 10–15 | 50–60 (105 in the 1622 count) | 150–175 | Took over Antwerp's trade after 1585; Baltic grain and Asian spices |
| Seville | 45–70 | 110–150 (peak, late 1500s) | ~130 before the 1649 plague, which killed nearly half | Legal monopoly port for the Americas from 1503 |
| Lisbon | 55–65 | 110–150 | 130–170 | Spice route to India; Brazil sugar |
| Antwerp | ~40 (1496) | ~100 in 1566, then ~42 in 1589 | ~50–60 | Boom as Europe's market, then siege (1585) and blockade of the river Scheldt |
| Madrid | under 20 (not yet capital) | 75–95 | 100–130 | Made the fixed capital in 1561 (about 18 then, about 80 by 1598) |
| Venice, Milan | 100–150 each | 110–150 each | Venice ~100–120; Milan smaller | Large but no longer growing; the 1629–31 plague killed about a third of Venice (46 of 140) and almost half of Milan (60 of 130) |
| Rome | 55–60 | ~100 | 100–120 | Papal capital rebuilt as a showpiece for pilgrims |

### Shifting rank: the Atlantic shift

The order of cities changes faster than in the Middle Ages. Three patterns are worth copying:

1. **Rise of ocean ports.** Seville, Lisbon, Antwerp, Amsterdam and London grow two to ten times. Seville grew from perhaps 45,000–70,000 people around 1500 to over 100,000 by the late 1500s, which made it the largest city in Spain.
2. **Rise and fall.** Antwerp grew from about 40,000 (1496) to 100,000 (1566). After the Spanish took it in 1585, the Dutch closed the river Scheldt to sea trade, and by 1589 only about 42,000 people were left. Its merchants and craftsmen moved to Amsterdam and London.
3. **Capital pulls life out of an old city.** When Madrid became the capital, the old regional centre Toledo, the largest city in the region until about 1600, declined (Ringrose 1973).

The Mediterranean does not collapse. Venice, Genoa, Naples and Istanbul stay huge. But the fastest growth is now on the Atlantic and North Sea coasts.

### Why capitals grow so much

A capital is now a **consumer city**. It holds the court, the law courts, the treasury, the nobles' town houses, foreign ambassadors, and thousands of servants and luxury craftsmen. By 1650 about one English person in thirteen lived in London (about 400,000 out of 5.3 million). In some realms the capital becomes 5–10 times bigger than the second city. England is the extreme case: London was more than ten times the size of the next English town. In other lands (Spain, Italy) several big cities stay close in size. See [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md) for the "primate city" pattern.

> **Map tip:** For a 1600-style map, draw the capital 2–4 times bigger than its medieval size, and the fastest-growing port or capital up to 5–10 times bigger. Show suburbs spilling far outside the old walls. Add one "fallen" city: a port with a silted or blockaded river, a smaller population and a grand but half-empty centre.

## Gunpowder and the new fortifications

### Why the old walls failed

Medieval walls were **high and thin**, to stop ladders. Cannon smashes high stone walls quickly. When Charles VIII of France invaded Italy in 1494, he brought about 18,000 men (other accounts say up to 25,000). He also brought a siege train of at least 40 guns on wheeled carriages (Parker). Castles that had once held out for months now fell in days. Round medieval towers also leave **dead ground**: spots at the foot of the wall that the defenders cannot shoot at.

> **Rule of thumb:** After 1500, a wall that is tall is weak. Good walls are now low, very thick, backed with earth, sunk behind a wide ditch, and shaped so that every part can be shot at from another part.

### The bastion and the star fort

A **bastion** is an arrow-shaped platform that sticks out from the wall. Guns on each bastion fire along the face of the next bastion, so there is no dead ground. Seen from above, a ring of bastions looks like a star. This system is called the **trace italienne** ("Italian outline") because it was first built in Italy. Extra **outworks** stand in or beyond the ditch, for example the **ravelin** (a triangular island fort in front of the wall).

| Date | Place | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1501–c. 1503 | Nettuno (papal fort, usually credited to Antonio da Sangallo the Elder) | Early angled bastions; very thick, low walls without battlements |
| 1508–1537 | Civitavecchia (Forte Michelangelo) | Papal port fort; often called the first key example of the new style |
| 1509 | Padua | The engineer Fra Giocondo replaced the old wall with a broad ditch swept by low gun positions; a huge imperial siege army failed |
| 1534–1537 | Fortezza da Basso, Florence | Large pentagonal (five-sided) bastioned fortress by Antonio da Sangallo the Younger; often cited as an early model |
| 1530s–1540s | Spain, France, the Low Countries, England | The style spreads out of Italy |
| 1564–c. 1570 | Turin citadel | Pentagon with five bastions (sources give finishing dates from 1566 to 1570) |
| 1566 | Valletta, Malta | New fortress capital, built after the Great Siege of 1565 |
| 1593 | Palmanova (Venice) | Planned nine-pointed star town; first ring of walls (finished 1623) about 7 km (4.3 mi) round |
| 1593 | Bourtange (Dutch) | Small five-pointed fort guarding a road through a bog |

### Sizes and garrisons

Sizes and garrisons below are rough estimates, except the named examples.

| Kind | Typical size | Garrison | Where to put it | Map symbol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Watchtower | 10–15 m tall | a handful of men | Every few km along raided coasts, each in sight of the next | Small triangle |
| Coastal artillery fort | 30–100 m across | 10–30 in peace (Walmer 18, Camber 29 in 1540) | Harbour mouths, anchorages, river mouths | Small round outline with rounded lobes (a **rosette**, like the plan of Deal Castle, 1539–40), never pointed |
| Small fort (*sconce*, Dutch *schans*) | 100–250 m across, 4–5 bastions | 50–300 | River crossings, dikes, roads through marshes | Small star outline (the star-fort symbol), never filled |
| Citadel | 400–700 m across, 4–6 bastions | several hundred to 2,000+ | At the edge of a big or rebellious city | Pentagon outline joined to the town (the bastioned-citadel symbol) |
| Fortress town | 1–2.5 km (0.6–1.5 mi) across, 6–12+ bastions | 1,000–5,000 in war | Frontiers, key river towns, big ports | Star-edged town outline, never filled |

Bastions are usually spaced so that musket fire from one reaches the next, roughly 200–350 m apart (an approximation). This is why star forts look so regular.

**Cost.** Bastions were enormously expensive. Siena bankrupted itself in 1544 to pay for its new defences. Amsterdam's 22 bastions cost 11 million florins. Henry VIII spent £376,000 on his coastal forts (1539–47); one small blockhouse cost about £500, and the three forts of the Downs in Kent cost £27,092 together.

> **Rule of thumb:** Only rich states can afford bastions everywhere. Put full star forts at frontiers, the capital, the main ports and a few river crossings. Ordinary towns keep their medieval walls, perhaps with a few earth bastions added on the side facing the enemy.

### Citadels: forts that watch their own city

A **citadel** is a star fort built at the edge of a city, half inside and half outside the walls. It defends the city, but it also controls the citizens. Its guns point both outward and into the streets.

- **Fortezza da Basso**, Florence (1534–37), built for the new Medici duke.
- **Turin** (from 1564), designed by Francesco Paciotto after Turin became the Savoy capital; meant to defend the city and also to overawe its people.
- **Antwerp** (1567–72), also by Paciotto, built on the Duke of Alba's orders during the Dutch Revolt. After the Spanish troops sacked the city in 1576, the citizens tore down part of it. It was rebuilt after 1585.

> **Map tip:** Draw a citadel as a pentagon outline (the guide's bastioned-citadel symbol) joined to one corner of the town wall, with an open space (an *esplanade*) between the fort and the houses. If a ruler has just conquered a rebellious city, give it a citadel.

### Coastal defence

- **England:** Henry VIII's "Device Forts" (1539–47). These were dozens of forts, blockhouses and earth gun platforms at the anchorages and river mouths most open to invasion: the Downs in Kent, the Thames, the Solent and Falmouth. Twenty-four sites were already finished and manned by the end of 1540. Most were small, round, squat gun forts with tiny garrisons. Seen from above, the bigger ones look like a flower: Deal Castle (1539–40), about 70 m (230 ft) across, is a round central tower inside two rings of six rounded bastions; Walmer has four rounded bastions.
- **Kingdom of Naples:** viceroys' orders of 1532 and especially 1563 pushed the building of watchtowers along the whole coast against corsairs (sea raiders, here mainly from North Africa). An inventory of 1748 listed 379 coastal towers. They passed warnings by smoke by day and fire by night.
- **Genoa** built about 85–90 towers around Corsica between 1530 and 1620 (90 were planned in 1531; a list of 1617 counts 86). See [Watchtowers, beacons and coastal warning](04-military-sites.md#watchtowers-beacons-and-coastal-warning).

> **Map tip:** On a raided coast, draw a chain of watchtowers (small triangles) 3–12 km (2–7 mi) apart, every one in sight of the next. Keep the villages a little inland and uphill. Put a real fort only at the harbour mouth, drawn as the coastal artillery fort symbol: a small rosette outline (rounded lobes, like Deal Castle's plan), never pointed. A harbour fort with angled bastions takes the star outline instead. The spacing is an estimate. About 379 towers on roughly 2,000–2,400 km (1,250–1,500 mi) of Neapolitan mainland coast is one per 5–6 km (3–4 mi). That count comes from 1748, so it may include some towers built after 1600. About 85–90 towers on Corsica's roughly 1,000 km (620 mi) of coast is one per 11–12 km (about 7 mi).

### Bigger armies, longer sieges

- **Army size:** Charles VIII's 18,000 men looked huge in 1494. By the 1630s the leading states kept armies of over 150,000 (Parker). These are paper strengths. The number of men actually present was often much lower.
- **Sieges become the main event.** A fortress with bastions could hold for months. Ostend held out for three years (1601–04) because it was supplied by sea; more than 100,000 people were killed, wounded or fell sick on both sides.
- **Military corridors.** Spain moved troops from Milan to the Netherlands along the **Spanish Road**, about 1,000 km (620 mi) through Savoy, Franche-Comté and Lorraine. A march took 32–60 days, usually 40–50, so about 20–25 km (12–16 mi) a day, with food bought at stopping places (*étapes*) along the way. More than 123,000 soldiers marched this route between 1567 and 1620, against only about 17,600 sent by sea.
- **Devastated land.** Large armies living off the land ruin whole regions. Show burnt villages and empty farms along their routes.

### Fortified lines on open frontiers

Where there are no mountains or rivers to block raiders, states built long lines:

- **Muscovy's Great Abatis Line** (nominally finished in 1566) stretched from Bryansk to Meshchera. It was made of felled trees laid as a barrier, ditches, earth banks, palisades and watchtowers, linking fortified towns such as Tula, Ryazan, Serpukhov and Kolomna. In the late 1500s about 35,000 local militia guarded it (some sources give more).
- **Muscovy's Belgorod Line** (1635–1654) was the second line, built further south along the southern edge of the forest steppe (the zone where woods give way to open grassland). It ran from the Vorskla River (today in Ukraine) east to the Tambov area. It was about 800 km (500 mi) long with its bends, or about 600 km (370 mi) in a straight line. It linked fortress towns such as Belgorod, Kozlov and Tambov (both founded in 1635–36 for the line) with earth banks, felled-tree barriers and palisades (walls of wooden stakes). It used rivers, marshes and woods as part of the barrier. Eighteen of its fortress towns were new. Sources differ a little on when it was finished; one gives 1646.
- **The Habsburg–Ottoman frontier** in Hungary and Croatia was a chain of fortresses and soldier-farmer settlements from the mid-1500s. For the medieval frontier layout it grew from (a forward line of small posts and a rear line of fortress towns), see [Outposts in wild and contested land](04-military-sites.md#outposts-in-wild-and-contested-land).

### What happened to the old castles

| Fate | Real examples | How to show it |
|---|---|---|
| Updated with earth bastions or gun platforms | Common in frontier and war zones | Castle icon inside a star outline (the star-fort symbol) |
| Rebuilt as a palace or residence | Chambord (begun 1519) and the other Loire châteaux; Heidelberg's Renaissance wings (1550s and 1601–07) | Castle icon with gardens, labelled "palace"; a royal palace or hunting lodge takes the small crown instead |
| Replaced by a country house nearby | Elizabethan "prodigy houses" (1570–1620): Burghley (finished 1587), Hardwick Hall (1590–97) | House inside a ring of park trees; the old castle nearby, drawn broken or grey |
| Kept as a prison, arsenal or mint | Tower of London | Castle icon in the town, labelled with its new use ("prison", "mint") |
| Deliberately wrecked ("slighted") | In and after the English Civil War, Parliament slighted, or planned to slight, more than 100 buildings (castles, town walls and houses); Corfe (1646), Kenilworth (1649) | Castle icon drawn broken or grey, labelled "(ruin)" |
| Abandoned and used as a quarry | Many minor castles in peaceful interiors | Small castle icon, broken or grey, often near a village |

> **Map tip:** In a 1500–1650 world, inland castles in peaceful regions should mostly be ruins, palaces or manor houses. New gun forts belong at frontiers, coasts, capitals and river crossings. Do not put a fresh star fort next to every village. See [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md) for the medieval castle network.

### When a strong ruler cuts the number of castles (France and Japan)

> **Rule of thumb:** When one ruler finally controls the whole land, private castles stop being useful to him and become a danger. The number of working castles then falls sharply, often by a single order.

**Why:** A castle lets a local lord defy the ruler. A ruler with his own army and cannon no longer needs these castles for defence. So he keeps the forts on the frontier and the seats of his governors, and orders the rest pulled down.

- **France, 1626:** a royal edict, issued in Louis XIII's name under Cardinal Richelieu, ordered that all towns and castles "in the interior of our realm" that were not important for frontier defence "shall be razed and demolished". It aimed at the strongholds of rebellious nobles.
- **Japan, 1615:** after the Tokugawa family took Osaka Castle in 1615, the shogunate (the military government) issued the "one castle per province" order (*ikkoku ichijō rei*). Each daimyo (great lord) kept one castle, normally his own residence, and demolished the others. A few of the greatest lords were allowed more. In the same year, the Laws for the Military Houses (*Buke shohatto*) said that every repair had to be reported to the shogunate, and that new building or enlargement needed its permission. The order stayed in force until the lords' domains were abolished in 1871.

| Japan | Before 1615 | After 1615 |
|---|---|---|
| Working castles | about 3,000 (a rounded figure) | about 170 (more than nine in ten gone) |
| Who holds one | Any lord strong enough to build one | Only the great lords, usually one each |
| Main job | War: base and refuge | Government: the seat of a lord's domain |
| New building | Free | Only with the shogunate's permission |

**Real examples:** Iwakuni Castle (western Japan) was built in 1601–08 and pulled down in 1615, when it was only seven years old. Himeji Castle, completely rebuilt in 1601–09, survived as a great lord's seat.

The castles that survived became the centres of **castle towns** (*jōkamachi*, "town below the castle"). The lord's officials, his warriors and the merchants who served them lived around the castle. These towns became the regional capitals of Japan for the next 250 years.

> **Map tip:** For a 1600–1650 map of a strong, centralised realm, keep about one working castle per province (or per great lord), set inside the province's main town. Draw that town around the castle, as the province's capital. Show the other old castle sites as ruins (the castle icon drawn broken or grey) or just as a place name such as "Castle Hill". Keep extra forts only on the frontier and the coast. A realm still split between strong nobles keeps many castles.

> **Fantasy twist:** If dragons or war-mages are your world's "artillery", the same logic applies: tall walls become weak, and defenders build low, thick, angled walls that cover each other. If the attackers **fly**, though, a star fort gives no protection. Expect roofed gun towers and underground shelters instead. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

## Ocean trade, ports and trading posts

### Bigger ships

| Ship | Main period | Typical size | Crew | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cog (medieval) | 1200s–1400s | 30–200 tons; a few were larger (see [chapter 05](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)) | small | North Sea and Baltic cargo |
| Carrack (Portuguese *nau*) | late 1400s–1600 | 400–2,000 tons; Portuguese India ships were often over 1,000; the *Madre de Deus* (captured 1592) was rated at about 1,600 tons | 600–700 aboard the *Madre de Deus* | India and spice route |
| Galleon | mid-1500s–1700 | up to about 1,000 tons on the Atlantic (many smaller); Manila galleons 1,700–2,000 | — | Treasure fleets, warships |
| Fluyt (Dutch) | from 1595 (first built at Hoorn) | medium cargo ship | about 12, where other ships of the same size needed about 30 | Cheap bulk cargo (grain, timber) |

Ship "tons" were measured in different ways (cargo space or the ship's own weight), so treat all these sizes as rough.

**Why it matters on the map:** big ships need deep water. Trade gathers in fewer, deeper harbours. Old river ports far inland lose out, and **outports** (small harbours near the sea) grow downstream. See [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md).

### Ocean routes and how long they took

- **Portugal to India (the *Carreira da Índia*):** about 6 months each way. Ships left Lisbon in February–April and came home in June–August of the following year, so a round trip, including the wait in India for the right wind, took about 14–18 months. There were 1,033 ship departures from Lisbon between 1497 and 1650, about 7 a year. Many never came back: of 806 ships sent out between 1497 and 1612, only 425 returned to Portugal (many others stayed in Asian waters, and 66 were lost at sea).
- **Spain to the Americas:** from the mid-1560s (the convoy system was set up in 1564–66) ships sailed in armed convoys. Usually two fleets a year left Seville. A fleet had about 17 ships in 1550 and more than 50, much larger ones by 1600. The fleets met at Havana for the voyage home.
- **Across the Pacific:** the Manila galleon linked Acapulco (Mexico) and Manila (Philippines) from 1565.

Ocean routes follow the wind belts, not straight lines. They touch land at a few famous stopping places: islands, capes and harbours for water, food and repairs.

### Monopoly ports

A state could force all ocean trade through **one port**, to collect taxes and control it. That port then grows very fast.

- **Seville:** in 1503 the Crown gave it the monopoly of trade with the Americas and set up the **Casa de Contratación** (House of Trade) there. By the late 1500s Seville had over 100,000 people and was the largest city in Spain.
- **Lisbon:** the Crown's India House controlled the spice trade.

### Chartered companies and trading posts ("factories")

A **chartered company** is a merchant company given a monopoly of trade with one region by the state. It could build forts, keep soldiers and make treaties.

A **factory** in this period is not a workshop. It is a trading post run by a *factor* (agent): a walled compound with warehouses, offices and homes, on land the local ruler allows. Some grew into forts and then into colonial cities.

| Power or company | Founded | Examples of posts (date) |
|---|---|---|
| Portuguese Crown (State of India from 1505) | — | Elmina, West Africa (1482); Goa (1510); Malacca (1511); Hormuz (1515) |
| English East India Company | 1600 | Surat, India (permanent factory 1613) |
| Dutch East India Company (VOC) | 1602 | Batavia, now Jakarta (1619), its Asian capital |
| Dutch West India Company | 1621 | Elmina (taken from Portugal 1637) |

**Scale:** between 1602 and 1796 the VOC sent 4,785 ships to Asia. The English company, its nearest rival, sent 2,690.

Merchants from abroad also have fixed quarters at home, for example the German merchants' hall in Venice, the *Fondaco dei Tedeschi* (rebuilt 1505–08).

> **Rule of thumb:** A trading post sits beside an existing local port city, not in empty land. It prefers a spot that is easy to defend from the sea: an island, a peninsula or a river mouth. It stays small (often tens to a few hundred Europeans; an estimate) unless it becomes a colonial capital.

> **Map tip:** On a continent map, mark ocean routes as long curving dotted lines (the sea-lane symbol), with arrows where the wind sets the direction, a sailing season ("out Feb–Apr") and two or three named ports of call. On the home coast, give the monopoly port the head-port symbol (a large anchor with a quay, name in capitals); on a local map add docks, a customs house and a "House of Trade". Abroad, mark trading posts with a small fort symbol next to a local port city, labelled with the company name. Do not use a flag: a small flag (pennant) marks a duchy seat.

### Planted colonial towns: the Spanish grid

Where a European power ruled the land itself, not just a trading post, it founded new towns. Spain founded the most in this period, and it planned them all to one pattern.

> **Rule of thumb:** A planted colonial town is a grid of square blocks around one central **plaza** (open square). The main church and the government houses stand on or beside the plaza. Every such town in a colony looks alike.

**Why:** A grid is quick to lay out with a cord and stakes. It makes it easy to give each settler a plot, and the town can grow outward in any direction. One central square gives the church and the royal officials a place to be seen, and space for markets and festivals.

**The rules:** Philip II's ordinances of 1573 on discovery and new settlement (148 articles, later part of the "Laws of the Indies") wrote down a pattern that had already been used for decades. Lima, for example, was founded in 1535 on a grid of 13 × 9 = 117 square blocks around its main square.

**Where the pattern came from:** Spain already had planned towns at home. The forerunner most often named is Santa Fe near Granada. In 1491, while besieging Granada, Ferdinand and Isabella turned their camp into a small walled town on a grid, with a square in the middle and a gate in each of the four sides (see [Camps that became towns](04-military-sites.md#camps-that-became-towns-or-nearly-did)). Historians of town planning have often called it the direct model for the American towns. Others say it was too small and too military, and point instead to older planned grid towns of the medieval Crown of Aragon (Bielza de Ory 2002). Either way, the idea of a planted grid town around a central square came from medieval Spain.

| Rule (1573 ordinances) | What it says |
|---|---|
| Where the plaza goes | At the landing place in a port town; in the centre of an inland town |
| Plaza shape | A rectangle, its length at least 1.5 times its width ("best for fiestas in which horses are used") |
| Plaza size | Matched to the number of people expected. Not smaller than 200 × 300 feet; not larger than about 530 × 800 feet; 400 × 600 feet is "a good proportion". These are Castilian feet of about 28 cm (11 in), so roughly 56 × 84 m, 148 × 223 m and 111 × 167 m |
| Streets | Twelve streets leave the plaza: one from the middle of each side and two from each corner |
| Church | On a whole block of its own, near the plaza |
| Government | The royal council and town council house, the customs house and the arsenal, near the church |

Real towns did not always follow every rule, but the plaza and the grid are almost everywhere. In Lima the blocks were about 110–125 m (360–410 ft) on a side (sources differ), and the cathedral and the viceroy's palace stand on the main square.

> **Map tip:** For a colony, draw small identical grid towns: a square of a few blocks around a plaza, with a church and a governor's or council house on the plaza. In a port town, put the plaza by the landing place. Draw the colonial capital to the same pattern, just with many more blocks. The regular grids should stand out against the irregular older towns of the local people. The grid is a local-map drawing: at kingdom scale, use the ordinary town symbols. For medieval planned towns with grids (*bastides*), see [Planted "new towns" and bastides](01-settlement-placement.md#planted-new-towns-and-bastides).

## Posts, roads and canals

### The post: relay stations

A **relay post** is a chain of stations where riders change horses (and sometimes riders) so that letters never stop.

- **Taxis post:** from about 1490 the Taxis family ran a line for Maximilian of Habsburg between Innsbruck and Mechelen in the Low Countries. Its centre moved to Brussels around 1500–01. The contract of 1505 added stations in Spain and France (Granada, Toledo, Blois, Paris, Lyon) and fixed delivery times for summer and winter.
- **Spacing:** stations every 20–40 km (12–25 mi).
- **Speed:** under the 1505 contract, Brussels to Innsbruck took 5½ days in summer and 6½ in winter, and Brussels to Paris 44 hours in summer (54 in winter). Relay posts covered up to about 150 km (95 mi) a day.
- **Public use:** the contract of 1516 let the Taxis carry private letters for profit, as long as court mail arrived on time. The emperor confirmed the Taxis monopoly in 1595, and from 1615 it was hereditary.
- **England:** there was a Master of the Posts from 1516, and post stages were about 32 km (20 mi) apart. Charles I opened the royal post to the public in 1635. The General Post Office was founded in 1660.

> **Map tip:** Along a post road, draw a small horn symbol (a post station) every 20–40 km (12–25 mi). Each station is an inn with large stables. Small towns on post roads grow; towns off them fall behind.

### Roads

Roads improve only a little. Some states make local communities repair them: England's Highways Act of 1555 made each parish give unpaid labour on its roads. Coaches for the rich become common in the later 1500s, but heavy goods still go by water wherever possible. The new thing is **planned military routes** such as the Spanish Road. Travel speeds stay as in the baseline: see [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md).

### Canals with pound locks

A **pound lock** is a chamber with a gate at each end. A boat enters, the gates close, and the water level rises or falls gently. This lets a canal climb hills. Most medieval canals were flat, or used dangerous "flash locks" (a single gate opened to let a rush of water carry boats through). Pound locks were not completely new: Damme near Bruges had one by 1396, and one of Milan's canals (the Naviglio di Bereguardo) got 18 of them in 1452–58. What is new after 1500 is that they spread, and that whole canals start to climb over hills.

| Canal | Dates | Size | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exeter Canal, England | 1564–66 | short: a few km (accounts differ; Wikipedia gives 5.2 km / 3¼ mi), 3 pound locks | First pound locks in Britain; brought small ships back to a city cut off by weirs |
| Briare Canal, France | 1604–42 (work stopped after Henry IV's murder in 1610 and restarted in 1638) | 57 km (35 mi), 36 locks, including a "staircase" (locks joined directly one after another) of 7 locks at Rogny | First summit-level canal in Europe with pound locks; joined the Loire and Seine river systems; 6,000–12,000 labourers |
| Dutch *trekvaarten* | 1630s–1660s (the first, Amsterdam–Haarlem, opened in 1632) | a network linking the cities of Holland | Straight canals for horse-towed passenger barges; about 7 km/h (4.5 mph), about 30 passengers, on a timetable |

A **summit-level canal** climbs over a watershed (the high ground between two river systems). It needs reservoirs on the summit to feed water to the locks.

> **Map tip:** Draw canals as straight or gently bending blue lines, unlike rivers. Mark each lock with two short bars close together across the line (a single bar with a "T" means a toll), grouped where the land rises. A summit canal needs a reservoir lake near its highest point. Towns grow at the canal's junctions with rivers.

## Money, printing and news

### Exchanges and banking centres

An **exchange** (French *bourse*) is a building where merchants meet daily to trade goods, loans and bills of exchange (written orders to pay a sum of money in another city, used instead of carrying coin).

| Date | Place | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1531 | Antwerp | First purpose-built exchange building |
| 1565–71 | London | Royal Exchange, copied from Antwerp's; founded by Thomas Gresham, opened by Elizabeth I in 1571 |
| 1609 | Amsterdam | Exchange Bank (*Wisselbank*), a city-backed public bank |
| 1611 | Amsterdam | Exchange building |

Great banking families still matter: the Fuggers of Augsburg lent to the Habsburgs, and Genoese bankers financed the Spanish crown.

> **Rule of thumb:** A continent has only one or two money centres at a time. They sit in the leading trading port of the day (Antwerp, then Amsterdam; Genoa; London), which is not always the capital.

### Printing towns

- By 1500, 236 towns in Europe had a printing press (a figure given by Braudel).
- Before 1650, printing took place in more than 1,300 places in Europe (Universal Short Title Catalogue data).
- Output grew roughly tenfold in the 1500s, to perhaps 150–200 million copies.
- **Big centres:** Venice (the largest c. 1500), Paris, Lyon, Antwerp, Basel, and the book-fair towns of Frankfurt and later Leipzig.
- **Ottoman lands:** printing in Arabic script was forbidden or discouraged until 1729. Jewish, Greek and Armenian presses did exist there.

A printing town needs paper (water-powered paper mills on nearby streams), customers (a university, a court, a bishop, lawyers) and good routes to send books out.

> **Map tip:** Label one or two cities "book fair" or "printing centre" in your realm, usually a university or trade city. Put paper mills on fast streams within a day or two of it. Printed news, maps and pamphlets now travel along the post roads.

## Capitals, states and borders

### Fixed capitals and palace complexes

> **Rule of thumb:** By 1600 most big states have one fixed capital, with a ring of royal palaces, hunting parks and monasteries within a day's ride.

- **Madrid:** Philip II made it the fixed capital in 1561. It was small before the court arrived: about 18,000 people in 1561. By 1598 it had about 80,000, and estimates for about 1600 range from 75,000 to 95,000.
- **El Escorial** (1563–84), about 45 km (28 mi) from Madrid: a huge palace, monastery, royal burial church and library in one building.
- **Other examples:** the Polish court moved from Kraków to Warsaw in 1596; German princes settled in fixed **residence towns** (*Residenzstädte*). For more, see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md).

States also become **more centralized**: more paid officials, permanent ambassadors, standing garrisons, and governors in provincial capitals. On the map this means a governor's palace and a citadel in each provincial capital, and the capital itself as a city of offices and noble houses.

### Surveying and mapping

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| 1533 | Gemma Frisius explains **triangulation**: measure one baseline carefully, then use angles to fix far-away points |
| 1569 | Mercator's world map with his new projection for sailors |
| 1570 | Ortelius publishes the first modern atlas (53 maps) in Antwerp |
| 1579 | Christopher Saxton's atlas of the counties of England and Wales (35 maps, surveyed in 1574–78) |
| 1615 | Snellius measures a long chain of triangles across Holland |

### Borders becoming lines, slowly

Maps now show borders as lines, and rulers start to think of their realms as areas with edges. But on the ground, borders were still often defined as **lists of places**. The Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659) made the mountains the border between France and Spain and gave France the "villages" of northern Cerdanya. Llívia was legally a town, not a village, so it stayed Spanish, and it is still a Spanish island inside France today. The exact line was only marked on the ground by the Treaties of Bayonne, 1856–68.

> **Map tip:** For a 1600-style map you can draw firmer, unbroken borders for big kingdoms. Keep the dash-dot border line; a solid line means a road. Keep enclaves (the owner's colour with a thin outline), disputed strips (two parallel dash-dot lines) and patchwork areas, especially in a "Holy Roman Empire" type region. Put pairs of fortresses facing each other across important borders, and mark a few contested towns. See [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md) for medieval frontier zones.

## The countryside: enclosure, new crops and drainage

### Enclosure

**Enclosure** means turning open fields and common land (shared by the whole village) into private fields surrounded by hedges, walls or ditches. Often it also meant turning ploughland into sheep pasture.

- J. R. Wordie estimated that about 45% of England was already enclosed by 1500, 47% by 1600 and 71% by 1700. So the famous Tudor enclosures covered only a small area; the big new wave came in the 1600s (and continued in the 1700s).
- In the 1500s it was most visible, and most hated, in the Midlands, where landlords turned whole villages into sheep runs. Parliament passed laws against it ("tillage acts") between 1489 and 1597. Enclosure was a cause of Kett's Rebellion (1549) and the Midland Revolt (1607).
- In eastern Europe (Poland, Prussia, the Baltic lands) a different change happened: lords built large grain estates worked by serfs, and shipped grain down the rivers to Baltic ports for export.

### New crops

| Crop | Arrives | Becomes a common field crop |
|---|---|---|
| Maize | Spain within a few decades of 1492 | A common peasant food in southern Europe by the 1600s |
| Potato | Canary Islands 1567; Spain about 1570; Britain and Ireland 1588–93 | Ireland on a wide scale in the early 1600s; most of Europe only in the 1700s–1800s |
| Tomato | Spain soon after 1492; eaten in Italy by 1544 | Garden crop in this period |

> **Rule of thumb:** Before 1650, American crops are mostly garden plants. The big exception is maize in Iberia and Italy. Do not cover your 1600 map with potato fields.

### Drainage and new land

- **Dutch polders:** a **polder** is low land surrounded by dikes and kept dry by pumping. The Beemster lake in North Holland was drained between 1607 and 1612 with 43 windmills, creating about 7,200 ha (72 km² or 28 sq mi) of new farmland. The Purmer (1622), Wormer (1626) and Schermer (1635, about 48 km² or 18 sq mi) followed.
- **English Fens:** drainage began in earnest in the 1630s, led by the Earl of Bedford and his investors. A second phase in the 1650s, with the New Bedford River (completed 1652), finished the main work. The Great Level covered about 1,300 km² (500 sq mi). Local people, who lost their fishing and fowling, fought it. The drained peat shrank, so by the late 1600s parts were flooding again.

> **Map tip:** Draw a polder as a perfectly regular grid of straight roads and ditches inside a ring dike, with windmills (an X of four sails on a dot) along the edge and farms evenly spaced along the roads. It should look completely different from the irregular old villages around it.

### Estates and country houses

Rich families build **country houses** with parks, avenues, walled gardens and deer parks, often on former monastery land (see [Religion](#religion-the-reformation-on-the-map)). England's "prodigy houses" (1570–1620) were built to host the monarch on a royal tour. Big parks can swallow whole villages and old roads. For village layouts see [Villages, Farms and the Countryside](06-villages-and-countryside.md).

> **Map tip:** Give each county-sized area one to three country houses. Draw each as a house inside a ring of park trees, an oval park 1–3 km (0.6–2 mi) across (an estimate), with a straight avenue to the nearest road. Put some on old abbey sites and some beside a ruined castle. Show enclosed fields as small irregular polygons (hedged fields) in the sheep country, and keep open fields (unhedged areas with blocks of hatching) elsewhere.

## Industry: furnaces, mines and mills

### Blast furnaces

A **blast furnace** is a tall stone furnace with bellows driven by a waterwheel. It makes liquid cast iron, which can be poured into moulds, for example to make cannon. It needs iron ore, a lot of charcoal (so a lot of managed woodland) and a dammed stream.

- England's first blast furnace was at Queenstock, Buxted (about 1490–91), followed by Newbridge in Ashdown Forest (1496). Both are in the Weald of Sussex.
- They stayed few until about 1530. Then many were built: by the later 1500s the Weald had about 50 furnaces, and as many as 100 furnaces and forges together. The Weald industry peaked around 1590. The first British furnaces outside the Weald appeared in the 1550s, and British output as a whole peaked around 1620.
- England cast its first iron cannon in 1543, at Buxted.
- The method came from the Namur region (today in Belgium), through Normandy, to the Weald.

> **Map tip:** Put iron works in wooded valleys with dammed streams: a chain of **hammer ponds** (mill ponds for furnaces and forges) down one valley, with charcoal burners' clearings in the woods around them. Label them "Furnace Pond" or "Hammer Pond", so they are not read as fishponds (small rectangles in a chain). Iron districts are wooded, not bare.

### Mining booms

| Place | Start | Peak size | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joachimsthal (Jáchymov), Bohemia | Silver town founded 1516 | about 18,000–20,000 by 1534, the second town of Bohemia after Prague | Its coin, the *Joachimsthaler*, gave us the word "thaler" (and later "dollar"). Declined because of war (the Schmalkaldic War, 1546–47), the Counter-Reformation driving Protestant miners away, deep mines that flooded for lack of pumps, and cheap American silver |
| Potosí (in today's Bolivia) | 1545 | about 130,000 by 1603 (at least 120,000 of them Andean); higher figures of 160,000–200,000 are claimed but disputed | At 4,067 m (13,343 ft); produced about 60% of the world's silver in the late 1500s; worked by forced Indigenous labour (the *mita*, about 13,000 men a year) |

> **Rule of thumb:** A rich strike can turn an empty valley into one of a kingdom's biggest towns within 10–20 years, and then fade almost as fast. Draw boom towns big, crowded and unwalled, in rough terrain, with a mint, mine symbols (crossed hammers) around them, and roads carrying food in and silver out.

### Mills

- **Paper mills** spread with printing. They need fast, clean water and linen rags. England got its first paper mill in the 1490s (John Tate's short-lived mill near Hertford, about 1495).
- **Gunpowder mills** grind saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal. They explode, so they sit away from towns, often as separate buildings spread along a stream. Example: Chilworth, Surrey, set up by the East India Company in the mid-1620s on the Tillingbourne stream.
- **Wind sawmills:** Cornelis Corneliszoon of Uitgeest patented his crankshaft for a wind-powered sawmill in 1593–94. It cut timber into planks about 30 times faster than sawing by hand. The Zaan district north of Amsterdam filled with industrial windmills and is often called the first industrial area in the world.

### Shipyards and arsenals

State shipyards become huge. The **Venetian Arsenal** covered about 45 ha (110 acres), around 15% of Venice. Up to 16,000 workers is often quoted for its peak in the early 1500s, but normal numbers were much lower. Henry VIII founded royal dockyards at Woolwich and Deptford near London. Dutch yards on the Zaan and at Amsterdam built ships in series.

> **Map tip:** Put shipyards at a sheltered deep-water port with timber arriving by river or sea. Draw them as a walled yard with slipways (sloping ramps where ships are built), next to a rope-walk (a very long, narrow shed for making rope). At kingdom scale, mark a state arsenal or royal dockyard with an anchor plus a castle tower. Put gunpowder mills a few kilometres outside a town on a stream, and paper mills on clean streams upriver of a printing town; draw both as watermill wheels on the stream, labelled "Powder Mills" or "Paper Mill". A wind-mill industrial district looks like a forest of windmill symbols (an X of four sails on a dot) along a waterway.

For medieval mines, mills and industrial towns, see [Industry, Resources and Special Towns](07-industry-and-resources.md).

## Religion: the Reformation on the map

### England and Wales: the dissolution of the monasteries (1536–1541)

- About 900 religious houses: about 260 for monks, 300 for canons, 142 nunneries and 183 friaries.
- About 12,000 religious people: 4,000 monks, 3,000 canons, 3,000 friars and 2,000 nuns.
- Monasteries held about a quarter of England's landed wealth.
- The small houses went first (243 were closed in 1536). The large ones "surrendered" by 1540.

**What happened to the buildings:**

| Fate | Example | Map symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Stripped of lead roofs and left as a ruin | Fountains and Rievaulx (Yorkshire); Tintern (Wales) | Abbey symbol (church with a square cloister) drawn broken or grey, labelled "(ruin)" |
| Turned into a country house | Lacock Abbey | House inside a ring of park trees, name ending in "Abbey" or "Priory" |
| Abbey church bought by the town as its parish church | Tewkesbury | Large parish-church symbol in a small town |
| Made a cathedral of a new diocese | Gloucester, Peterborough, Oxford, Bristol, Chester (1540–42) | Cathedral (church with two towers, or a mitre) |
| Used as a quarry for local building stone | Very common | A small grey fragment of the abbey symbol, or just the name |

Great shrines were destroyed (Walsingham and Becket's shrine at Canterbury, both 1538), and pilgrim routes emptied.

### Elsewhere in Europe

- **Protestant lands:** Scotland's Reformation came in 1560. Protestant German princes took over church lands, and in 1525 the Teutonic Knights' state became the secular Duchy of Prussia. Former abbeys became schools, hospitals, offices or princely residences.
- **Religious borders:** the Peace of Augsburg (1555) let each German ruler choose Lutheranism or Catholicism for his land. This created a patchwork, with churches of one faith just across the border from the other. In France the Edict of Nantes (1598) gave the Protestant Huguenots fortified "security towns" such as La Rochelle, which fell to the king after a siege in 1627–28. In the Dutch Republic, Catholics could worship only in hidden "house churches".
- **Catholic lands:** monasteries stay and even multiply. The **Counter-Reformation** (the Catholic reform movement) adds new building: Jesuit colleges (the order was founded in 1540), new churches and new pilgrimage shrines.

> **Map tip:** Make your religious map as clear as the political one. Where each ruler chose his land's faith (as in Germany after 1555), the faith border follows a political border, so the border line is already there. Give each faith a soft colour tint, not a new line, so it is never mistaken for a realm border. On the Protestant side, use ruined abbeys, country houses called "Abbey", and big town churches. On the Catholic side, keep the working monasteries and add a Jesuit college in the main towns. Put a few pilgrim shrines (scallop shell or reliquary symbols) just inside the Catholic side of the border. For medieval religious sites, see [Religious, Cultural, Legal and Ancient Sites](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md).

## Drawing a world in transition

> **Rule of thumb:** A real 1600 landscape has three layers of age. Old things (castles, abbeys, medieval walls) are ruined, converted or patched. Middle-aged things (villages, fields, market towns) carry on unchanged. New things (star forts, canals, polders, country houses, post roads) are geometric and stand out.

### A checklist

1. **Start with your medieval map.** Keep villages, market towns and roads.
2. **Grow the top.** Enlarge the capital and one or two ocean ports. Shrink one old port whose river has silted up or been blockaded.
3. **Change the castles.** Interior castles become ruins or palaces. Draw a few country houses with parks nearby. In a strongly centralised realm, keep only about one working castle per province.
4. **Add new fortifications only where they pay:** a citadel in the capital or a rebellious city, star-fort towns on the dangerous frontier, gun forts at harbour mouths, towers along raided coasts.
5. **Mix old and new walls.** A frontier town can have a medieval wall on the safe side and new bastions on the side facing the enemy. This half-finished look is very realistic.
6. **Add the new links:** post stations every 20–40 km (12–25 mi) on the main roads, a straight canal or two, and ocean routes from your main port.
7. **Change the landscape where money went:** a geometric polder in a former lake or marsh, enclosed fields in the sheep country, a mining boom town in the hills, iron furnaces in wooded valleys.
8. **Draw the religious map:** a confessional border (a border between two faiths, shown with a colour tint for each faith), abbey ruins on one side, working monasteries on the other.
9. **Add an overseas layer if you have a world map:** trading posts beside foreign port cities, labelled with company names.

### Symbols and labels

These symbols match the guide's [master legend](13-quick-reference.md#master-legend-and-label-hierarchy).

| Feature | Suggested symbol | Label example |
|---|---|---|
| Star fort or fortress town | Five- or six-pointed star **outline**, or a star-edged town outline; never filled (a filled star means a capital) | "Fort St John", "Neufort" |
| Bastioned citadel | Pentagon outline joined to the town | "Citadel" |
| Ruined castle | Castle icon drawn broken or grey | "Old Castle (ruin)" |
| Royal palace or hunting lodge | Small crown on its own | "Hartwood (royal lodge)" |
| Country house | House inside a ring of park trees | "Elmswood House", "Lacey Abbey" |
| Ruined abbey | Abbey symbol (church with a square cloister) drawn broken or grey | "Abbey (ruin)" |
| Post station | Small horn on the road | "Post" |
| Canal with locks | Straight blue line; each lock as two short bars close together across it | "Royal Canal" |
| Polder | Grid inside a dike, with mill symbols | "New Polder (drained 1612)" |
| Monopoly port | Large anchor with a quay; name in capitals | "SALTHAVEN" |
| Ocean route | Dotted line at sea, with arrows where the wind sets the direction | "To the Spice Isles (out Feb–Apr)" |
| Trading post | Small fort beside a foreign port city | "Company factory" |
| Coastal tower | Small triangles along the coast | none needed |
| Arsenal or royal dockyard | Anchor plus a castle tower | "Royal Dockyard" |

> **Map tip:** Date new things on your map or in its notes: "New Polder (drained 1612)", "Citadel (1567)". Dates make the history visible and help you keep the layers consistent.

> **Fantasy twist:** In many fantasy worlds, magic plays the role of gunpowder: a new power that makes old defences useless. Use the real pattern: the rich centre adopts it first, old walls get patched, special forts appear on the frontier, and the remote countryside barely changes for generations. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md) and [Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them](11-common-mistakes.md).

**Soon after 1650:** Several famous changes come just after this chapter's period. Vauban built France's great fortress belt (the *pré carré*) from the 1670s. The Canal du Midi (1666–81) linked the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. Versailles became the model palace capital from the 1680s. The potato and turnpike (toll) roads changed the countryside mostly in the 1700s.

## Quick summary

- After 1500 the countryside stays medieval; the change is at the top: capitals, ports, forts and long-distance links.
- Europe grows from about 62 to 78 million (without Russia and the Ottoman lands) between 1500 and 1600, then stalls. Capitals and ocean ports grow fastest: London went from about 50,000 to about 400,000 between 1500 and 1650.
- City ranks change quickly: Antwerp rose to about 100,000 and then collapsed; Amsterdam, London, Seville and Lisbon boomed.
- Cannon made high walls weak. Bastioned "star" fortifications appeared in Italy from the late 1400s, matured there c. 1500–1540, and spread north in the 1530s–40s. They were very expensive, so only frontiers, capitals, ports and key crossings get them.
- Citadels control cities; chains of towers and gun forts guard coasts; armies grow to over 150,000 for great states, and sieges can last years.
- Old castles become ruins, palaces, prisons or are replaced by country houses. Strong rulers order most castles destroyed: France in 1626; Japan in 1615, from about 3,000 castles to about 170.
- Ocean ships of several hundred to 2,000 tons link monopoly ports (Seville, Lisbon) with trading posts abroad; chartered companies (1600, 1602) build fortified "factories". Spain plants colonial towns on one standard grid around a central plaza (rules of 1573).
- Relay posts with stations every 20–40 km (12–25 mi) carry letters up to about 150 km (95 mi) a day; pound-lock canals start to climb hills (Briare, 1604–42).
- Exchanges (Antwerp 1531, London 1571, Amsterdam 1609–11) and printing (236 towns by 1500, 1,300+ places by 1650) create new kinds of special towns.
- Borders become lines on maps but are still lists of villages on the ground; surveying and atlases arrive.
- The countryside sees more enclosure, a few new crops (mainly maize), drained polders and fens, blast furnaces, and mining boom towns.
- The Reformation leaves ruined and converted abbeys and clear religious borders.
