# Where Settlements Are Built (and Why)

Real people did not put towns in random places. Every village, town and city answered the same few questions: is there water, can we grow food, can we defend it, and can goods and people reach it? This chapter explains those questions, shows the classic places where settlements grow, and gives you numbers for spacing, so that every dot on your map has a reason to be there.

## The six things every settlement needs

> **Rule of thumb:** Water, food, fuel, safety, access, health. A village needs the first three close by. A town also needs access. A city needs access by water.

| Need | What people looked for | How close it must be | What it looks like on a map |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh water | River, stream, spring, reliable well, lake | Inside or next to the settlement | Every village touches blue: a river, a stream or a spring line |
| Food | A **mix** of land: arable (ploughed fields), meadow (hay), pasture (grazing), woodland | Fields within a 15–30 minute walk (roughly 1–2.5 km, 0.6–1.5 mi) | Villages sit where two kinds of land meet |
| Fuel and building material | Wood, peat or coal; stone, clay or timber | Within a few km for daily fuel; farther for building stone (by water) | Woods near most villages; quarries near stone towns |
| Defence | Hills, river bends, islands, spurs (rock tongues), marsh around a dry spot | The site itself | Towns on hills, in river loops, on islands |
| Communications | Fords, bridges, confluences, harbours, the mouth of a mountain pass, crossroads | The site itself | Bigger places at river crossings and route junctions |
| Shelter and health | Sunny slopes, out of the wind, above floods, away from "fever marshes" | The site itself | Villages on the sunny side of valleys and on the edge of marshes, not in them |

### Fresh water

**Rule:** No settlement is far from water. Villages need it every day for people, animals, brewing and washing.

**Why:** Water is heavy. Carrying it uphill every day is hard work. Deep wells were expensive to dig.

**Real examples:**
- Old Sarum is an Iron Age hill fort near modern Salisbury, England, with a Norman castle and cathedral inside it. The clergy complained about three things: water was scarce and expensive (and the castle guards sometimes controlled access to it), the wind was fierce, and they quarrelled with the castle garrison. Pope Honorius III gave permission to move (1217–18), and in 1220 the bishop laid the foundation stone of a new cathedral down in the river valley. New Salisbury grew by the river. Old Sarum slowly emptied.
- On chalk and limestone hills, water sinks into the rock. It comes out as springs where the porous rock sits on clay. Villages form a line along these springs (see [spring-line villages](#classic-site-types) below).

**Flood risk.** People wanted water close, but not in the house. The usual answer was a **river terrace**: a flat step of gravel a few metres above the flood plain. Roman and medieval London stood on two low hills (Cornhill and Ludgate Hill) on the north bank of the Thames. The higher one, Cornhill, rises only to about 18 m (58 ft) above sea level. Across the river, on the south bank, was a patch of dry ground at Southwark. The flood plain itself was left as meadow.

### Food: a mix of land

**Rule:** A farming village needs four kinds of land, not one.

| Land type | Use | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| Arable | Grain for bread, ale and pottage (a thick stew of grain and vegetables) | Well-drained, fairly flat land |
| Meadow | Hay to feed animals in winter | Flood plains and wet valley floors |
| Pasture | Summer grazing for oxen, sheep, cows | Hills, heaths, rough ground, fallow fields |
| Woodland | Fuel, building timber, pigs (acorns) | Steeper or poorer land, parish edges |

**Why:** Ploughs need oxen. Oxen need hay in winter. Hay needs meadow. Fields need manure from animals. So a village with only one kind of land fails.

**Numbers:**
- Meadow was precious. Medieval English land surveys usually valued an acre of meadow well above an acre of arable, often several times as much.
- A person needs about 1 ha (2.5 acres) of arable land, or 2–3 ha (5–7 acres) of all land including pasture, meadow and woodland (see the shared baseline in [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md)).
- So a village of 200 people needs about 2 km² (0.8 sq mi) of fields and 4–6 km² (1.5–2.3 sq mi) of land in total. Add common waste (unfarmed rough land shared by the village) and woods, and you get the typical English lowland **parish** (the area served by one village church) of about 14 km² (5.4 sq mi).

**Map result:** Villages love **edges**. They sit where a river valley meets a hillside, where a fen meets dry land, or where downland meets a clay vale. From one spot the village can reach meadow, fields and pasture.

> **Rule of thumb:** Put villages on the boundary between two kinds of land, not in the middle of one.

### Fuel and building material

Every household burned fuel every day for cooking, heating, baking and brewing. Most villages had woodland or a fuel source within a few kilometres. Where wood was scarce, people burned peat (fens, the Netherlands, Ireland, the Scottish islands) or dung (steppe). Building material was local: timber and wattle in forest country, stone where there was stone, clay brick in the stone-poor plains of the Low Countries and the Baltic coast. Fine building stone for cathedrals and castles moved long distances, but almost always by water.

### Defence

**Rule:** The more dangerous the times, the higher and more enclosed the site.

| Defensive site | How it helps | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hilltop or plateau | Attackers must climb | Orvieto (Italy, on a volcanic rock plateau); Laon (France); Old Sarum (England) |
| Spur or promontory | Cliffs on three sides; wall only one side | Luxembourg (the Bock rock, acquired by Count Siegfried in 963); Edinburgh (castle rock with a ridge behind it) |
| River bend (meander) | Water on three sides | Durham (founded 995 in a loop of the River Wear); Bern (founded 1191 in a loop of the Aare); Shrewsbury (Severn) |
| Island | Water all round | Paris (Île de la Cité); Stockholm (first recorded 1252); Venice; Lindau (Lake Constance) |
| Dry spot in a marsh | Marsh slows attackers | Ely (England, on an "isle" in the Fens) |

**Mediterranean example:** In Latium, central Italy, many scattered farms were regrouped into fortified hilltop villages called *castelli*. This happened mainly in the 10th and early 11th centuries. Historians call it *incastellamento* ("castle-building"), a term made famous by Pierre Toubert (1973). Scholars still argue about exactly when and why it happened, but the result is clear: central Italy is full of hilltop villages. Raided coasts show the same effect (see [Coasts](#coasts)).

**The trade-off:** A strong site is often a bad site for trade and water. Old Sarum lost to Salisbury for exactly this reason. In peaceful times, towns move down to the river.

### Communications

A place that people **must pass through** has a reason to grow: a ford, a bridge, a harbour, the foot of a pass. These are explained in [Classic site types](#classic-site-types). The routes themselves are in [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md).

### Shelter and health

- **Sunny slopes.** In the Alps the sunny side of a valley (*adret* in French) has a longer growing season, less snow and higher tree and crop limits than the shady side (*ubac*). Villages and fields are on the adret; the ubac is left to forest.
- **Out of the wind.** At Old Sarum, the writer Peter of Blois said the wind was sometimes so strong that the church services could not be heard.
- **Away from fever marshes.** Malaria ("ague", "marsh fever") existed in Europe's coastal marshes, including Kent, Essex, the Fens, the Italian Maremma and the Pontine Marshes. Mary Dobson (1997) showed that in the 17th and 18th centuries, marsh parishes in Kent and Essex often buried more people than they baptised.
- **Healthy air.** The Abbasid caliph al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762. Historians list his reasons as its place on trade routes, its water supply and its mild climate. When he later built his Khuld Palace there, he put it on the highest part of the Tigris bank, for fresh air and away from the insects of the riverside.

> **Map tip:** Before you draw any settlement, mark on your terrain map: rivers and springs, flood plains, marshes, sunny valley sides, good soil, and defensible spots (hills, river loops, islands). Most villages will fall on the edges between these zones. A dot with none of these features nearby needs a special reason, such as a mine or a monastery.

## Site and situation

These two words come from school geography, and they are the most useful tools in this chapter.

| | **Site** | **Situation** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The exact spot: the ground the settlement stands on | Its place in the wider world: routes, neighbours, resources, region |
| Questions | Is it dry? Is there water? Can it be defended? Is there room to grow? | Is it on a river people sail? At a crossing? Between two regions that trade? In rich farmland? |
| Decides | Whether the place **survives** | Whether the place **grows big** |

**Rule:** Thousands of places have a good site. Only a few have a great situation. Situation decides which village stays a village and which becomes a city.

**Real examples:**
- **London.** The *site* was modest: two low gravel hills by a marshy river. The *situation* was superb: the lowest point where the Thames could be bridged, near the old tidal limit (the highest point the tide reaches up a river), where sea ships, river boats and roads all met. That is why it became the largest city in England.
- **Venice.** The *site* was terrible: mud islands in a lagoon, no farmland, and little fresh water (rain cisterns, plus water brought by boat). The *situation* was excellent: safe from land armies, at the head of the Adriatic, close to the passes into Germany. Venice became one of Europe's largest cities (see [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md)).
- **Old Sarum and Salisbury.** Same situation, different site. The better site (water, flat ground by the river) won.

**Why situation matters so much:** Moving bulk goods overland was very expensive. In England around 1300, carrying goods by land cost about 8 times as much as by sea and 2 times as much as by river (the shared baseline gives land : river : sea ≈ 8 : 4 : 1). So towns that needed to import grain, timber or stone had to sit on **navigable** water (water deep enough for boats). Nearly every town above about 10,000 people sits on a navigable river or the coast. The few exceptions had something special. Granada sat in an unusually rich irrigated plain (the *Vega*). Siena lived on banking and on the main pilgrim road to Rome.

> **Rule of thumb:** Good site = the village survives. Good situation = the village becomes a town. Great situation on water = it becomes a city.

> **Map tip:** For each large settlement, write two short notes: "site: ..." and "situation: ...". If you cannot write the situation note for a city, it should probably be a town.

## Classic site types

These are the standard "reasons to be here." Most real towns combine two or three of them.

| Site type | What it is | Why a town grows there | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ford town** | Where a river is shallow enough to wade or drive a cart across | Roads must meet here; travellers stop | Oxford ("oxen ford", first recorded 912); Frankfurt ("ford of the Franks", first recorded 794); Hertford; Stratford |
| **Bridge town** | Where a bridge replaced or added to a ford | Tolls, markets, controls traffic | Cambridge; Innsbruck ("bridge over the Inn"); Saarbrücken |
| **Lowest bridging point** | The bridge nearest the sea | Roads on both banks AND ships from the sea; often also the tidal limit | London (Thames); York (Ouse); Exeter (Exe); Gloucester (Severn); Stirling (Forth: a map by Matthew Paris from the 1240s–1250s seems to show only one bridge in all Britain, at Stirling) |
| **Head of navigation / break of bulk** | The highest point big ships can reach, or a point where cargo must change boats ("break of bulk") | Goods must be unloaded, stored, sold, reloaded | Cologne (ships from the lower Rhine were too big for the river above, so cargo usually changed boats here; from 1259 a *staple right*, a town's legal right to make passing merchants stop, forced them to unload and offer their goods for sale for 3 days); Schaffhausen (the name probably means "boat houses": goods were unloaded above the Rhine Falls and carried around them); Seville (sea port about 80 km (50 mi) up the Guadalquivir from the Atlantic) |
| **Confluence town** | Where two rivers join | Two river routes meet; a defensible tongue of land between them | Koblenz (Latin *Confluentes*: Rhine and Moselle); Lyon (Rhône and Saône); Passau (Danube, Inn and Ilz); Belgrade (Sava and Danube) |
| **River-bend (meander) town** | Inside a tight loop of a river | Water on three sides; only one side to wall | Durham (995); Bern (1191); Shrewsbury; Besançon (Doubs); Toledo (Tagus) |
| **Hilltop town** | On a hill, ridge or plateau | Defence, health, view | Italian *castelli*; Orvieto; Carcassonne; Shaftesbury |
| **Island town** | On a river, lake or sea island | Defence; easy river crossing in two short bridges | Paris (Île de la Cité); Stockholm; Venice; Lindau; Mont-Saint-Michel (tidal island) |
| **Spring-line village** | A row of villages where springs come out at the foot (or near the top) of a hill **scarp** (a steep slope along the edge of a ridge) | Reliable water plus fields below and pasture above | Lincoln Cliff (a limestone ridge over 80 km (50 mi) long, with more than 40 villages and towns along it: roughly one every 2 km (1.2 mi)); the foot of the South Downs (Fulking, Poynings, Edburton); the edges of the Mendips (Wells, Wookey Hole, Blagdon, Compton Martin) |
| **Gap town** | Where a river cuts a gap through a line of hills | All routes across the hills funnel through the gap | Lincoln (River Witham through the limestone ridge); Guildford (River Wey through the North Downs); Lewes (River Ouse through the South Downs) |
| **Pass town** | At the foot of a mountain pass | Travellers rest, change animals, hire guides; customs posts | Altdorf and Bellinzona (Gotthard: the first bridge across the Schöllenen gorge, built around 1220, opened the route); Chur (several Alpine passes); Susa (Mont Cenis); Aosta (Great St Bernard) |
| **Harbour town** | A sheltered bay, deep water, protection from storms | Sea trade, fishing, shipbuilding | Genoa; Bergen; Dubrovnik (Ragusa); Brest |
| **Estuary town** | Up a tidal river mouth, usually at the first good crossing | Sheltered from storms and raiders; reaches inland by river | Bordeaux (Gironde/Garonne, about 100 km inland); Hamburg (Elbe, about 110 km); Antwerp (Scheldt, about 80 km); Rouen (Seine) — all roughly 80–120 km (50–75 mi) from the open sea |
| **Oasis town** | Around a desert spring, river or underground channel | The only water for days of travel; caravans must stop | Sijilmasa (Morocco, founded 757, northern end of a trans-Saharan route, abandoned 1393); Damascus (watered by the Barada river and the Ghouta gardens); Yazd and Kerman (Iran, on *qanats*, underground water channels: see [Desert](#desert)) |
| **Wet-point settlement** | A spot chosen for water in a dry land | Water is the scarce thing | Spring-line villages on chalk; oasis villages; wells on dry plateaus |
| **Dry-point settlement** | A spot chosen for dry ground in a wet land | Dry land is the scarce thing | Ely (on a clay "isle" about 26 m (85 ft) high in the Fens; abbey founded 673); Glastonbury (Somerset Levels); Frisian *terpen* (man-made dwelling mounds) |

### Notes on the most important types

**Lowest bridging point and head of navigation.** On many rivers the lowest bridge, the old tidal limit and the highest point sea ships could reach were all at the same place, or close together. That place usually became the region's chief town (London, York, Exeter, Gloucester). It is the single best spot on your map for a big town.

**Tidal coasts: ports sit inland.** On the tidal Atlantic and North Sea coasts, the big medieval ports often sat 50–120 km (30–75 mi) up an estuary (a wide tidal river mouth). There they were safe from storms and raiders, they had the first bridge, and the tide helped ships upstream. Later, as ships grew bigger or rivers silted up, small "outports" grew nearer the sea: Damme and Sluis for Bruges, Topsham for Exeter, Sanlúcar (at the river mouth) for Seville. In the almost tideless Mediterranean, ports more often sat directly on a sheltered bay (Genoa, Marseille, Barcelona, Dubrovnik).

**Fords.** A ford needs firm gravel and water shallow enough for carts at normal levels. On big rivers fords are rare, so a crossing that serves several roads is a strong candidate for a town.

### Reading place names

Many real names tell you the site type. You can use the same trick for your fantasy names.

| Name element | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -ford, -furt, -voorde | Ford | Oxford, Frankfurt, Erfurt |
| -bridge, -brück, -bruck | Bridge | Cambridge, Innsbruck, Saarbrücken |
| -mouth, -münde, Aber- | River mouth | Plymouth, Monmouth, Warnemünde, Aberdeen |
| Confluentes, Coblenz, Condé | Confluence | Koblenz, Condé-sur-l'Escaut |
| -wick, -wich, -vik | Trading place, bay | Ipswich, Reykjavík |
| -ley, -sart, -rode, -roth | Forest clearing | Many English villages; "Les Essarts" in France; Wernigerode |
| -ey, -holm | Island, or dry ground in wet land | Chertsey, Romsey, Stockholm |

> **Fantasy twist:** If griffon riders or teleport circles move people and letters, crossings matter less for **travellers**. They still matter for **goods**: grain, timber and stone still go by boat and cart. Keep the big towns at the crossings. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

> **Map tip:** Give each town one or two site types and draw them clearly: the river loop around the meander town, the bridge symbol at the lowest crossing, the gap in the hills at the gap town. Then name it so the name matches the site, like "Oakford" at a ford or "Highbridge" at a bridge. Readers notice when geography and names agree.

## Settlement by terrain and biome

The same rules apply everywhere, but the answer changes with the land.

| Terrain | Where settlements go | Typical pattern | Density (see [chapter 02](02-population-and-sizes.md)) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fertile river valleys and plains | River terraces, valley edges, crossings | Many nucleated (tightly grouped) villages; towns on the river | Highest: 30–50 per km² (80–130 per sq mi) at peak |
| Rolling hills | Valley floors, spring lines, sheltered slopes | Villages in valleys; hilltops empty or pasture | Medium |
| Uplands and mountains | Valley floors, sunny terraces, pass feet | Hamlets and farmsteads; seasonal huts higher up | Low: 2–10 per km² (5–25 per sq mi) |
| Coasts | Sheltered bays, estuaries, river mouths, slightly inland on raided coasts | Chains of fishing villages and ports | Varies |
| Marsh and fens | Islands of dry ground, fen edges, man-made mounds | Villages on the edge; few inside | Low inside, high on the edge |
| Forest | Clearings, river valleys through the forest | Strings of new villages pushing in | Low, rising as land is cleared |
| Steppe and grassland | River valleys, steppe edges | Nomad camps; a few trade towns on rivers | Very low: under 1–2 per km² (under 3–5 per sq mi) |
| Desert | Oases, wadis, *qanat* lines | Isolated clusters; caravan stops | Near zero between oases |
| Islands | Sheltered side, inland on raided islands | One main port; villages on fertile patches | Varies |
| Cold north | Sheltered fjords, coastal lowlands | Scattered farms; very few towns | Very low |

### Fertile river valleys and plains

This is where most people lived. Villages sit on terraces just above the flood plain, every 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi). The flood plain is meadow. Towns grow at crossings and confluences. In England's Midlands (the "Central Province" of Roberts and Wrathmell 2000), large nucleated villages with open fields filled almost all the land.

### Rolling hills

Valleys hold the villages; hilltops hold sheep pasture and sometimes old hill forts. On chalk and limestone, the spring line decides everything. Parishes here often became **strip parishes**: long, thin parishes running from the hilltop pasture, through the village at the spring line, down to the meadows in the valley. Each village gets a slice of every land type.

### Uplands and mountains

- **Valley floors and terraces** hold permanent villages, usually on the sunny side and on alluvial fans (fan-shaped slopes of gravel where side streams enter the valley) that are safe from the main river's floods.
- **Altitude bands.** In the Valais (Switzerland), hamlets sit on sunny terraces at about 1,000 m (3,300 ft). Higher hay barns and chalets (*mayens*) sit at 1,500–1,600 m (4,900–5,200 ft). Summer pastures start around 2,000 m (6,600 ft).
- **Seasonal settlement.** Herds moved up in summer and down in winter (*transhumance*). This is very old in the Alps: archaeology traces it back to about 3000 BC, and written records mention summer pastures by 1204. In northern England and Scotland the summer huts used for the same purpose are called *shielings*.
- **The upper limit.** Permanent villages above about 2,000 m (6,600 ft) were very rare. Juf in Switzerland, at 2,126 m (6,975 ft), was settled by Walser colonists in 1292 and is usually called the highest permanent village in western Europe.
- **Passes** create towns at both ends (see the table above).

### Coasts

Fishing villages sit in every sheltered cove. Real ports need shelter plus a route inland, usually a river. On coasts with raiders, villages sit a few kilometres inland and uphill, with only boat sheds and a watchtower on the shore. In Sardinia, the village of Posada moved to the foot of its castle hill in the late 15th or early 16th century to escape coastal raids. On many Aegean islands, villages came back down to the shore only after the pirate threat ended, often in the 19th century.

### Marsh and fens

People lived **around** marshes, not in them. The fen edge was rich: fish, eels, reeds, peat, salt and summer grazing. Inside the marsh, settlements sat on islands of higher ground (Ely, Glastonbury) or on man-made mounds. In Friesland and Groningen alone there were about 1,200 *terpen* (dwelling mounds), the first built around 500 BC.

### Forest

In 1000–1300 Europe cleared huge areas of forest. Clearing land for farming was called **assarting** in England (from French *essarter*, to grub out). Clearings show up in place names (-ley, -field, -royd, -sart, -rode). In Germany, new forest villages were often laid out as **Waldhufendörfer** ("forest-strip villages"): a line of farms along a road or stream, each with a long strip of land running back into the forest. This form appeared around 1000 in the northern Black Forest and spread east with German settlers in the 12th–13th centuries.

**A "forest" was not always a forest.** A medieval English "forest" was a **legal** area for royal hunting, not always woodland. At its peak, in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, about a third of the land of southern England was royal forest. Villages existed inside it, but clearing land needed the king's permission, and people who cleared without it were fined or charged rent.

### Steppe and grassland

Nomads lived in camps, not villages. They often had fixed winter camps in sheltered river valleys and moved to open pastures in summer. Towns appeared only on big rivers and at the steppe edge, where farmers, nomads and merchants met. Sarai, the Golden Horde's capital, was founded by Batu Khan in the 1240s–1250s on the lower Volga and grew into a large city. Kiev stands on high river bluffs on the Dnieper, near the line where forest meets steppe.

### Desert

Water decides everything. Settlements are dots and lines: dots at oases, lines along wadis (dry riverbeds that flood sometimes) and along *qanats*. A *qanat* is a gently sloping tunnel that brings groundwater from the foot of the mountains to the fields. They are typical of Iran. Most Iranian qanats are under 5 km (3 mi) long, but some near Kerman measure about 70 km (43 mi). Desert cities such as Yazd and Kerman sit at the foot of mountains where qanats can be dug. Caravan stops sit one day's march apart, about 30–40 km (19–25 mi) (see [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)).

### Islands

An island usually has one main port on the sheltered side, facing the nearest mainland or trade route. Villages sit on fertile patches. On raided islands the main village (the *chora* on Greek islands) sits inland on a hill, often under a castle, with a small harbour below.

### Cold north

Growing grain is hard, so people lived on scattered farms with hay meadows and animals, along sheltered fjords and coastal strips. Towns were very few. Norway had only a handful, such as Bergen, Trondheim and Oslo, all on the coast or a fjord. Medieval Iceland had no towns at all: foreign traders came in summer to seasonal trading places such as Gásir. Norse Greenland had around 500 farms in its Eastern Settlement and maybe 100 in its Western Settlement, deep in the fjords. Estimates of its peak population vary: recent ones say about 2,000–3,000, while some older ones were higher.

> **Fantasy twist:** Dwarves change the mountain rule. A dwarf city can be large and deep inside a mountain, but it still needs food, so expect a busy human or dwarf market town at the mountain gate, on the nearest valley floor. Treat it like a pass town. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

> **Map tip:** Colour or shade your map by terrain type before placing settlements. Then use the table above as a checklist. Plains get dense villages. Uplands get a few hamlets in the valleys. Marshes get a ring of villages around the edge. Deserts get a few oasis dots joined by caravan routes. Leave the high mountains, deep forest and open steppe mostly empty.

## Patterns and spacing

### Nucleated, dispersed and linear

| Pattern | What it looks like | Where and why | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nucleated** | Houses grouped tightly around a church, green or crossroads; fields all around | Good arable land farmed in common open fields; or a need for defence | English Midlands "Central Province"; Italian *castelli*; much of northern France |
| **Dispersed** | Single farms and small hamlets scattered over the land | Uplands, pastoral farming, forest clearings, wet or broken land | Wales, Devon, Cornwall, the Alps, Scandinavia, much of south-east England |
| **Linear** | Houses in a line along a road, stream, dyke, spring line or shore | Each farm needs access to one line feature | German *Waldhufendorf* and *Straßendorf* ("street village"); marsh villages along dykes; spring-line rows |

England is a useful model: Roberts and Wrathmell (2000) mapped a central belt of nucleated villages (their "Central Province") running from the north-east down through the Midlands to the south coast, with more dispersed settlement on both sides. A real kingdom usually has more than one pattern. Village layouts in detail are in [Villages, Farms and the Countryside](06-villages-and-countryside.md).

### Central place theory, made simple

In 1933 the German geographer Walter Christaller studied towns in southern Germany. He noticed that towns of the same rank were spaced fairly evenly, and he built a model to explain why. The key ideas:

- **Central place:** a settlement that sells goods and services to the people around it.
- **Threshold:** the number of customers a service needs to survive. A smith needs few customers; a cathedral or university needs many.
- **Range:** how far people will travel for it. People walk a short way for bread and a long way for a lawyer.
- **Result:** many small centres close together, a few big centres far apart. Each market area is roughly a **hexagon**, because hexagons fill the land with no gaps.

In his main model (called **K = 3**), each step up the hierarchy is spaced √3 (about 1.7) times farther apart than the step below, and serves about 3 times the area.

| Christaller's level (1930s Germany, as his table is usually reproduced) | Spacing between places of this level | Model population (1930s) |
|---|---|---|
| Market hamlet (*Marktort*) | ~7 km (4.5 mi) | ~1,000 |
| Township centre (*Amtsort*) | ~12 km (7.5 mi) | ~2,000 |
| County seat (*Kreisstadt*) | ~21 km (13 mi) | ~4,000 |
| District city (*Bezirksstadt*) | ~36 km (22 mi) | ~10,000 |
| Small state capital (*Gaustadt*) | ~62 km (39 mi) | ~30,000 |
| Provincial capital (*Provinzstadt*) | ~108 km (67 mi) | ~100,000 |
| Regional capital (*Landstadt*) | ~186 km (116 mi) | ~500,000 |

**Careful:** These are model numbers for the 1930s. Medieval places at each level were much smaller (see [chapter 02](02-population-and-sizes.md)), but the **spacing pattern** is a good guide. The medieval English evidence (below) shows market places 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart, close to Christaller's lower levels.

Christaller had two other versions. In the **transport version (K = 4)**, smaller towns sit on the straight roads between bigger towns. In the **administrative version (K = 7)**, each big centre's territory fully contains its smaller centres, as in a county or diocese. Real landscapes mix all three. Outside Europe, G. William Skinner (1964) found a similar pattern in rural China: each basic market town served about 18 villages.

**What it looks like on a map:** Put a city in the middle. Around it, at roughly equal distances, put six towns. Between and around those, put market towns. Then **distort** the pattern: stretch it along rivers and roads, squeeze it together on rich land, spread it out on poor land, and break it at mountains and coasts. Real maps never show perfect hexagons, but you can usually see the idea underneath.

### How far apart: the numbers

These spacings come from the shared baseline used across this guide.

| Feature | Typical spacing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Villages, lowland | 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) | England c. 1300: one parish per ~14 km² (5.4 sq mi), about 4 km (2.5 mi) church to church. Upland parishes cover 50–100+ km² (20–40+ sq mi). |
| Market towns, legal rule | 6⅔ mi (10.7 km) | The lawyer Bracton (13th century): a new market should not be closer than this to an old one. It was rarely enforced. |
| Market towns, in practice | 9–16 km (6–10 mi) | 1,746 English markets were recorded by 1300 (one per 75 km² (29 sq mi), ~9 km (6 mi) apart), but many were tiny. In the late 16th century there were ~650 working markets in England and Wales, ~16 km (10 mi) apart. |
| Peasant walk to market | 6–10 km (4–7 mi) one way | There and back in one day, with time to trade. |
| Towns of 5,000+ | Flanders 35–45 km (22–28 mi); northern Italy 50–60 km (31–37 mi); France, Germany, Iberia 80–95 km (50–59 mi); Britain 130+ km (80+ mi); Poland 180–250 km (110–155 mi) | An upper bound derived from city data. |
| Cities of 10,000+ | Flanders and Italy 60–90 km (37–56 mi); France, Germany, Iberia 130–170 km (80–105 mi); Britain and Poland 260–400 km (160–250 mi) | Same basis. |

**How many in one area?** If places are spaced *d* apart in a rough hexagon pattern, each one "owns" about 0.87 × *d*² of land. Using that rule on the table above, here is what you would expect in a square of 100 × 100 km (62 × 62 mi), which is 10,000 km² (3,860 sq mi):

| Parish villages | Upland | 50–100+ km² | 100–200 |
| Market places | England at the c. 1300 peak (many tiny) | ~75 km² | ~130 |
| Market places | Busy markets only (England and Wales, late 1500s) | ~230 km² | ~45 |
| Towns of 5,000+ | Very urban (Flanders) | 1,100–1,750 km² | 6–9 |
| Towns of 5,000+ | Northern Italy | 2,200–3,100 km² | 3–5 |
| Towns of 5,000+ | France, Germany, Iberia | 5,500–7,800 km² | 1–2 |
| Towns of 5,000+ | Britain / Poland | 15,000+ / 28,000–54,000 km² | Under 1 / 0–1 |

### Chains along rivers, coasts and roads

Settlements line up along anything that carries traffic:
- **Rivers.** On the Rhine between Mainz and Koblenz (about 90 km (56 mi) by river), small towns follow each other every 5–20 km (3–12 mi): Bingen, Bacharach, Oberwesel, St Goar, Boppard. Many had toll castles above them.
- **Coasts.** On the southern Baltic, Hanseatic ports (members of the Hanse, a league of north German trading towns) such as Wismar, Rostock and Stralsund sit about 50–70 km (30–45 mi) apart in a straight line, each on its own bay or river mouth.
- **Roads.** On main roads, inns and stopping places appear every 15–30 km (9–19 mi), so a traveller always finds one within a day's travel. Roman road stations (*mansiones*) were about 25–30 km (16–19 mi) apart, and many later towns grew on them.

### Clusters and empty spaces

Settlement is never even. In England in 1086, Suffolk had about 23–25 people per km² (60–65 per sq mi), while much of the north and west had under 5 per km² (under 13 per sq mi). Good land is crowded; poor land is nearly empty. A map with evenly spaced dots looks false.

> **Map tip:** First decide which pattern each region has: nucleated, dispersed or linear. Then use the spacing table. Draw villages close together on good lowland, in lines along springs and rivers, and widely spaced in uplands. Put market towns about 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart, so that every village can walk to a market and back in one day. Space larger towns by the regional row in the table. Finally, check the "how many" table. If your 100 × 100 km (62 × 62 mi) farming region has only five villages, it is far too empty. If it has three cities, it is too full, unless it is your Flanders.

## How settlements change over time

A real map is a snapshot of a long story. Showing that story makes a map feel alive.

### Reuse of ancient and Roman sites

Old sites were reused because they already had walls, roads, a bridge or a bishop. In Italy, southern France and the Rhineland, most Roman cities stayed cities: Cologne, Trier, Lyon, Milan. In Britain many Roman towns shrank to almost nothing after 400 and later revived (London, York, Winchester). Some never came back:
- **Silchester** (Roman *Calleva*) was abandoned sometime between about 550 and 650 and never became a town again. A small medieval village grew up beside it and was deserted around 1400. Today its Roman walls still stand around open fields, with only a church and a farm inside.
- **Wroxeter** (Roman *Viroconium*, probably the fourth-largest town in Roman Britain) faded out in the late 7th or early 8th century. **Shrewsbury**, 8 km (5 mi) away in a strong river loop, became the main town of the area instead.

### Planted "new towns" and bastides

In the High Middle Ages lords founded towns on purpose, to earn rents, tolls and market dues and to control land. Planted towns are easy to spot: a **grid** or a regular street plan, a big **central market place**, and often a name with "New", "Ville-", "-ville", "Villa Nova" or "Bastide".

| Region | How many | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| England (1066–1370) | About 170 planted towns (Beresford 1967) | Bury St Edmunds (grid laid out c. 1080 by Abbot Baldwin; Domesday Book records 342 houses built on former ploughland by 1086); New Salisbury (1220s); New Winchelsea (charter 1281, laid out from 1288) |
| Wales | More than 80 (Beresford) | Edward I's castle towns in North Wales (see [chapter 04](04-military-sites.md)) |
| Gascony and southwest France | About 125 in English Gascony (Beresford); almost 700 **bastides** (planned new towns of southern France) between 1222 and 1372 | Cordes (1222, usually called the first); Monpazier (1284, Edward I); Villeneuve-sur-Lot |
| German lands | Central Europe had only a few hundred towns around 1100. Very many were founded from 1120 onwards, mostly in the next 250 years. Estimates vary with the definition of "town", but by the late Middle Ages there were several thousand, most of them small | Freiburg im Breisgau (1120); Bern (1191); Lübeck (founded 1143, refounded 1159) |

Many planted towns failed. A lord could grant a charter, but he could not force traders to come if the situation was poor.

### Castle-gate and monastic towns

Castles and abbeys were big customers. They needed food, crafts, labour and services, so a town often grew at the gate.
- **Castle-gate town:** Ludlow (castle begun c. 1085; a planned town laid out in the 12th century). See [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md).
- **Monastic town:** Bury St Edmunds; St Albans; Ely. See [Religious, Cultural, Legal and Ancient Sites](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md).

### Why some places grow and others shrink

| Cause | What happens | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| Silting | The harbour fills with mud; ships cannot reach the town | Bruges: the Zwin inlet began silting in the 13th century. By the 14th century big ships could no longer reach Damme and used Sluis instead. In the 15th century trade suffered badly, and it moved to Antwerp (the Hanseatic merchants moved their office there in 1520) |
| The sea | Storms and erosion destroy the town | Dunwich (England): storms in 1286–87, 1328, 1347 and 1362 destroyed much of it; a leading port became a village. Old Winchelsea was destroyed by storms in 1287–88 and rebuilt on a hill |
| A blocked river | A weir (a low dam across a river) or a bridge cuts the town off from the sea | Exeter: weirs built in the 1270s–80s and in 1317 cut off its port; ships had to unload at Topsham |
| A better site nearby | People move a short distance | Old Sarum to Salisbury (1220s) |
| A new route | A new bridge, road or pass shifts traffic | The first Schöllenen bridge (around 1220) opened the Gotthard pass and brought trade to central Switzerland |
| Plague and low prices | Too few people to farm all the land; the worst land is abandoned | The Black Death (1347–51) and later plagues |
| Conversion to pasture | Lords turn arable land into sheep runs | Many deserted villages in the English Midlands, mostly in the 15th century |
| Politics | A ruler moves the court, a bishop moves his see (the seat of his diocese), a war ruins a region | See [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md) |

### Deserted medieval villages

England has **more than 3,000** known deserted medieval villages, counting all dates and causes (estimates vary). Most were deserted between about 1350 and 1520, especially in the 15th century, when landlords turned arable land into sheep pasture. Others were lost to floods, sand, the sea, or later to parks around great houses. The plague usually weakened a village; sheep or a landlord finished it. The best-studied is **Wharram Percy** in Yorkshire, on the western edge of the chalk Wolds. It shrank after the Black Death, and its last villagers were evicted for sheep pasture in the early 1500s. Archaeologists excavated it every year from 1950 to 1990. Today such sites show as bumps in fields: house platforms, sunken lanes and a lonely church.

> **Later era (1500s+):** New planned towns appear again, now for war and for princely show: Valletta (Malta, 1566, after the Great Siege of 1565), Palmanova (Italy, 1593, a star-shaped fortress town), Freudenstadt (Germany, 1599), Charleville (France, 1606). Engineers also make new land and new routes. The Exeter Canal (1564–66) bypassed the blocking weirs and brought small ships back to the city. Ocean trade makes Atlantic ports such as Seville, Lisbon, Antwerp and then Amsterdam grow fast. Seville's river was hard for big ships, and silting made it worse by the 1620s, so more and more of the American trade moved downstream to Sanlúcar and the Bay of Cádiz. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Map tip:** Add history to your map. Mark a few ruins ("Old Sarum"-style hill forts above a newer valley town). Draw one or two planted towns with a grid plan and names like "Newbridge" or "Villeneuve". Leave a deserted village or two as a "ruined church" symbol in sheep country. If your map is set after a plague or war, show fewer villages on poor land and more sheep pasture.

## Where people do not build

Empty space is as realistic as settled space. Here is where medieval people avoided building, and what you find there instead.

| Place | Why people avoid it | What you find there instead |
|---|---|---|
| Active flood plain | Floods every winter | Hay meadows, mills at the edge, causeways |
| Fever marsh | Malaria; no firm ground | Fishing huts, salt pans, summer grazing; villages on the edge |
| High mountains (above ~2,000 m (6,600 ft) in the Alps) | Too cold for crops; snow for months | Summer huts and pastures only |
| Shady valley sides | Short growing season; long snow cover | Forest |
| Raided coasts | Pirates and slave raiders | Watchtowers, beacons, boat sheds; villages a few km inland |
| Royal forests and hunting parks | Laws limit clearing and building | Hunting lodges, keepers' houses, a few old villages |
| Poor soils (sandy heath, thin chalk, bare rock) | Crops fail | Sheep walks, rabbit warrens, a few hamlets |
| Contested borders | War and raids | Castles and empty "march" land (see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md)) |
| Deep old forest | Clearing takes generations | Charcoal burners, swineherds, hermits, slowly growing clearings |
| Desert away from water | No water at all | Nothing, except caravan stops at wells |
| Right next to an existing market | Competition and legal rules | Villages, but not another market town |

> **Rule of thumb:** On a realistic map, a lot of land is empty or only lightly used. Mountains, marsh cores, heaths, forests and borders should look thin.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Two changes. First, drainage begins to turn empty marshes and shallow lakes into farmland with new farms and villages (the Beemster lake in Holland, drained by 1612; the English Fens, 1630s–1650s). Second, Barbary corsair (North African pirate) raids make some Mediterranean coasts **more** dangerous: Genoa built about 90 watchtowers around the coast of Corsica between 1530 and 1620, and coastal villages stayed inland and uphill. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Monsters push people together. Where wolves, trolls or raiding beasts are real dangers, expect fewer lone farms, more walled villages, and settlements on defensible sites even in peaceful kingdoms. The "empty" lands become truly empty: no charcoal burners in the cursed forest. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

> **Map tip:** After you place settlements, look for "holes" in the pattern. Each hole should have a reason you could explain: marsh, mountain, forest, heath, a royal forest, a dangerous border. If a hole has no reason, add villages. If a crowded area sits on bad land, remove some.

## Placing settlements on your map: a method

Work from big to small, and from nature to people. Start with a finished terrain map: coasts, mountains, rivers, forests, marshes.

1. **Mark the water.** Draw all rivers. Decide which stretches are **navigable** (deep enough for boats) and where they stop: falls, rapids, shallows. Mark fords and the tidal limit on each major river.
2. **Mark the land quality.** Shade good farmland (river valleys, plains, gentle hills), pasture land (uplands, heath), marsh, forest, mountain and desert.
3. **Place the great city or cities.** Look for the best situation in the richest region: the lowest bridging point or estuary of a big navigable river, or a superb harbour. Remember that a city of 50,000+ must be fed by water.
4. **Place regional towns.** Put them at the next-best nodes: confluences, heads of navigation, gap towns, pass towns, harbours, crossings of major roads. Space them by the regional row in the spacing table. Cities of 10,000+ are 60–90 km (37–56 mi) apart in a very urban region, 130–170 km (80–105 mi) in an average one, and 260–400 km (160–250 mi) in a thinly urban one.
5. **Place market towns.** Fill the farmland with market towns about 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart, on rivers and road junctions where possible. Spread them more widely on poor land.
6. **Place villages.** Fill good land with villages 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) apart, on terraces, spring lines and land edges. In uplands, use hamlets and single farms in valleys. Leave marsh cores, mountains and heaths nearly empty.
7. **Add special sites.** Castles ([chapter 04](04-military-sites.md)), monasteries and shrines ([chapter 08](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md)), mines, salt works and other special towns ([chapter 07](07-industry-and-resources.md)). These are the main reasons for a settlement in an "odd" place.
8. **Connect them.** Draw roads and river routes ([chapter 05](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)). Small adjustments are fine: shift a village to the road, or a town to the bridge.
9. **Add time.** Mark ruins, deserted villages, moved towns and planted towns.
10. **Run the test below on every settlement.**

### The "why is it here?" test

Ask these questions about every dot. A **village** must pass 1–3. A **town** must also pass 4. A **city** must pass all six.

| # | Question | If the answer is "no" |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is there fresh water on or next to the site? | Move it to a river, stream, spring or lake shore. |
| 2 | Is there a mix of farmland, meadow, pasture and wood within a few km? | Move it to an edge between land types, or make it a special site (mine, monastery). |
| 3 | Is it safe from floods (on a terrace, rise or island)? | Move it up a few metres onto a terrace or rise. |
| 4 | Do routes meet here (crossing, confluence, junction, harbour, gap, pass)? | Make it a village, or move it to the crossing. |
| 5 | Is it on navigable water or the coast? | Make it a town under ~10,000 people. |
| 6 | Is it the best-connected place in a rich region? | Make it a regional town, not a great city. |

Also check:
- **Defence:** In a dangerous region, is there a hill, a river loop or a wall? If not, why not?
- **Spacing:** Is it too close to a place of the same rank? Two market towns 3 km (2 mi) apart compete; one should be a village.
- **Name:** Does the name fit the site (ford, bridge, mouth, hill)?

> **Fantasy twist:** A magical reason, such as a ley-line nexus, a dragon's peace treaty or a portal, can replace test 4 or 6. It cannot replace tests 1–3. Even a wizard's city needs water and food, or a very good explanation of where they come from.

> **Map tip:** Keep a short list next to your map: settlement name, rank, site type, and one-line situation ("Kingsford: town; ford and confluence; where the north road meets the Silverwater"). It takes a few minutes and catches most mistakes. More common errors are in [Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them](11-common-mistakes.md). The full workflow is in [Step-by-step](00-step-by-step.md).

## Quick summary

- Every settlement needs **water, food, fuel, safety, access and health**. Villages need the first three nearby; towns also need access; cities need access **by water**.
- Villages sit on **edges** between land types (valley and hill, fen and dry land, downland and clay), on **river terraces** above the flood plain, and along **spring lines**.
- **Site** (the spot) decides if a place survives. **Situation** (its place in the network) decides if it grows big.
- The best town sites are **lowest bridging points, heads of navigation, confluences, gaps, pass feet, sheltered harbours and estuaries**. On tidal coasts, big ports often sat 50–120 km (30–75 mi) up an estuary.
- In dangerous times and places, people choose **hills, river loops, islands and spurs**.
- Spacing: villages **1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi)**, market towns **9–16 km (6–10 mi)**, towns and cities much farther apart, depending on how urban the region is.
- Christaller's **central place** model: many small places close together, a few big ones far apart, in rough hexagons. Distort it along rivers and roads.
- Settlement is **clustered on good land and thin on bad land**. Empty areas need a reason, and so do crowded ones.
- Places change: Roman sites reused or abandoned, **planted towns** with grids, towns moved, harbours silted, and **more than 3,000 deserted villages** in England alone.
- Use the **"why is it here?" test** on every dot.
