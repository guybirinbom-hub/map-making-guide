## The numbers you will use most

> **Rule of thumb:** Almost everyone lives in villages. A "big city" in medieval Europe is anything above 10,000 people. A place of 50,000 is one of the great cities of the known world.

| What | Number |
|---|---|
| People per household (hearth) | 4.5–5 |
| Share of people living in the countryside | 85–95% |
| Good lowland at its peak | 30–50 people per km² (80–130 per sq mi) |
| Whole realm, uplands included | about 7–40 per km² (18–100 per sq mi); Europe c. 1300 about 20 per km² (52 per sq mi) |
| Land per person | about 1 ha (2.5 acres) of ploughland, or 2–3 ha (5–7 acres) of all land |
| Village | 75–500 people (usually 150–300); 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) apart in lowland |
| Small market town | 500–2,000 people; 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart |
| Town of 10,000 | needs farmland within 21–27 km (13–17 mi) on good land, or water transport |
| For every city of 10,000+ | about 10 towns, 50–100 small market towns and a few thousand villages |
| Capital of a strong kingdom | 1–2% of the realm's people; 3–7 times the second city |
| Walker / ox cart / army with baggage / royal messenger | 25–35 / 15–25 / 13–20 / 50–90 km a day (15–22 / 10–15 / 8–12 / 30–56 mi) |
| Transport cost, land : river : sea | about 8 : 4 : 1 (England c. 1300) |
| Inns on main roads | every 15–30 km (10–19 mi) |
| Working castles | 20–30 km (12–19 mi) apart in a peaceful core; 4–6 km (2.5–4 mi) on a contested border |
| Peacetime castle garrison | 5–20 men |
| Field army | 0.2–0.5% of the realm's people |
| Religious houses | about 1 per 150 km² (60 sq mi) |
| Cities of 100,000+ in Europe, the Middle East and North Africa | 4 (c. 1000), 8 (c. 1300), 8 (c. 1500) |

Source: [The settlement ladder](02-population-and-sizes.md#the-settlement-ladder), [How many of each kind](02-population-and-sizes.md#how-many-of-each-kind), [Counting the cities](02-population-and-sizes.md#counting-the-cities), [The rank-size rule and primate cities](02-population-and-sizes.md#the-rank-size-rule-and-primate-cities), [Population density](02-population-and-sizes.md#population-density), [Carrying capacity](02-population-and-sizes.md#carrying-capacity-how-much-land-feeds-people), [Feeding towns](02-population-and-sizes.md#feeding-towns-the-hinterland), [How far apart: the numbers](01-settlement-placement.md#how-far-apart-the-numbers), [Speed per day](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#speed-per-day), [Cost: why water beats land](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#cost-why-water-beats-land), [Inns, hospices and caravanserais](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#inns-hospices-and-caravanserais), [Borderlands and conquered land](04-military-sites.md#borderlands-and-conquered-land), [Garrisons and armies](04-military-sites.md#garrisons-and-armies), [How many of each, per area](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#how-many-of-each-per-area) and [Monasteries, friaries and military orders](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#monasteries-friaries-and-military-orders).

## Settlement tiers

> **Rule of thumb:** Make the label size follow the tier, not the story. To make a small place look important, give it a special icon (castle, cathedral, shrine), not a bigger label.

| Tier | People | Households | What it usually has | Symbol: continent / kingdom / local | Label |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Isolated farmstead | 5–15 | 1–2 | Own fields and pasture; no church | — / — / tiny dot | none |
| Hamlet | 10–75 | 2–15 | A few houses; a chapel at most; belongs to a village's parish | — / — / 2–5 small house marks | 6–7 pt italic, or none |
| Village | 75–500 (English lowland usually 150–300) | 15–100 | Parish church, manor house, mill, smithy, open fields, common pasture | — / tiny dot (notable villages only) / house marks around a church | 7–8 pt |
| Small market town | 500–2,000 | 100–400 | Weekly market, yearly fair, market place, burgage plots, inns, craftsmen, one parish church, often a hospital | — / small open circle / houses around a market square | 8–9 pt |
| Town | 2,000–10,000 | 400–2,000 | Usually walled; several parishes, guilds, friaries, hospitals, often a castle, a borough charter, its own council | dot (important ones only) / circle with a ring (battlements on the ring if walled) / outline with wall and gates | 9–10 pt |
| City | 10,000–50,000 | 2,000–10,000 | Cathedral, many parishes and friaries, suburbs, guilds, law courts, mint; sometimes a university | dot or circle / circle with a thick battlemented ring (a larger walled symbol) / full outline | 11–12 pt, bold or small capitals |
| Great city | 50,000+ | 10,000+ | Capital or major port; several markets, university, foreign merchants' quarters, organised water supply; grain by river or sea | large circle with a ring / large walled symbol / full outline with suburbs | 13–14 pt CAPITALS |

A capital takes a filled star in place of its tier symbol (see the [master legend](#master-legend-and-label-hierarchy)).

Source: [The settlement ladder](02-population-and-sizes.md#the-settlement-ladder) and [Map symbols and labels](02-population-and-sizes.md#map-symbols-and-labels).

**How big to draw it** (towns held about 100–200 people per hectare, or 40–80 per acre):

| Place | People | Built-up area | Width if roughly round |
|---|---|---|---|
| Village | 150–300 | 5–15 ha (12–37 acres) | 250–450 m (0.15–0.3 mi) |
| Small market town | 500–2,000 | 5–20 ha (12–50 acres) | 250–500 m (0.15–0.3 mi) |
| Town | 2,000–10,000 | 20–80 ha (50–200 acres) | 0.5–1 km (0.3–0.6 mi) |
| City | 10,000–50,000 | 80–300 ha (200–740 acres) | 1–2 km (0.6–1.2 mi) |
| Great city | 50,000–200,000 | 300–1,000+ ha (740–2,500+ acres) | 2–4 km (1.2–2.5 mi) |

Source: [How big to draw it](02-population-and-sizes.md#how-big-to-draw-it).

**Regional exceptions:**

| Region | What is different |
|---|---|
| Italy | *Castelli* (fortified hilltop villages) of 200–1,000 people. Hundreds of small bishoprics, so a cathedral does not prove a big city. |
| Southern Spain, Sicily, Apulia | *Agro-towns* of 2,000–10,000 people who are mostly farmers: big in people, not real towns in function. |
| Germany and the Low Countries | Many chartered "legal towns" had fewer than 2,000 people. |
| England | Only 17 bishoprics, so a cathedral does mean an important city. |
| Byzantine lands | Most villages well under 100 households (estimate). |

Source: [The settlement ladder](02-population-and-sizes.md#the-settlement-ladder).

> **Later era (1500s+):** The ladder stays the same, but the top grows taller: by 1600 Istanbul had perhaps 400,000–700,000 people, and Paris, Naples and London 200,000–300,000 each. See [Later-era changes](#later-era-changes-15001650).

> **Fantasy twist:** Where monsters roam the countryside, shift hamlet people into fortified villages of 300–1,000, as Italy did in its *incastellamento*. See [Fantasy adjustments](#fantasy-adjustments).

> **Map tip:** On a kingdom map at 1 cm = 10 km (about 1 inch = 16 mi), even a great city is only 2–4 mm across. Use symbols at that scale and draw real outlines only on local maps (1 cm = 1 km, about 1 inch = 1.6 mi, or larger).

## Population density by land type

> **Rule of thumb:** Above about 50 people per km² (130 per sq mi), a region needs towns, specialised commercial farming or imported grain.

| Land type | per km² | per sq mi | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rich lowland, peak times | 30–50 | 80–130 | Northern Italy 34–40 c. 1300; England's whole-country average already ~36 in 1290 |
| Very urban, rich lowland | 50–75 | 130–190 | Flanders and Holland in the 15th century (estimate) |
| Average mixed farmland | 15–30 | 40–80 | Suffolk ~23–25 in 1086; England ~19 in 1377 |
| Poorer land, young kingdoms | 7–15 | 18–40 | Hungary ~7 (c. 1200), ~9–11 (c. 1495); Crown of Poland 8.6 (1370), 13 (1500) |
| Hill country (farmed valleys with upland grazing), peak times | 8–15 | 21–39 | Cumberland and Westmorland about 15–17 in 1290 as whole counties (valleys and coastal plain included); about 6–7 in 1377 |
| High uplands, moors, mountains | 2–5 | 5–13 | Scottish Highlands, high Alps, Pyrenees, northern Pennines (estimate) |
| Forest or steppe frontier | 1–5 | 3–13 | Lithuania, the Rus' forest zone (estimate) |
| Shifting (swidden) farming in tropical forest | about 6 on average; 12–35 in busier districts; at most about 55–56 | about 14 on average; 30–90; at most about 145 | 1950s world average about 6; modern Southeast Asia from 12 in northern Laos to 35 in northern Thailand |
| Steppe nomads | under 1–2 | under 3–5 | Herders need huge pastures |
| Desert | close to 0 | close to 0 | Except oases and irrigated valleys |
| Irrigated river valley | 100+ on the farmed strip | 260+ | Egypt c. 1340 |
| Wet-rice or lake-garden basin (outside Europe) | 80–125 over a region; 230–300 in a city-and-rice-field zone | 210–325; 600–780 | Yangzi delta ~80 (1102); Valley of Mexico ~100–125 (1519); Greater Angkor ~230–300 (13th century) |

These are typical values, not limits. Exceptional districts went far above them: Norfolk, a mostly rural county, had about 487,000 people in 1290 (a model estimate) on about 5,400 km² (2,100 sq mi), roughly 90 per km² (230 per sq mi). It still fits the rule of thumb: it had very intensive farming, one of England's largest cities (Norwich) and two busy ports (Lynn and Yarmouth). Keep such a figure for one exceptional district, not a whole realm.

Source: [By type of land](02-population-and-sizes.md#by-type-of-land) (including Norfolk, from [Broadberry, Campbell & van Leeuwen 2011](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/seminars/seminars/conferences/venice3/programme/english_medieval_population.pdf), Table 8B: 486,920 people in 1290) and [Density beyond Europe](02-population-and-sizes.md#density-beyond-europe); on Norfolk's farming, Campbell, "Agricultural progress in medieval England: some evidence from eastern Norfolk", *Economic History Review* 36 (1983).

**Quick values for the population calculator** (people = area × density):

| Land | per km² | per sq mi |
|---|---|---|
| Rich lowland | 30–40 | about 78–104 |
| Average land | 15–25 | about 39–65 |
| Hill country | 8–15 | about 21–39 |
| High uplands, mountains, forest and marsh | 2–5 | about 5–13 |
| Desert | 0 | 0 |

A hex's area is about 0.866 × (width across the flat sides)²: a 30 km hex is about 780 km², a 30 mi hex about 780 sq mi. Multiply per km² by 2.59 to get per sq mi.

Source: [Step-by-step population calculator](02-population-and-sizes.md#step-by-step-population-calculator).

**Food: how far a town's farmland reaches** (assumes 30 people per km² on good land, 10 on marginal land; real radii are bigger where towns compete):

| Town size | Overland radius, good lowland | Overland radius, marginal land | With water transport |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 7–8 km (4–5 mi) | ~18 km (11 mi) | Not needed |
| 10,000 | 21–27 km (13–17 mi) | ~55 km (35 mi) | Food can come 2–10 times farther along a river or coast |
| 50,000 | 46–59 km (29–37 mi) | ~125 km (78 mi) | Must have river or sea supply |

Source: [Feeding towns: the hinterland](02-population-and-sizes.md#feeding-towns-the-hinterland).

> **Map tip:** Before placing villages, lightly shade the map into crowded lowland, ordinary farmland, thin hill country and empty land. Then draw a faint circle at the overland radius around each town. If two circles overlap heavily, shrink one town or put it on water. Do both in pencil or on a layer you hide later, and remove them from the finished map: in the [master legend](#master-legend-and-label-hierarchy) a soft tint means a language area and a ring around a place belongs to the town symbol.

## Urbanization and how many of each tier

> **Rule of thumb:** For every city of 10,000+, expect about 10 towns, about 50–100 small market towns and a few thousand villages.

| Region | c. 1300 | c. 1500 |
|---|---|---|
| Europe | ~5% in cities of 10k+; ~9.5% in all towns | 5.6% in 10k+ |
| England | ~2% in 10k+ (only London); ~10% in towns of 2k+; 15–20% counting small boroughs | 3.1% in 10k+ |
| France | ~3–4% in 10k+; ~7–9% in 2k+ | ~4.2% in 10k+ |
| Low Countries | Flanders ~33–40% in towns of all sizes | Belgium 21%, Netherlands 16% in 10k+; Holland ~45% urban |
| Northern Italy | ~18% in 10k+ (Centre-North); ~20% in 5k+ | ~15–16% in 10k+ |
| Iberia | ~8–10% in 10k+ (inflated by agro-towns) | Spain ~6%, Portugal ~3% in 10k+ |
| Germany | ~2–3% in 10k+ | 3.2% in 10k+ |
| Denmark | ~10% in all towns | ~14% in all towns |
| Poland, Hungary | under 2% in 10k+ | ~1–2% in 10k+ |
| Arab Middle East and North Africa | ~7% in 10k+ | ~8% in 10k+ |

Source: [Urbanization by region](02-population-and-sizes.md#urbanization-by-region).

**Urban profiles** (choose one per realm):

| Share of people | Frontier (Poland, Hungary, Scotland, Scandinavia) | Average (England, France, Germany c. 1300) | Highly urban (Flanders, Lombardy, Tuscany) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cities 10,000+ | ~1% | ~3% | ~15% |
| Towns 2,000–10,000 | ~3% | ~5% | ~12% |
| Small market towns 500–2,000 | ~4% | ~6% | ~8% |
| Villages, hamlets, farms | ~92% | ~86% | ~65% |

| Per 1 million people | Frontier | Average | Highly urban |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cities 10,000+ | 0–1 (largest often 8–15k) | 1–2 (largest 15–30k) | 5–10 |
| Towns 2,000–10,000 | 8–12 | 12–18 | 30–40 |
| Small market towns | 40–60 | 60–90 | 80–100 |
| Villages | 3,000–4,500, plus many hamlets and farms | 2,500–3,500 | 2,000–2,500 |

To turn shares into places, divide by a typical size: towns about 3,000–4,000, market towns about 800, villages about 250 in lowland and 100–150 in hill country. The capital of a strong kingdom holds about 1–2% of all its people; in a fragmented realm the largest city holds about 0.5% or less.

Source: [Step-by-step population calculator](02-population-and-sizes.md#step-by-step-population-calculator).

**A model kingdom: England c. 1300** (4.75 million people on 130,000 km², 50,000 sq mi):

| Tier | Approximate number | One per … people |
|---|---|---|
| Parishes (each with a village church) | ~9,500 | ~500 |
| Places with a market grant | ~1,750 (many only village markets) | ~2,700 |
| Boroughs (places with town status) | about 600, many tiny | ~8,000 |
| Towns of 2,000–10,000 | roughly 50–60 (estimate) | ~80,000–100,000 |
| Cities of 10,000+ | ~6: London (60–100k) plus about five of 10–15k | ~800,000 |

Source: [How many of each kind](02-population-and-sizes.md#how-many-of-each-kind) and [The rank-size rule and primate cities](02-population-and-sizes.md#the-rank-size-rule-and-primate-cities).

> **Map tip:** On a kingdom map you do not draw every village. Draw the capital, the cities, all the towns and the 30–40 most important market towns, then add villages as texture so the viewer feels the crowding. Count your symbols at the end: far more villages than towns, far more towns than cities.

## Spacing

> **Rule of thumb:** Many small places close together, a few big places far apart. Stretch the pattern along rivers and roads, squeeze it on rich land and spread it on poor land.

| Feature | Typical spacing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Villages, lowland | 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) | One parish per ~14 km² (5.4 sq mi); about 4 km (2.5 mi) church to church |
| Parish churches in hills | about 7–10 km (4–6 mi), more in the emptiest uplands | Upland parishes cover 50–100+ km² (20–40+ sq mi) |
| Peasant walk to market | 6–10 km (4–6 mi) one way | There and back in one day |
| Market towns, legal rule | 6⅔ mi (10.7 km) | Bracton (13th century); rarely enforced |
| Market towns, in practice | 9–16 km (6–10 mi); up to about 25 km (15 mi) in a thinly settled realm | 1,746 English markets by 1300; ~650 working markets in the late 1500s |
| Towns of 5,000+ | Flanders 35–45 km (22–28 mi); northern Italy 50–60 km (31–37 mi); France, Germany, Iberia 80–95 km (50–59 mi); Britain 130+ km (80+ mi); Poland 180–250 km (110–155 mi) | An upper bound from city data |
| Cities of 10,000+ | Flanders and Italy 60–90 km (37–56 mi); France, Germany, Iberia 130–170 km (80–105 mi); Britain and Poland 260–400 km (160–250 mi) | Same basis |
| Rival city-states | 50–90 km (30–55 mi) | Each territory 1–3 days across |
| Inns and stops on main roads | 15–30 km (10–19 mi) | Roman *mansiones* 25–30 km (16–19 mi); pilgrim stages about 20 km (12 mi) |
| Caravanserais | 30–40 km (19–25 mi) on plains and desert; 10 km (6 mi) or less in mountains | One day's stage |
| Bridge towns on a settled lowland river | about 20 km (12 mi) of river; gaps 10–35 km (6–22 mi) | Thames c. 1300: one bridge every 20–22 km (12–14 mi) |
| Head ports | about 1 per 100–150 km (60–95 mi) of coast | 15 for all of late medieval England |
| Galley watering ports | 100–300 km (60–190 mi) | Venice–Jaffa stops 100–450 km (60–280 mi) apart |
| Main roads on a kingdom map | about 30 km per 1,000 km² (about 48 mi per 1,000 sq mi) | From the Gough Map of Britain |
| Rhine toll stations, Mainz–Cologne, 1250 | about 15 km (9 mi) | At least 5 km (3 mi) apart |
| Active castles, England and Wales after 1066 | one per 250–300 km² (about 95–115 sq mi); about 16–17 km (10–11 mi) apart | 500–600 in use at once |
| Working castles, peaceful core | 20–30 km (12–19 mi) (estimate) | A castle per county town plus a few baronial seats |
| Castle sites, march or contested border | 4–6 km (2.5–4 mi) | Welsh March, 1070s–1280s |
| Castles in a conquest chain | 20–40 km (12–25 mi), one day's march | Each reachable by water |
| Beacons | about 5–20 km (3–12 mi) in hilly country; 55–100 km (35–60 mi) between high peaks | Estimates |
| Religious houses | about 1 per 150 km² (60 sq mi) | Monks in valleys, friars in towns |
| Leper houses | 1–2 km (about 1 mi) outside each town of 2,000+ | On a main road |
| Mills on a good stream | every 1–2 km (0.6–1.2 mi) | About one mill per village |
| Mining towns in one district | 10–40 km (6–25 mi) | 2–7 towns plus a mint town |
| Champagne fair towns | 45–65 km (28–40 mi) | Two or three days by road |

Source: [How far apart: the numbers](01-settlement-placement.md#how-far-apart-the-numbers), [Parish churches and chapels](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#parish-churches-and-chapels), [Inns, hospices and caravanserais](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#inns-hospices-and-caravanserais), [How often is a river bridged?](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#how-often-is-a-river-bridged-the-thames-example), [Port ranks](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#port-ranks-how-many-ports-on-a-coast), [Coastal hopping and open sea](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#coastal-hopping-and-open-sea), [How much main road to draw](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#how-much-main-road-to-draw), [Tolls and customs](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#tolls-and-customs), [How many castles and how far apart](04-military-sites.md#how-many-castles-and-how-far-apart), [Watchtowers, beacons and coastal warning](04-military-sites.md#watchtowers-beacons-and-coastal-warning), [Monasteries, friaries and military orders](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#monasteries-friaries-and-military-orders), [Mills and water management](06-villages-and-countryside.md#mills-and-water-management), [Mining districts on the map](07-industry-and-resources.md#mining-districts-on-the-map), [Fair towns](07-industry-and-resources.md#fair-towns), [Drawing the unusual kinds](03-capitals-and-borders.md#drawing-the-unusual-kinds).

> **Later era (1500s+):** Add post stations every 20–40 km (12–25 mi) on main roads and, on raided coasts, towers 3–12 km (2–7 mi) apart, each in sight of the next. > **Map tip:** If two market towns are only 3 km (2 mi) apart, make one of them a village. If two towns on a road are more than 40 km (25 mi) apart, add a village or an inn between them.

## Travel per day and transport costs

> **Rule of thumb:** Over land a traveller covers about 30 km (19 mi) a day and a loaded ox cart about 20 km (12 mi). By water, goods move at least as fast for a small part of the cost.

| Mode | km per day | mi per day | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walking traveller | 25–35 | 15–22 | Pilgrim stages were shorter: about 20 km (12 mi) |
| Peasant to market | 6–10 one way | 4–6 | Out, trade and home in one day |
| Ox cart | 15–25 | 10–15 | Slow, strong, cheap to feed |
| Horse cart or wagon | 30–40 | 20–25 | Horses can double the speed of oxen |
| Pack horse or mule train | 20–40 | 12–25 | Loads: horse 100–120 kg, mule 150–180 kg, donkey 80–100 kg |
| Camel caravan | 25–40 | 15–25 | About 230 kg per camel |
| Cattle or sheep drove | 16–30 | 10–20 | About 3 km/h (2 mph), grazing on the way; mostly 1700s–1800s evidence |
| Army with baggage | 13–20 | 8–12 | — |
| Army living off the land | 10–26 | 6–16 | — |
| Forced march, a few days only | 30–55 | 20–35 | — |
| Mounted force with spare horses | 55–65 | 35–40 | — |
| Rider, many days | 30–50 | 20–30 | — |
| Royal messenger with fresh horses | 50–90 | 30–56 | 30–40 mi normal, 52–56 mi when urgent |
| Inca relay runners | 240–300 | 150–190 | No horses or wheels |
| River boat, downstream | 40–100 | 25–60 | Estimate; daylight travel |

> **Map tip:** Write travel days on long routes ("12 days to the capital"). That is how travellers thought, and it shows at once if your map is too big or too small for the story.

## Political units and sizes

> **Rule of thumb:** Kingdom > duchy > county > barony > manor, each several to a few dozen times smaller than the one above. But medieval units overlapped and were scattered: do not expect a neat pyramid.

| Unit | Typical area | Typical population (c. 1300) | Seat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Empire | 0.8–1.7 million km² (300,000–650,000 sq mi) | 5–15 million | One great capital, or none (Holy Roman Empire) |
| Kingdom | 10,000–450,000 km² (4,000–175,000 sq mi) | 0.1–17 million | Capital or travelling court |
| Duchy or principality | 10,000–50,000 km² (4,000–19,000 sq mi) | 0.5–2 million (estimates) | Ducal city with castle, cathedral and court |
| County (shire) | 1,000–10,000 km² (400–4,000 sq mi); England averaged ~3,300 km² | 20,000–300,000; England averaged ~120,000 | County town with castle, sheriff and court |
| Hundred or wapentake | ~130–150 km² (50–60 sq mi) in 1086; ~150–200 km² (60–80 sq mi) by 1316 | 2,000–10,000 | Open-air meeting place: mound, ford, crossroads, stone |
| Barony, lordship | a few manors to 100+, often scattered over several counties | — | *Caput* (head manor), usually with a castle |
| Knight's fee | 1–2 manors; no fixed size | — | Manor house |
| Manor | 2–10 km² | 50–500 | Manor house, often moated |
| Parish | ~14 km² in lowland; 50–100+ km² in uplands | 75–500 | Parish church |

Source: [The units, top to bottom](03-capitals-and-borders.md#the-units-top-to-bottom).

**How many units:** England c. 1300 had 1 kingdom, 39 shires, about 630 hundreds (812 traceable in 1086), ~9,500 parishes and 5,000–6,000 knights' fees. France c. 1200 had the royal domain plus 6 great lay fiefs. Hungary had about 70 counties in the 15th century. The Holy Roman Empire in 1521 had about 400 imperial estates. Song China had about 1,230 counties, each about 2,000–2,500 km² (770–970 sq mi), with seats about 45–55 km (28–34 mi) apart. Europe c. 1500 had roughly 500 political units.

Source: [How many units in one kingdom?](03-capitals-and-borders.md#how-many-units-in-one-kingdom), [A different model: the bureaucratic empire](03-capitals-and-borders.md#a-different-model-the-bureaucratic-empire-china), [Realistic realm sizes](03-capitals-and-borders.md#realistic-realm-sizes).

| Poland | ~240,000 km² (1370) | ~2 million | 6–10 days |

Source: [Realistic realm sizes](03-capitals-and-borders.md#realistic-realm-sizes) and [Realm populations and areas](02-population-and-sizes.md#realm-populations-and-areas).

**Kinds of state and how they look:**

| Kind | How it looks on the map |

On a continent map use at least three kinds of state: one or two feudal kingdoms, one fragmented zone and one unusual kind.

## Capitals checklist

> **Rule of thumb:** A capital sits in the richest, most loyal region of the realm, on navigable water, at a place that is easy to defend. Centrality matters less than you think.

| # | Check | Rule and numbers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rich core region | The court, officials and garrison must be fed from the ruler's own estates |
| 2 | River or sea access | A capital grows into a big city, and big cities need grain by water |
| 3 | Defensible core site | Hill, ridge, island or river loop for the palace, treasure and archives |
| 4 | Loyal land | In the dynasty's home region, which may be off-centre (Paris, London, Kraków) |
| 5 | Legitimacy | An old sacred or royal site: coronation church, saint's tomb |
| 6 | Type | Central, core-region, or forward (moved toward a frontier the ruler is pushing) |
| 7 | Size | Strong kingdom: 1–2% of the realm's people and 3–7 times the second city. Loose realm: the largest city holds about 0.5% or less, and may not be the capital |
| 8 | Fixed or travelling | Before about 1200 most western courts travelled: draw 5–15 royal residences about a day apart (about 30 km, 19 mi) |
| 9 | Inside the capital | Palace or castle, cathedral or great abbey, treasury, writing office and archives, law courts, mint, later a university |
| 10 | Palace and merchant city | Often two linked parts, such as Westminster about 3 km (2 mi) from London |
| 11 | Royal landscape | 3–6 royal residences within about 60 km (37 mi); hunting forests 10–100 km (6–60 mi) away; burial church 0–20 km (0–12 mi); a coronation town 50–150 km (30–90 mi) away if you want one |
| 12 | Mints | Strong kingdom: the capital, 1–3 church or regional centres and one at each big silver mine. Loose realm: a mint in every princely seat |
| 13 | University | One or two per kingdom of 2–5 million, in the capital and the oldest cathedral city |
| 14 | Reach | Anything beyond about 12 days' messenger travel (roughly 700 km or 430 mi by road) needs a viceroy or becomes semi-independent |

Source: [What makes a capital](03-capitals-and-borders.md#what-makes-a-capital), [Travelling courts and fixed capitals](03-capitals-and-borders.md#travelling-courts-and-fixed-capitals), [What is in and around a capital](03-capitals-and-borders.md#what-is-in-and-around-a-capital), [How many mints?](03-capitals-and-borders.md#how-many-mints), [The rank-size rule and primate cities](02-population-and-sizes.md#the-rank-size-rule-and-primate-cities), [Universities and schools](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#universities-and-schools).

> **Later era (1500s+):** Fixed capitals win everywhere (Madrid from 1561), and some capitals grow to 5–10 times the second city. See [Why capitals grow so much](09-later-era-1500-1650.md#why-capitals-grow-so-much).

> **Fantasy twist:** A capital chosen for magic (a ley-line crossing, a dragon-proof mountain) still needs food, water transport and loyal land, unless you show how magic replaces them.

> **Map tip:** In a strong kingdom, make the capital the biggest city on the map. In a loose realm, put the court in a middling town and let a trading port or cloth city be bigger: the contrast tells the viewer how the realm works.

## Fortifications by date and where they go

> **Rule of thumb:** Every fortification controls something: a road, a crossing, a town, a border, an estate, a refuge or a lookout. If you cannot name it, move or delete the fort.

| Type | Dates | What it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ringwork | 10th–12th centuries | Earth bank and ditch with a timber fence; no mound | Early castles; common in south-west England and south Wales after 1066 |
| Motte-and-bailey | From c. 1000 (Loire, Normandy); England after 1066 | A motte (man-made earth mound) with a timber tower, and a bailey (fenced yard) | Beside towns, villages and crossings; about 80% of Norman castles in England |
| Stone keep | From c. 950 in France; England from the 1070s | Massive stone tower; about ten years to build | Royal and great baronial castles |
| Shell keep | Mostly 11th–12th centuries | Stone ring wall on top of a motte | Rebuilt mottes |
| Curtain-wall castle | Late 12th–13th centuries | Strong wall with flanking towers | Important royal and baronial sites |
| Concentric castle | Byzantine 11th century; crusader from the 1160s; Wales 1268–1295 | Two or more rings of walls | Great royal and crusader fortresses on conquest frontiers |
| Tower house | 14th–17th centuries | Tall stone house of 3–6 storeys | Raided borderlands: Ireland over 3,000, Scotland about 800, England over 250 |
| Peel tower and bastle house | Peels 14th–17th; bastles mostly 16th–17th centuries | Small border tower; fortified farmhouse | Anglo-Scottish border |
| Fortified manor house | 13th–15th centuries | Moat, gatehouse, battlements; stops a raid, not an army | Lowland estates; about 460 English licences to crenellate |
| Town walls and gates | All periods; big wave 12th–14th centuries | Wall or bank and ditch with gates | Cities, regional capitals, frontier towns, raided ports; English murage tolls from 1220 |
| Citadel | All periods | Fortress at the edge of a city, watching it too | Capitals and big or rebellious cities |
| Fortified bridge | 12th–14th centuries | Bridge with a gate tower | Main-road crossings; collects tolls |
| Fortified church | 13th–17th centuries | Thick walls, strong tower, often a ring wall | Raided villages (Transylvania, Slovenia) |
| Reused hillfort or Roman fort | Whenever handy | New castle inside old walls | Old Sarum, Pevensey, Portchester |
| Crusader castle | 1100–1291 | Large stone castles on spurs and hills | Hostile frontier, few defenders |
| Islamic *ribat* | 8th–11th centuries | Fortified lodge with a signal tower | Chains along coasts and borders |
| Military-order castle | 12th–15th centuries | Castle and monastery in one | Crusading frontiers (Malbork, Calatrava) |
| Siege castle | All periods | Fort built to block an enemy castle or town | 180–270 m (200–300 yd) from the target, just beyond bow range |
| Watchtower and beacon | All periods | A handful of men who see, signal and run | Passes, fords, wells, hilltops, headlands |

Source: [Types of fortification](04-military-sites.md#types-of-fortification).

**Where to put a castle:**

| Site | Why it works |
|---|---|
| River crossing (ford or bridge) | Every road and every boat must pass |
| Mountain pass or gap | The only easy way between two regions |
| Spur or promontory | Cliffs on two or three sides; one ditch across the neck |
| River bend (meander) | Water on three sides |
| Confluence | Controls traffic on both rivers |
| Island or lake shore | Water as a moat; controls the river or lakeside road |
| Coastal headland or harbour mouth | Supply by ship; watches the harbour |
| Hill above a town | Protects the town and watches it |
| Flat wet lowland | Dig a moat or flood the ground (a "water castle") |

In the German lands about two-thirds of castles stood on high ground; in England most stood on low ground beside villages and towns.

Source: [Where to put a castle](04-military-sites.md#where-to-put-a-castle).

**How many to draw, per 1,000 km² (about 390 sq mi), c. 1300:**

| Zone | All sites ever built | Active castles | Kingdom map | Local map |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peaceful lowland core | 4–8 | 1–3 | County-town castle plus 1 major baronial castle per 2,000–3,000 km² | All active castles plus ruins |
| Average (England and Wales) | ~12 | ~4 | Royal and major castles, about 1 per 1,000 km² | All |
| Hilly land with many small lords (German type) | ~25 | ~8–12 (estimate) | Major and toll castles | All, on spurs and river cliffs |
| March or contested border | 35–60 | 12–20 | A castle-studded band | All, including mottes and towers |
| Raided border, late Middle Ages | 8–10 castles plus many tower houses | Most of them | Castles and the main tower houses | Every tower house and bastle |
| Thin conquered frontier (Prussia type) | 1–3 | 1–3 | All, each paired with a town | All |

On a continent map, show only about 1 fortress per 20,000–50,000 km² (8,000–19,000 sq mi). England and Wales had 1,761 castle sites in all, but only 500–600 in use at once; draw one or two ruins or earthworks for every active castle in long-settled land.

Source: [How many to draw](04-military-sites.md#how-many-to-draw), [Counts and densities](04-military-sites.md#counts-and-densities), [Ruined and active castles](04-military-sites.md#ruined-and-active-castles).

**Garrisons and armies:**

| Situation | People |
|---|---|
| Peacetime caretaker garrison | 5–20 |
| New frontier castle in peacetime | 30–60 (Harlech 1284: 36) |
| Small castle under siege | 20–150 |

## Trade goods and route types

> **Rule of thumb:** The higher the value per kilogram, the farther a good can travel over land. Cheap, heavy goods stay local unless they can float.

| Good | Value per weight | How far over land | How far by water |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grain, flour | Very low | Usually under 30–50 km (19–31 mi); routine sales within about 17 km (10 mi) | Hundreds of km by river and sea |
| Firewood, timber, stone, brick | Very low | A few km | Far by water |
| Salt | Low | About 100 km (62 mi) or more on "salt roads" | Across Europe |
| Wine | Medium | Short distances | Very far |
| Salted herring, stockfish | Medium | Short to medium | Continent-wide |
| Wool | Medium to high | By pack horse and cart to ports | England to Flanders and Italy |
| Metals (copper, tin, silver, iron) | Medium to high | Long distances | Long distances |
| Cloth, furs, wax, amber | High | Any or long distances | Any or long distances |
| Spices, silk, dyes, gems, sugar | Very high | Across continents | Across oceans |
| Live animals | Walk themselves | Hundreds of km along drove roads | — |

Source: [Bulk goods and luxury goods](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#bulk-goods-and-luxury-goods).

**Route types:**

| Route | What it is | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Old Roman road | Straight, engineered, often decayed; still used | Former Roman lands |
| Royal highway | Main route between cities under the ruler's protection | Between major towns |
| Local road or lane | Village to market town, village to village | Everywhere |
| Hollow way | Lane worn below the land, up to 5 m (16 ft) deep | Soft rock on slopes near towns and fords |
| Causeway | Raised road across marsh or flood plain | Fens, flood plains, bridge approaches |
| Pack-horse track or bridle way | Narrow track for animals, not carts | Hills and mountains |
| Drove road | Wide grassy route, 12–27 m (40–90 ft) wide, halts about 16 km (10 mi) apart | From uplands to towns and fairs |
| Caravan route | Track from well to well and caravanserai to caravanserai | Desert and steppe |
| Navigable river | Highway with a one-way discount: fast downstream, towed upstream | Up to the head of navigation |
| Portage | Boats or cargo carried overland between two rivers or round rapids | Watersheds, rapids |
| Sea lane | Coast-hugging route with short open-sea crossings | Coasts and island chains |

As a rough guide, a village has 2–3 roads, a market town 3–5 and a city 5–8 or more. Each mountain range gets 1–3 main passes and 2–5 minor ones.

Source: [Kinds of roads](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#kinds-of-roads), [Drove roads](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#drove-roads), [Mountain passes](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#mountain-passes), [Which places become hubs?](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#which-places-become-hubs).

**Trade nodes, from small to great:**

| Node | How often | How many |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly market | Weekly | Market towns 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart |
| Yearly fair | Once a year, days to weeks | Many market towns had one |
| International fair cycle | A cycle through the year | One or two fair regions per continent at a time |
| Staple town | Permanent | One per river stretch or export trade |
| Entrepot (great storage-and-reshipping port) | Permanent | Only a handful in all of Europe and the Mediterranean c. 1300 |
| Merchant colony (*Kontor*, *fondaco*) | Permanent | One per foreign nation in each big port |

Source: [Trade nodes: markets, fairs, staples and entrepots](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#trade-nodes-markets-fairs-staples-and-entrepots).

**Ships, ports and seasons:**

| Item | Number |
|---|---|
| Cog (North Sea and Baltic, 12th–14th centuries) | 30–200 t; the Bremen cog (1380) about 90–130 t; about 1 sailor per 10 t |
| Merchant great galley (Mediterranean, c. 1290s–1530s) | about 140–250 t of cargo; up to about 250 crew |
| Caravel (15th century) | 50–60 t |
| Head ports | 15 for all of England; about 1 per 100–150 km (60–95 mi) of coast; several member ports under each; landing places in most creeks; one or two named anchorages |
| Mediterranean sailing season | roughly April to October |
| North Sea and Baltic | most winter sailing banned, roughly mid-November to late February |
| Indian Ocean | one voyage each way per year, timed to the monsoon |

Source: [Ship types](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#ship-types), [Port ranks](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#port-ranks-how-many-ports-on-a-coast), [Sailing seasons](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md#sailing-seasons).

> **Later era (1500s+):** Ocean routes open (India by sea 1498; Seville's monopoly of the Americas trade 1503), and Atlantic ports grow fast. See [Ocean trade, ports and trading posts](09-later-era-1500-1650.md#ocean-trade-ports-and-trading-posts).

> **Map tip:** For each region, write one line: "exports ..., imports ...". Then draw the route that carries each export to the nearest water or market. The biggest cities sit where networks meet: where land meets water, at confluences, at both ends of a pass or portage.

## Rural features

> **Rule of thumb:** At local scale almost every square kilometre has a human feature. Empty white space means marsh, moor or forest, and should be labelled as such.

**What is in a village:**

| Feature | How common | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|
| Parish church | One per parish | Slight rise, often at one end, next to the manor house |
| Manor house | One per manor (a village could have two or three) | Next to the church; often moated |
| Mill | About one per village (England c. 1300) | On the stream with its leat; windmill on rising ground |
| Smithy | Most villages | Main street, green or road |
| Green, pond | Many villages | In the middle; pond at the lowest point |
| Pound | Most villages by the 16th century | Edge of the village or green |
| Tofts and crofts | All villages | House plots along the streets, gardens behind |

Source: [What is in a village](06-villages-and-countryside.md#what-is-in-a-village).

**Village territory:**

| Settlement | People | Territory, lowland | Farthest regular fields |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hamlet | 10–75 | 1–5 km² (0.4–2 sq mi) | under 1 km (0.6 mi) |
| Small village | 75–150 | 2–7 km² (0.8–2.7 sq mi) | 1–1.5 km (0.6–1 mi) |
| Typical lowland village | 150–300 | 5–15 km² (2–6 sq mi) | 1.5–2.5 km (1–1.5 mi) |
| Large village | 300–500 | 10–20 km² (4–8 sq mi) | 2–3 km (1.2–1.9 mi) |
| Upland parish | 100–500, in many hamlets | 50–100+ km² (20–40+ sq mi) | Infields under 1 km away; summer pastures much farther |

| Ring | Distance from the houses | What is there |
|---|---|---|

Source: [Rings of land use around a village](06-villages-and-countryside.md#rings-of-land-use-around-a-village).

**Land budget of a settled lowland kingdom c. 1300:**

| Land | Share |
|---|---|
| Ploughland (open fields, fallow included) | 35–40% (England ~36% in 1300: 11.5 million acres, 7.4 million sown) |
| Woodland and wood-pasture | 7–15% |
| Meadow | about 5% |
| Deer parks | nearly 2% |
| Villages, gardens and roads | a few per cent |

## Industry siting

> **Rule of thumb:** Industry goes to the heaviest thing it needs: the ore, the fuel or the water power, not the customer. Only the light, finished product travels.

| Resource | Where it goes on the map | What settlement it creates |
|---|---|---|
| Silver, lead-silver | Old mountains and hill country, up side valleys | Chartered mining town, a mint, mining hamlets around it |
| Gold | Mountains; river sands | Mining town with a royal mint |
| Copper | Hills and mountains | Mining town with bare, smoke-killed land around it |
| Iron | Wooded hills with fast streams | Scattered bloomeries and forges; an iron-trading town where the valley meets a big river |
| Tin | Moorland valleys | Tinners' camps; "coinage towns" at the edge of the moor |
| Coal | Coalfields near a coast or navigable river | Pit hamlets; a coal port |
| Salt from brine | Lowland springs, with wood or peat nearby | A rich salt town; salt roads leading away |
| Rock salt | Foothills and mountains | Mining town with a royal official's castle |
| Sea salt | Lagoons and sheltered bays with dry summers | Salt-pan villages and a loading port |
| Building stone | Hill edges near rivers or the sea | Quarry hamlet; stone shipped by boat |
| Clay and brick | River valleys, estuaries, stoneless lowlands | Brickfields outside towns; potters' villages |
| Lime | Limestone edges, building sites, ports | Kilns, not towns |
| Timber | Upland forests above floatable rivers | Rafting villages; a timber market in a river town |
| Charcoal, glass, potash, tar | Deep forest | Temporary camps; glasshouses that move |
| Peat | Wet lowlands with few trees | Turf commons; flooded pits that become lakes |
| Wool | Downs, limestone hills, uplands, dry plateaus | Sheep villages, monastic farms, wool market towns |
| Cloth | Towns; fulling mills in hill valleys | Cloth towns; weaving villages along mill streams |
| Dye plants (woad, madder) | Rich plains near cloth regions | Woad towns and woad mills |
| Herring | Shallow seas and straits | Seasonal beach markets and curing stations |
| Cod (stockfish) | Arctic coasts | Fishing stations, drying racks, one big trading port |
| Ships | Sheltered estuaries near forest and rivers | Shipyards; walled state arsenals in great ports |
| Wine | South-facing river slopes | Wine villages; an export port downriver |
| Sugar cane | Irrigated, frost-free coastal plains (Cyprus, Sicily, Valencia) | Fortified estate with mill and boiling house; a loading port |

Fuel numbers: very roughly 50–150 kg of wood per 1 kg of bloomery iron; 150–200 kg of wood per 1 kg of forest glass; a Wealden blast furnace needed the woods of about a 5 km (3 mi) radius.

Source: [The master table](07-industry-and-resources.md#the-master-table), [Iron](07-industry-and-resources.md#iron), [Forest industries](07-industry-and-resources.md#forest-industries).

**In and around a town:**

| Trade | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking water, breweries | Upstream, or at wells and springs | Clean water |
| Grain and fulling mills | At weirs above and below town; on country streams | A fall of water |
| Butchers | Edge of town, near the river | Waste into the water |
| Tanners, dyers, flax retting | Downstream and outside the walls | Smell and pollution |
| Smiths, potters, brick, tile and lime kilns, glass | Outside the walls or at the edge | Fire and smoke |
| Tenter grounds, rope walks, timber yards | Suburbs and open fields | Space |
| Smelters, charcoal, glasshouses | At the mine and in the forest | Fuel and ore too heavy to move |
| Shipyards | Waterfront, at the edge of the port | Shore and space |

Source: [Where industry sits in and around a town](07-industry-and-resources.md#where-industry-sits-in-and-around-a-town).

**Special towns:**

| Town type | Typical size | Signature on the map |
|---|---|---|
| Mining town | 1,000–5,000; boom towns 10,000–20,000; camps 20–300 | Up a valley; spoil heaps, shafts, mint, big church, little farmland |
| Salt town | Small to middle-sized, rich for its size | Brine spring or salt mine; salt roads radiating out |
| Fishing village | 50–500; seasonal stations swell in season | Beach or cove; drying racks or smokehouses as labels ("Fish Racks"). Do not draw boats: a small boat marks the head of navigation |
| Cloth town | 2,000–10,000; the Flemish giants 20,000–65,000 | Cloth hall, tenter grounds, fulling mills, dyers by the river |
| Fair town | Often only a small or middle town | Big open fairground, many inns, several roads meeting |
| Iron or craft town | 1,000–10,000 | Forges and grinding mills along streams |
| Wine town | Small; the export port can be large | Vineyards on slopes behind, river quay |
| Spa town | Small | Hot springs, bath houses, many inns |

Per kingdom, aim for 1–3 mining districts (each 2–7 mining towns plus a mint town), 1–3 salt sources, one cloth region, one or two fair towns and several ports. On a continent map, put mining symbols on 3–6 mountain areas.

Source: [Special town types and how to spot them](07-industry-and-resources.md#special-town-types-and-how-to-spot-them) and [Mining districts on the map](07-industry-and-resources.md#mining-districts-on-the-map).

> **Later era (1500s+):** Blast furnaces, coal and boom towns: the Weald had about 50 furnaces by the later 1500s, and Joachimsthal reached about 20,000 people by 1534. See [What a mining town is like](07-industry-and-resources.md#what-a-mining-town-is-like) and [Industry: furnaces, mines and mills](09-later-era-1500-1650.md#industry-furnaces-mines-and-mills).

> **Map tip:** For every industry, point to three things on the map: the resource, the fuel and the way out (a river, a coast or a road to one). If one is missing, move the industry or add what is missing. Every realm needs salt, or a route that brings it in.

## Later-era changes (1500–1650)

> **Rule of thumb:** After 1500 the bottom of the map stays medieval and the top changes. Villages, fields and market towns look much the same; capitals, ports, fortresses and long-distance links are new or much bigger.

| Map feature | Medieval (c. 1000–1500) | 1500–1650 |
|---|---|---|
| Village spacing; market towns; travel speeds | 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi); 9–16 km (6–10 mi); walker 25–35 km (15–22 mi) a day | Unchanged |
| Capital | Often a travelling court; 3–7 times the second city in strong kingdoms | Fixed capital, 5–10 times the second city in some realms; draw it 2–4 times its medieval size |
| Biggest city in the West | Paris ~200,000 | Paris, Naples, London 200,000–400,000+; Istanbul 400,000–700,000 |
| Town walls | High and thin | Low, thick, earth-backed, bastioned (rich or threatened towns only) |
| Castles | Thousands; main military and lordly centre | Ruins, palaces, prisons or manor houses; replaced by forts and country houses |
| Coastal defence | Walled ports, a few towers | Watchtower chains 3–12 km (2–7 mi) apart; artillery forts at anchorages |
| Field armies | Usually 5,000–20,000 | Total forces of 50,000–150,000+ for great states (paper strength) |
| Ships | Cogs of 30–200 tons; galleys carrying about 140–250 tons | Carracks and galleons of several hundred to 2,000 tons; Dutch fluyts |
| Messages | Royal messengers 50–90 km (30–56 mi) a day | Relay posts every 20–40 km (12–25 mi), up to ~150 km (95 mi) a day |
| Canals | Short and flat, mostly flash locks; the first pound locks (Damme 1396, Milan 1452–58) | Pound locks spread; summit canals; passenger barge canals |
| Books | Hand-copied | Printed in 1,300+ places by 1650 |
| Countryside | Open fields, commons, deserted villages | More enclosure (47% of England by 1600); serf grain estates in the east |
| Wetlands | Mostly left wet; sea banks and small polders on the coasts | Whole lakes drained as polders (Beemster by 1612); inland fens drained |
| Monasteries | Everywhere | Ruined or converted in Protestant lands; flourishing in Catholic lands |
| Iron and mining | Bloomeries; steady mining towns | Blast furnaces; sudden boom towns (Joachimsthal, Potosí) |

**New fortifications:**

| Kind | Typical size | Garrison | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Watchtower | 10–15 m tall | a handful | Every few km along raided coasts |
| Coastal artillery fort | 30–100 m across | 10–30 in peace | Harbour mouths, anchorages, river mouths |
| Small fort (*sconce*) | 100–250 m across, 4–5 bastions | 50–300 | River crossings, dykes, marsh roads |
| Citadel | 400–700 m across, 4–6 bastions | several hundred to 2,000+ | Edge of a big or rebellious city |
| Fortress town | 1–2.5 km (0.6–1.5 mi) across, 6–12+ bastions | 1,000–5,000 in war | Frontiers, key river towns, big ports |

Bastions sit roughly 200–350 m apart. When one ruler controls the whole land, castles are cut sharply: France's edict of 1626 ordered interior castles razed; Japan went from about 3,000 working castles to about 170 after 1615.

Source: [Sizes and garrisons](09-later-era-1500-1650.md#sizes-and-garrisons) and [When a strong ruler cuts the number of castles](09-later-era-1500-1650.md#when-a-strong-ruler-cuts-the-number-of-castles-france-and-japan).

> **Map tip:** Do not redraw the countryside for a later setting. Keep your medieval villages and market towns, then change the top layer: enlarge the capital and one or two ocean ports, shrink one silted or blockaded port, add star forts only where they pay, post roads, a canal, a drained lake and faith areas (soft colour tints, never a new border line).

## Fantasy adjustments

> **Rule of thumb:** Fantasy does not cancel the rules of geography. It changes the numbers you feed into them. Change one input (water, food, defence, travel cost, threats or resources) and follow the chain of effects.

**How danger reshapes settlement:**

| Threat level | Real analogue | Settlement pattern and defences |
|---|---|---|
| Low (wolves, bandits) | England or France c. 1250 | The normal map; working castles about 20–30 km (12–19 mi) apart; villages unwalled |
| Seasonal raids | Anglo-Scottish border, 14th–16th centuries | Lone farms become tower houses; villages huddle |
| Frequent raids or large monsters | Latium c. 920–1030; Wessex 870s–890s | Walled or hilltop villages of 300–1,000; refuge forts within a day's walk; beacons |
| Constant danger | The "Great Wilderness" of the Baltic; crusader frontier | Only fortified towns and garrison castles; fields only near walls |

Source: [How danger reshapes settlement](10-fantasy-variants.md#how-danger-reshapes-settlement).

**Key fantasy numbers:**

| If your world has… | What changes | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Monsters in the countryside | Fewer lone farms; fields close to a refuge | Fields within about one hour's walk, 4–5 km (2.5–3 mi), of the walls (heuristic) |
| A "points of light" frontier | Isolated walled settlements | Each point about 50–80 km² of fields and 1,500–4,000 people |
| A monster-haunted frontier | Refuge forts manned by the farms around them | Refuges about a day's walk, 24–32 km (15–20 mi), apart; a frontier band of 30–100 km (20–60 mi) |
| Fertility magic | Higher density, bigger towns | +50% yield: 40–60 per km² (100–155 per sq mi); about 2× yield: 50–75 per km² (130–190 per sq mi), like Flanders and Holland |
| Healing magic | Faster growth, same ceiling | 2–3% a year doubles the people in 23–35 years |
| Flying mounts | Fast messages and luxuries, not bulk | One load about 100–120 kg; a cog's 30–200 tons is roughly 250–2,000 flying loads; a griffon post at 300 km (190 mi) a day is 3–6 times a royal messenger |
| Portals | Hub towns with customs, warehouses and forts | Only shiploads of grain every day free inland cities from the water rule |
| Mage academies | Placed like universities | 1–3 per kingdom, in cities of 10,000+ |
| Dwarves | Mountain holds that buy food through gate towns | Holds of a few thousand to perhaps 15,000; a hold of 10,000 needs farmland within 21–27 km (13–17 mi) on lowland or about 55 km (35 mi) on marginal land |
| Elves | Low-density forest realm, wide border wilderness | 1–5 per km² (3–13 per sq mi) or less |
| Halflings | Dense farm villages, few castles | 30–75 per km² (78–190 per sq mi); villages 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) apart |
| Steppe "hordes" | Seasonal camps, raid trails, frontier markets | Under 1–2 per km² (under 3–5 per sq mi) |
| Dragons | Empty ring around the lair, tribute villages beyond | One cow a week needs a herd of about 250–500 cattle (rough example) |
| Undead or blights | Like the Black Death | 30–60% dead; walled graveyards outside towns; quarantine islands |

Use no more than three or four of these in any one region. A fantasy danger frontier (30–100 km, 20–60 mi) is wider than a real march band (10–50 km, 6–30 mi), but both are drawn with the same hatching; truly wild land beyond it takes dense cross-hatching. For the symbols, see the fantasy group of the [master legend](#master-legend-and-label-hierarchy).

Source: [Dangerous wilderness and monsters](10-fantasy-variants.md#dangerous-wilderness-and-monsters), [Suggested symbols](10-fantasy-variants.md#suggested-symbols), [Magic, effect by effect](10-fantasy-variants.md#magic-effect-by-effect), [Non-human peoples](10-fantasy-variants.md#non-human-peoples-archetypes-with-real-analogues), [Gods, undead and dangerous seas](10-fantasy-variants.md#gods-undead-and-dangerous-seas), [What changes if: summary table](10-fantasy-variants.md#what-changes-if-summary-table), and for the griffon post [How big can a realm be](03-capitals-and-borders.md#how-big-can-a-realm-be).

> **Map tip:** For each fantasy element, write one line: "X exists, so Y changes" ("griffon riders exist, so castles have roofed towers and every lord keeps an aerie"). Then draw the edges where peoples meet: gate towns, trading stones, frontier markets, tribute villages.

## Master legend and label hierarchy

> **Rule of thumb:** One symbol, one meaning. A filled star means a capital and nothing else. Borders are always dash-dot lines; routes are always solid or dotted lines; colour fills and tints are for areas only.

The chapters suggest symbols for their own topics. This legend gives one symbol per feature for the whole guide, and chapters 02–11 now use it. If a map tip anywhere seems to use a symbol differently, this legend wins. Scale: C = continent, K = kingdom or region, L = local area. Many small works (kilns, racks, shipyards) take a word, not a picture: a new little picture easily looks like a symbol that already means something else.

| Shape | Use it only for | Not for |
|---|---|---|
| Star | Filled = realm capital; outline = star fort (1500s+) | Great city (large ringed circle), citadel (castle icon at the town edge), shrine (scallop), synagogue (lamp), lighthouse (tower with rays), portal (spiral) |
| Ring or dot in a circle | Ring = town; central dot = county town | Motte (mound with a tower tick), moot (lone tree), hinterland circles (pencil only) |
| Small square | Solid = tower house; open = manor house | Farmstead (tiny dot); tofts (narrow plots with a house mark) |
| Small rectangles in a chain | Fishponds | Hammer ponds (one pond, straight dam, labelled) |
| Triangle | Watchtower | Beacon (flame dot), barrow (filled mound), standing stone (tick) |
| Cross | Church or chapel | Windmill (X of sails on a dot) |
| Bars across a line | One bar with "T" = toll; two bars = lock | Bridge (")(") |

**Label hierarchy:**

| Feature | Lettering | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Realm | Widely spaced CAPITALS across the whole territory | Largest on the map |
| Duchy or great fief | Smaller italic capitals | Below realm |
| County | Lower case | Below duchy |
| Great city | CAPITALS | 13–14 pt |
| City | Bold or small capitals | 11–12 pt |
| Town | Regular | 9–10 pt |
| Small market town | Regular | 8–9 pt |
| Village | Regular; label only notable ones at kingdom scale | 7–8 pt |
| Hamlet | Italic, or none | 6–7 pt |
| Rivers, lakes, seas | Italic, traditionally blue, along a straight stretch of the line | By importance |
| Marches and empty zones | Named in the area ("the West March", "moor", "fen") | Area label |

Rules: nothing under about 6 pt in print; 2–3 typefaces at most; upper case only for realms and the largest features; put point labels above and to the right of the symbol, or directly above; keep area labels inside the area, curved to its shape, with letter gaps no more than about 4 times the letter height; place labels last, biggest first.

**Older layers:** Ordnance Survey maps have long used separate typefaces for antiquities of different periods (pre-Roman, Roman and medieval), so ruins read as ruins without extra symbols. Copy the idea: pick one extra typeface for your older layers, and use it only for them. Saxton's printed county atlas of 1579 shows the period look: towns and villages as small church-tower symbols.

## Sanity checklist: 15 questions

> **Map tip:** Run this check when the map is finished. Every "no" is a place to look again before you add a single label.

| # | Question | If the answer is "no" | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does every settlement touch fresh water (river, stream, spring, lake, coast, or a drawn well or cistern)? | Move it to water, or draw the engineering that brings water | [Fresh water](01-settlement-placement.md#fresh-water) |
| 2 | Does every desert town have a river, oasis or qanat, with green gardens around it? | Add the water source and its green belt | [2. A desert city with no oasis or river](11-common-mistakes.md#2-a-desert-city-with-no-oasis-or-river) |
| 3 | Does every town have villages and farmland within its radius (21–27 km, 13–17 mi, for a town of 10,000 on good land)? | Draw the farmland ring, or shrink the town | [Feeding towns](02-population-and-sizes.md#feeding-towns-the-hinterland) |
| 4 | Is every city over 10,000 on navigable water or a coast, or explained by a canal, irrigated plain, rich farmland or silver? | Move it to water or make it smaller | [4. A huge city with no water transport](11-common-mistakes.md#4-a-huge-city-with-no-water-transport) |
| 5 | Are there far more villages than towns and far more towns than cities (about 10 towns and 50–100 market towns per city of 10,000+)? | Rebalance the symbols | [How many of each kind](02-population-and-sizes.md#how-many-of-each-kind) |
