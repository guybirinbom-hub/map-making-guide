# Fantasy Variants: Magic, Monsters and Other Races

The other chapters describe a world without magic. This chapter shows how to add magic, monsters and non-human peoples without breaking that realism. The method is simple: apply the real rules first, then ask which **input** a fantasy element changes (water, food, defence, travel cost, threats or resources) and follow the consequences. Every adaptation here is tied to a real historical case that worked the same way.

## The method: change the inputs, not the rules

> **Rule of thumb:** Fantasy does not cancel the rules of geography. It changes the numbers you feed into them. Change one input, then follow the chain of effects across the whole map.

Every settlement rule in this guide comes from six inputs. Each fantasy element changes one or more of them.

| Input | The realistic rule (see chapter) | Fantasy elements that change it |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | Every settlement needs reliable water. Desert towns exist only at oases, rivers or *qanats* (underground water channels). See [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md). | Water magic, gods, cursed or poisoned rivers |
| **Food** | About 1 ha (2.5 acres) of arable land per person. Good lowland holds 30–50 people per km² (78–130 per sq mi). A town of 10,000 needs farmland within 21–27 km (13–17 mi). See [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md). | Fertility magic, weather control, healing (more mouths) |
| **Defence** | Towns of 2,000+ are usually walled. A castle controls about 15–30 km (10–20 mi) around it. See [Castles, Forts and Military Outposts](04-military-sites.md). | Monsters, flying enemies, magical wards, undead |
| **Travel cost** | Land : river : sea cost ≈ 8 : 4 : 1. Towns over 10,000 sit on navigable water. See [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md). | Portals, flying mounts, dangerous seas, guarded roads |
| **Threats** | War, raids, famine, plague | Monsters, dragons, raiding peoples, blights, angry gods |
| **Resources** | Ore, salt, stone and timber create special towns. See [Industry, Resources and Special Towns](07-industry-and-resources.md). | Magic crystals, ley lines, monster parts, dragon hoards |

**Why this works:** a reader cannot check your magic, but they can feel when a map is wrong. A city in a desert with no water looks wrong. A desert city with a great water-temple at its centre and a ring of gardens around it looks right, because the cause is visible.

> **Map tip:** For each fantasy element, write one line: "X exists, so Y changes." For example: "Griffon riders exist, so castles have roofed towers and every lord keeps an aerie." If you cannot write the second half, the element is only decoration. That is allowed, but it will not make the map feel real.

## Dangerous wilderness and monsters

> **Rule of thumb:** Danger concentrates people. The more dangerous the countryside, the fewer, bigger and better-defended the settlements, and the wider the empty land between them.

**Why:** a lone farm cannot defend itself against a troll or a raiding party. A wall is cheaper per person when many people share it. Fields must be close enough to a refuge that workers can run back in time. All three push people together.

### How danger reshapes settlement

The real world already gives us every level of danger. Pick the row that fits each region of your map.

| Threat level | Real analogue | Settlement pattern | Defences | How it looks on the map |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** (wolves, bandits) | England or France c. 1250, away from borders | Normal mix: nucleated (clustered) villages on good land, lone farms in uplands | Working castles about 20–30 km (12–19 mi) apart (see [chapter 04](04-military-sites.md#borderlands-and-conquered-land)); villages unwalled | The baseline map in the other chapters |
| **Seasonal raids** (a few times a decade) | Anglo-Scottish border, 14th–16th centuries | Lone farms survive but become tower houses; villages huddle | Tower houses and fortified farmhouses. Late medieval England had over 250 tower houses, Scotland ~800, Ireland over 3,000 | Small tower symbols scattered over farmland |
| **Frequent raids or large monsters** | Latium (central Italy) c. 920–1030; Wessex in the 870s–890s; the Provence coast in the 900s | Farms abandoned; people move into walled or hilltop villages of 300–1,000 | Walled villages; refuge forts within a day's walk; watchtowers; beacons | Fewer, bigger dots on hilltops; empty valley floors; coasts deserted |
| **Constant danger** | The "Great Wilderness" between the Teutonic Order and Lithuania (13th–14th centuries); Crusader frontier castles | Only fortified towns and garrison castles; fields only near walls | Great garrisons. The Templar castle of Safed (1260s) held 1,700 people in peacetime and 2,200 in war | Islands of settlement in empty land |

How far can fields be from a refuge? There is no measured figure. A useful heuristic is **about one hour's walk: 4–5 km (2.5–3 mi)**. That gives workers time to reach the walls when the alarm sounds.

### Nucleated and walled villages

The best real case is Italian *incastellamento* (the "castle-ing" of the countryside). In Latium (the region around Rome), scattered farms were regrouped into fortified hilltop villages. Pierre Toubert (1973) put the main wave between about 920 and 1030; other historians see it continuing into the 12th century (see [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md)). Historians still argue how much of this was caused by raids and how much by lords wanting control. For your map, the result is what matters: hilltop villages everywhere, and few houses in the open.

Where raids are seasonal, lone farms do not disappear. They turn into towers. On the Anglo-Scottish border, families built small stone tower houses and fortified farmhouses so they could shut themselves and their animals in for a few days.

The most extreme refuge is underground. In Cappadocia (central Anatolia), the underground city of Derinkuyu reaches about 85 m (280 ft) deep. It was greatly enlarged during the Arab–Byzantine wars (roughly 780–1180) as a refuge from Arab raids. It may have sheltered up to about 20,000 people with their animals and food, although that is an estimate, not a count.

### Refuge networks and frontier forts

When King Alfred of Wessex faced Viking armies in the 870s–890s, he built a network of *burhs* (fortified towns). The Burghal Hidage is a list of these burhs and the land assigned to man them. It is usually dated to the early 900s, although some historians argue that it describes Alfred's own plan of about 880. Its two versions together list 33 burhs:

- Each hide (a unit of taxed land) supplied one man.
- Four men defended each pole (about 5 m or 16½ ft) of wall.
- Winchester was assessed at 2,400 hides, so 600 poles, or about 3 km (1.9 mi) of wall. This closely matches the length of its old Roman walls.
- The burhs were spread so that few country people lived more than about 24–32 km (15–20 mi), roughly one day's walk, from one.

This is the best real model for a monster-haunted kingdom: **a planned net of refuges about a day's walk apart, each manned by the farms around it.**

Denmark shows a different version. In about 970–980, King Harald Bluetooth built five large circular "ring fortresses" (Trelleborg, Fyrkat, Aggersborg, Nonnebakken and Borgring) spread across his kingdom. They were royal strongholds that showed the king's power, more than refuges for farmers. A fantasy king can do the same: a few identical, planned forts placed across the realm at once.

Rivers can carry the threat inland. Against Viking ships, the Frankish king Charles the Bald ordered fortified bridges at towns on rivers in the Edict of Pîtres (864). The best known was at Pont-de-l'Arche on the Seine. If your monsters swim or sail, expect fortified bridges and river barriers below important towns.

### Watchtowers and beacons

A warning network buys time. Two real systems show the range of options:

| System | Date | Length and spacing | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| Byzantine beacon chain | 9th century | About 720 km (450 mi), about nine mountain-top stations 55–100 km (35–60 mi) apart (details in [Watchtowers, beacons and coastal warning](04-military-sites.md#watchtowers-beacons-and-coastal-warning)) | Sent one of 12 pre-arranged messages, each tied to an hour of the day. Modern experiments suggest a message crossed the whole line in about an hour |
| Hungarian *gyepű* | 11th–13th centuries | A belt around the whole kingdom | An outer border zone deliberately kept empty, blocked with felled trees, hedges, ditches and banks of earth and stone. Gates were the only ways through. Guard settlements of border troops (often archers) kept watch. It failed against the Mongols in 1241, after which Hungary built stone castles |

How close should local watchtowers be? They must see each other's smoke or fire. In broken or wooded country that means a few kilometres apart. Mountain-top beacons can be 55–100 km (35–60 mi) apart. These are rough figures.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Muscovy's Great Abatis Line (complete by 1566) and the later Belgorod Line (1635–54) are real versions of this: belts of felled trees, ditches, palisades, watchtowers and small forts that slowed raiders so villagers could flee and troops could gather. See [Fortified lines on open frontiers](09-later-era-1500-1650.md#fortified-lines-on-open-frontiers).

### Guarded roads

Roads through dangerous country need clearing, patrols and safe stops. All three existed:

- **Clearing:** England's Statute of Winchester (1285) ordered highways between market towns to be cleared for 200 feet (about 60 m) on each side. Ditches, undergrowth and bushes had to go, so that no robber could hide close to the road. Big trees such as oaks could stay if the ground under them was kept clear. The gates of walled towns were to be shut from sunset to sunrise, and a night watch kept.
- **Patrols:** in the 12th century, towns and peasants in northern Spain formed brotherhoods (*hermandades*) to police the pilgrim road to Santiago. In 1476 the Catholic Monarchs (Isabella and Ferdinand) set up the Santa Hermandad, a kingdom-wide police force with power over crimes on roads and in open country. The Knights Templar were founded c. 1119–1120 to protect pilgrims on the roads of the Holy Land.
- **Safe stops:** the hospice on the Great St Bernard Pass in the Alps was founded in 1049 (the traditional date) to shelter travellers. Put such stops a day's travel apart: 15–30 km (10–20 mi) on foot roads, 30–40 km (19–25 mi) on caravan routes.

The opposite also happened. Muslim raiders from al-Andalus set up a base at Fraxinetum on the Provence coast in about 889. From about 906 until they were driven out in 972–973, they held some or all of the western Alpine passes. The chronicler Flodoard wrote in 951 that they let travellers to Rome pass only after paying tribute. **A monster that holds a pass becomes a toll-collector.** Traffic either pays, fights, or finds another pass.

### Hunters, rangers and their lodges

Kings paid people to fight dangerous animals:

- Charlemagne's estate rules, the *Capitulare de villis* (c. 800), told stewards to hunt wolves and take wolf cubs in May. The French royal wolf-hunters (the *louveterie*) trace their origin to wolf-hunters (*luparii*) appointed by Charlemagne. French tradition dates this to 813.
- William of Malmesbury (writing c. 1125) says King Edgar (r. 959–975) took a tribute of 300 wolf skins a year from the Welsh kings. He wrote over a century after the events, and the story may be exaggerated.
- Edward I of England employed Peter Corbet (1281) to destroy wolves in five western counties.
- James I of Scotland (1427) ordered three wolf hunts a year.

Wolves were rare or gone in England by about 1500. They survived in Scotland until the 1600s or later. Organised hunting with royal support worked, but it took centuries.

> **Map tip:** Put hunters' or rangers' lodges at forest edges, at fords and passes, and where a road enters the wild. Space them about a day's patrol apart, 15–30 km (10–20 mi), which matches a castle's control radius. A monster-hunting order is a military order (like the Templars): give it castles on the frontier and a chapter house (its headquarters building) in a city.

### "Points of light": use it for frontiers, not whole kingdoms

"Points of light" is a game-design term from *Dungeons & Dragons* 4th edition (2008): a mostly wild world with a few small, isolated islands of civilization. It is a good picture of a **frontier**, but not of a whole realm. In real history, even dangerous kingdoms had a dense core where peasants walked to market in safety.

You can estimate the size of each "point". Suppose people farm only within 4–5 km (2.5–3 mi) of their walls. That circle covers about 50–80 km². At 30–50 people per km², each point holds roughly **1,500–4,000 people**. Larger towns are possible only where a river or a protected sea route brings food from farther away.

> **Map tip:** Shade your map in three tones: safe core, frontier, and wild. In the core, use the normal rules. On the frontier, use fewer, bigger, walled settlements on hills, with watchtowers, refuge forts about a day's walk apart, and cleared roads with fortified stops. In the wild, draw only ruins, lairs and the occasional ranger lodge. A frontier band of 30–100 km (20–60 mi) is a good default.

## Magic, effect by effect

Take each kind of magic separately. Ask which input it changes, how strongly, and **who controls it**. The answer to the last question tells you where the new buildings go.

### Food and fertility magic

> **Rule of thumb:** Fertility magic raises the ceiling on population. It does not remove the transport limit. Cities above about 50,000 still need water transport, unless magic also moves the grain.

**Why:** with a typical medieval yield of 4 : 1, a farmer keeps back 1 of every 4 units of grain as seed and eats or sells 3. If magic doubles the yield to 8 : 1, the farmer keeps 7. Net food more than doubles. The land fills up with people, and the surplus feeds bigger towns.

The best real comparison is the most densely farmed land in medieval Europe: Flanders and Holland in the 15th century. The figures below come from the model in [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md). The "moderate" row is interpolated.

| Strength of the magic | Treat the region like | Rural density | Share living in towns | Farmland radius for a town of 10,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None | Good lowland c. 1300 | 30–50 per km² (78–130 per sq mi) | 10–15% | 21–27 km (13–17 mi) |
| Moderate (+50% yield) | Between the rows | 40–60 per km² (100–155 per sq mi) | 15–20% | ~15–19 km (9–12 mi) |
| Strong (about 2× yield) | Flanders and Holland (15th century) | 50–75 per km² (130–190 per sq mi) | 20–40% | ~12–15 km (7–9 mi) |
| Very strong (3× or more) | Nothing in medieval Europe | Higher still | Higher still | Small. The limit becomes water, fuel and transport, not food |

**Who controls it?** If priests bless the fields, the temple takes a share, as the real Church took a tithe (a tenth). Expect rich temples in every village and a great temple-estate near the capital. If the magic is in the soil (a blessed valley), that valley becomes the core of a realm, like the Île-de-France around Paris.

> **Map tip:** Draw a blessed region exactly like Flanders: villages 1.5–2 km (about 1 mi) apart, market towns every 10 km (6 mi), and many walled towns of 5,000–20,000. Keep the edges of the blessing visible: the density should drop where the magic ends.

### Water magic and desert cities

Real desert cities show what water magic would do, because a *qanat* (an underground channel that brings groundwater from the hills) is a kind of engineered spring.

- **Marrakesh** was founded c. 1070 by the Almoravids on a dry plain. From the 11th century, *khettaras* (the Moroccan name for qanats) brought groundwater to it from the direction of the Atlas Mountains. That water turned the site into an imperial capital with large gardens.
- **Yazd and Kerman** in Iran grew at the foot of mountains where qanats could be dug (see [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md)).
- **Valencia's** irrigation farmers still settle water disputes at a weekly Water Tribunal, held every Thursday at a door of the cathedral. Water rights become law and politics.

**What changes:** each magical spring acts as an oasis. Settlement forms **dots** around each water source, not a carpet. The size of each dot depends on how much water the magic gives. Food is the next limit: irrigated gardens are very productive, but there are only so many of them.

**Who controls it?** If one mage or one temple makes the water, the whole city depends on them. That gives them enormous power. It also makes the city fragile: real desert towns died when their water failed. A water-mage's tower in a desert city is the citadel, so draw it at the centre, walled.

> **Map tip:** In a magically watered desert, draw: the water source (temple, tower, sacred well) in the centre; a ring of green gardens around it; then the town; then bare desert. Caravan stops still sit 30–40 km (19–25 mi) apart. Each one needs its own water: either a real well or a smaller magical one.

### Healing magic and population growth

> **Rule of thumb:** Healing magic makes a population grow faster. It does not raise the ceiling. A healed realm fills its land in a few generations, then hits the food limit.

**Why:** in the Middle Ages births and deaths were nearly equal. Europe's population grew only 2–3 times between 1000 and 1300, which is about 0.2–0.4% a year. If healing removes most deaths from disease and childbirth, growth can jump.

| Yearly growth | Population doubles in | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2–0.4% | 175–350 years | Europe 1000–1300 |
| 1% | ~70 years | — |
| 2–3% | 23–35 years | North America (the British colonies, then the United States). In 1798 Malthus noted that its population had doubled about every 25 years, because land was plentiful |

**What changes on the map:**
- Faster frontier expansion: new villages cut into forests, and lords found planned new towns (see [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md#planted-new-towns-and-bastides)).
- More pressure on borders and more wars over land.
- No Black-Death-style collapse, so fewer deserted villages and ruins.
- Towns grow on their own. In real medieval towns, deaths outnumbered births, and towns grew only because people moved in from the countryside. Healers in a town would change that.

If healers are rare and expensive, only towns and nobles benefit. Then the towns grow and the countryside does not.

### Teleport circles and portals

> **Rule of thumb:** A portal is a harbour without a sea. Treat it like a port, a bridge or a strait: a fixed point where traffic gathers and where the ruler can tax it.

**Real analogues:**
- **Øresund (the Sound):** from 1429 every foreign ship passing between the North Sea and the Baltic had to stop at Helsingør and pay the Danish king. A castle (later rebuilt as Kronborg) guarded the strait.
- **Staple right:** from 1259, Cologne forced passing merchants to stop, unload and offer their goods for sale for three days before moving on. Any city that owns a portal will try the same.

**What grows around a portal:** a customs house, warehouses, inns, money-changers, a market, a guard fort, and very often a temple. The portal town becomes a hub, like a crossroads town. If one end is in another realm, expect a **twin town** at each end.

**Capacity decides everything.** Compare the portal with a ship. A typical cog (the main northern cargo ship) carried 30–200 tons (see [chapter 05](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)).

| If a portal moves... | It acts like... | Effect on the map |
|---|---|---|
| A few people and letters a day | A royal messenger service | Capitals govern farther (see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md)). Trade barely changes |
| Cartloads of valuable goods | A mountain pass or a caravan route | Luxury trade (spices, silk, gems) moves through portals. Portal towns get rich. Bulk goods still go by water |
| Shiploads of grain every day | A great river or sea lane | Inland cities can grow beyond 50,000 without rivers. The capital can sit wherever the portal network meets |

**Effect on capitals:** a realm with portals will put its capital at the portal hub or build the network around the capital, the way Roman roads led to Rome. A portal into the capital is also a weak point. Guard it like a harbour mouth: Constantinople closed its harbour, the Golden Horn, with a chain.

### Flying mounts and airships

> **Rule of thumb:** Flying moves people, messages and luxuries. It does not move bulk. A griffon carries about what a pack horse carries.

**Why:** a pack horse carries about 100–120 kg. A flying mount strong enough to carry a rider can carry a similar load. One cog carried 30–200 tons, which equals roughly **250 to 2,000** flying loads. Grain, timber, stone and wine stay on rivers and seas.

**What changes:**

| Change | Real analogue | Map result |
|---|---|---|
| Fast messages | Nur ad-Din ran a regular pigeon post between Baghdad and Syria by 1167. The Mamluk sultan Baybars (r. 1260–1277) rebuilt the state relay post, the *barid* | A chain of relay aeries (nests or stables for flying mounts) a day's flight apart. If a griffon flies about 300 km (190 mi) a day, as in [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md), the capital can govern much farther |
| Trade over mountains | Mule trains over Alpine passes carried mostly high-value goods | Gems, spices and letters fly straight over the range. Pass towns lose travellers and post but keep bulk freight |
| Aeries | Royal mews (falcon houses) and pigeon lofts sat in castles and towns | Aeries on crags and tower tops, with pasture or herds nearby to feed the animals |
| Defence against the sky | No direct real case | Roofed towers and wall-walks, bolt-throwers on towers, small courtyards, nets, lookouts on every peak, and keeps that can be held against an attack from above |

**How fast do fortresses change?** The best real comparison is gunpowder. After the French invasion of Italy in 1494, new artillery forts with low, thick walls and angled bastions spread across Italy within a few decades. If flying enemies are new in your world, show a mix: old open-topped castles beside new roofed ones.

> **Later era (1500s+):** The bastion fort (*trace italienne*) is the real example of fortresses redesigned for a new threat. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

### Weather control

**Real analogue:** around 815, Archbishop Agobard of Lyon wrote *On Hail and Thunder* against a popular belief in *tempestarii*, "storm-makers" who could raise or turn away hail. He complained that peasants paid them a share of their crops for protection. So even an imaginary weather-mage got paid like a tax collector.

**What changes:**
- **Fewer famines.** The Great Famine of 1315–17 was caused by years of heavy rain. Weather control prevents that kind of disaster, so there are fewer deserted villages.
- **Farming moves into marginal climates.** The Norse settled Greenland c. 985 and abandoned it in the 15th century, partly because the climate cooled. Weather magic would keep such colonies alive and push farming higher up mountains and farther north.
- **Safer seas.** Reliable winds make sea trade cheaper and more ports viable.
- **Weather as a weapon.** The Mongols tried to invade Japan by sea in 1274 and 1281. A storm hit the 1274 fleet as it withdrew (how much damage it did is debated). In 1281 a typhoon destroyed much of the second fleet at anchor. The Japanese called it *kamikaze*, the "divine wind". Coastal realms with storm-mages would be hard to invade by sea.

**Who controls it?** Like the lord's mill, which peasants were often forced to use and pay for, a weather-mage would be a lordly monopoly. Put weather towers on hilltops overlooking the most valuable farmland.

### Magical resources: crystals, ore and ley lines

> **Rule of thumb:** A magic crystal is ore. A ley line is a river. Place them, then apply the real rules for mines and rivers.

**Crystals as ore.** Real silver strikes show how fast a boom town grows:

| Real boom town | Timeline | Size and features |
|---|---|---|
| Kutná Hora (Bohemia) | Mining from c. 1260; royal mining code in 1300; central mint soon after 1300; main mining zone closed in 1543 | Grew fast and became the second town of Bohemia after Prague. Around 1300 its mines may have produced about a third of Europe's silver. (Some popular sources say 60,000 people. That is unlikely for a town that was smaller than Prague, so use a much lower figure.) |
| Novo Brdo (Serbia) | Saxon miners brought in under King Milutin (1282–1321); named in 1326; taken by the Ottomans in 1455 after a 40-day siege | About 6,000 kg of silver a year by 1450. In the 15th century 4,000–5,000 people lived inside the walls and 8,000–10,000 more in satellite settlements around them |
| Freiberg (Saxony) | Silver found in 1168 | Grew into a major mining town |

What a crystal boom brings: miners from far away with their own laws; a fortress and garrison; a mint; roads for food coming in; charcoal burners stripping the forests; and, when the lode runs out, decline. See [Industry, Resources and Special Towns](07-industry-and-resources.md).

**Ley lines as rivers.** The modern idea of ley lines comes from Alfred Watkins in the 1920s. He saw them as old straight tracks. Only in the 1960s were they reimagined as lines of earth energy. Archaeologists reject them as real features, but in fantasy they are useful. Treat them like rivers:
- Towers and towns form **chains** along the line.
- A **nexus** (where lines cross) acts like a confluence (the place where two rivers meet): it attracts the biggest settlement and the strongest fortress.
- A place where a line is easy to reach acts like a ford: expect a tower, a toll and a fight over who owns it.

### Mage towers and academies

> **Rule of thumb:** Place academies like universities (in big cities) and lone mage towers like monasteries (remote, but supplied).

| Type | Real analogue | Numbers | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academy of magic | Universities: about 20 in Europe c. 1300, 60–80 by 1500. England had only two (Oxford and Cambridge) | 1–3 per kingdom | In cities of 10,000+: they need food, lodging, books, patrons and students |
| Order of mages with many houses | Cistercian monks: about 500 houses by 1300 and nearly 750 at their peak in the 1400s; roughly 75–80 abbeys of monks in England and Wales (about one per 2,000 km² or 770 sq mi) | Dozens per kingdom, if magic is as common as monasteries | Remote valleys, but with granges (outlying farms run by the monastery). Some granges were next door; others were tens of kilometres away. Their size varied greatly |
| Dangerous experiments | Leper houses and plague hospitals, placed outside towns or on islands | A few per city | Outside the walls, downwind (so smoke and smells blow away from the town), across water |

### Magical defences

Wards (magical barriers) behave like walls. A wall's cost depends on its **length**, but the space inside depends on its **area**. So big wards are cheaper per person than small ones. This favours fewer, bigger warded towns, the same logic as the burhs, where each hide paid for a share of the wall.

Ask two questions:
1. **What does the ward stop?** If it stops monsters but not armies, towns still need walls, and castles stay. If it stops armies too, castles shrink and frontier forts become ward-stone posts that are watched and maintained.
2. **Who pays for its upkeep?** English towns paid for their walls with *murage*, a toll the king allowed them to charge. A warded town needs a ward-tax and a mage-guild, and it falls when the mages leave.

> **Map tip:** Add magic in this order: (1) mark magical resources (crystals, ley lines, springs) like ore and rivers; (2) place the towers, academies and boom towns that use them; (3) adjust density for fertility or healing magic; (4) add the new hubs (portals, aeries); (5) adjust the fortresses. Do this after the realistic steps in [Step-by-step](00-step-by-step.md), not instead of them.

## Non-human peoples: archetypes with real analogues

Each common fantasy people matches a real way of life. Use the real way of life to decide where they live, how many there are and how their land looks.

| People | Real analogue | Settlement type | Density | Map signature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dwarves** | Medieval mining communities (Saxon miners in Bohemia and Serbia); Cappadocian underground refuges | Mountain holds plus trade towns at the mountain gates | Holds like mining towns: a few thousand to perhaps 15,000 | Gate towns on valley floors, mine symbols, smelters, cleared slopes |
| **Elves** | Forest peoples of the Baltic: pagan Lithuanians and Prussians | Small clearings, hill-forts, sacred groves | 1–5 per km² (3–13 per sq mi) or less | Big forest, few dots, no roads, wide border wilderness |
| **Halflings** | Free peasant communities of fertile lowlands: Dithmarschen, Flanders | Dense villages, mills, small market towns | 30–75 per km² (78–190 per sq mi) | Many small villages, few castles |
| **Orcs and other "horde" peoples** | Steppe nomads: Mongols, Cumans, Crimean Tatars | Seasonal camps, a few royal cities | Under 1–2 per km² (under 3–5 per sq mi) | Pastures, camps, raid trails, frontier markets |
| **Merfolk** | Sea nomads such as the Orang Laut of the Strait of Malacca | Reefs and sheltered waters, shore markets | — | Shore market towns, safe or unsafe straits |
| **Giants and dragons** | Raiders who are paid tribute (Vikings in England); predators that need huge ranges | Lairs | — | Empty rings around lairs, tribute villages beyond |

### Dwarves: mountain holds and gate towns

**Real model:** medieval mining towns. German ("Saxon") miners settled in Bohemia, Hungary and Serbia under special privileges. They had their own mining law, their own courts and their own churches. Novo Brdo and Kutná Hora (see the table above) show the size: a few thousand people inside the walls and more in the settlements around them.

**The food problem:** a hold of 10,000 people needs farmland within 21–27 km (13–17 mi) on good lowland, or about 55 km (35 mi) on marginal land. Mountains are marginal land. So a dwarf hold must buy food. That creates a **gate town**: a market town on the nearest valley floor, ideally on a river, where grain comes in and metal goes out. Treat it like a pass town or a mining town's market.

**Underground roads:** real tunnels were short. The tunnel said to link Derinkuyu with the next underground town, Kaymaklı, is about 8–9 km (5–5.6 mi) long. A long underground road between holds is a wonder of the world. Draw a few, not a network.

> **Map tip:** For each dwarf hold, draw one gate town on the valley floor, a road climbing to the gate, mine-tip and smelter symbols, and bare slopes where trees were cut for charcoal. Group holds into a league (like the Hanse) with one leading hold.

### Elves: forest realms

**Real model:** the forest and marsh lands of pagan Lithuania (Christian only from 1387) and the Baltic Prussians. Density was low: 1–5 people per km² (3–13 per sq mi). Their realm had no sharp line border. Between the Teutonic Order and Lithuania lay the "Great Wilderness", a forest belt emptied by raids from both sides and crossed by war parties in summer and winter.

**What it looks like:** a large forest with few, small clearings; hill-forts and sacred groves; paths but no roads; and a wide empty border. Human villages pushing into the forest edge with new clearings are a source of conflict.

**Contact:** reclusive peoples may trade without meeting. Herodotus (5th century BC) described "silent trade" on the West African coast: Carthaginian traders left goods on the beach, lit a smoky fire and withdrew, and local people left gold beside the goods. Silent trade continued in the West African gold trade in the Middle Ages. Mark a few **trading stones** at the forest edge.

### Halflings: fertile farmland

**Real model:** self-governing peasant societies on rich land. Dithmarschen, a marshland on the North Sea coast of Germany, was a peasant republic from the 13th century until 1559. Its parishes ruled themselves. In 1500, at Hemmingstedt, its farmers used dykes and flooded ditches to defeat a Danish royal army.

**What it looks like:** density like Flanders (50–75 per km²) or good lowland (30–50 per km²). Villages 1.5–4 km (1–2.5 mi) apart, many mills, market towns 9–16 km (6–10 mi) apart. Very few castles. Their neighbours, however, build castles along the halfling border.

### Orcs and other "horde" peoples

Do not draw them as mindless. Model them on real steppe societies, which had laws, crafts, trade and some of the best logistics of the Middle Ages.

**How steppe societies worked:**
- **Herds:** life rested on sheep (meat, milk, wool, fuel) and horses (milk, riding, war). Each warrior rode with several horses, so he could change to a fresh one. Estimates vary: 3–4 horses per Mongol soldier, or 5–8 per steppe warrior (Devereaux).
- **Few people, much land:** density was under 1–2 per km². In 1206 the whole Mongol army probably numbered under 100,000 men (Timothy May, cited by Devereaux).
- **Seasonal movement:** camps moved in a regular yearly cycle, usually between summer pastures (uplands or open steppe) and sheltered winter camps (river valleys).
- **Dependence on farmers:** nomads needed grain, metal and cloth that they could not produce. They got them by trade or by raiding (Khazanov 1984). Ming China refused the Mongol leader Altan Khan's requests for trade for years, and he raided instead; in 1550 he even burned the suburbs of Beijing. In 1571 the Ming gave him a title and trading rights, and border horse markets opened at Datong, Xuanfu and other garrisons. After that, raiding fell sharply.
- **Cities too:** nomad rulers built capitals and trade towns. Karakorum was built in the 1220s–1230s. Batu founded Sarai on the lower Volga in the 1240s. The word "horde" comes from *ordu*, the ruler's camp and court.

**What it looks like:** open grassland with seasonal camp symbols; winter camps along rivers; a royal camp or capital; raid trails into farmland (like the Muravsky Trail used by Crimean Tatar raiders into Muscovy); frontier markets and defence lines on the farmers' side.

> **Rule of thumb:** If a horde raids constantly, ask why the frontier markets are closed. Raids and trade are two ways of getting the same goods.

### Merfolk: coastal trade

**Real model:** the Orang Laut ("sea people") of the Strait of Malacca. For centuries they served the trading states of Srivijaya and later Malacca as pilots, fishermen and a naval force. They kept the straits safe for friendly ships and dangerous for others.

**What it looks like:** markets at sheltered bays and river mouths where land and sea peoples meet. The sea people sell fish, pearls, coral and salvage. The land people sell metal, grain, timber and cloth. A land realm allied with merfolk controls the nearby strait. An enemy has to fight for every harbour.

### Giants and dragons: no-go zones and tribute

**Tribute:** a power you cannot defeat, you pay. England paid the Vikings huge sums in silver: 10,000 pounds in 991, 16,000 in 994, 36,000 in 1007 and 48,000 in 1012 (*Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*). A dragon that takes tribute creates a belt of paying villages, an official who collects the tribute, and perhaps a treaty stone or shrine where it is delivered.

**Food:** a big predator needs a big range. Here is a rough example. Suppose a dragon eats one cow a week, or 52 a year. A herd can lose perhaps 10–20% of its animals a year without shrinking (an assumption). The dragon then needs a herd of about 250–500 cattle. That is the cattle of many villages. A Domesday plough-team, for comparison, had eight oxen.

**No-go zones:** around a lair, draw an empty ring, then a thin belt of tribute villages, then normal land. Giants in the mountains work the same way. Their lands look like a dangerous frontier, with watchtowers on the human side and ruined farms in between.

> **Map tip:** Give each non-human people its own density and pattern. Then draw the **edges**: gate towns for dwarves, trading stones for elves, frontier markets for nomads, shore markets for merfolk, tribute villages for dragons. The edges are where most of the story happens.

## Contact zones between peoples

> **Rule of thumb:** Peoples who distrust each other trade at fixed places, under fixed rules, near the edge of one realm. Draw those places.

### Trading posts and merchant compounds

| Real example | Dates | How it worked | Fantasy use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peterhof, the Hanseatic *kontor* (trading post) in Novgorod | c. 1200–1494 | A palisaded compound of wooden houses around a stone church of St Peter, which was also the warehouse. About 150–200 German merchants per season, arriving as summer and winter groups. They lived under their own written rules (the *Schra*) | A dwarf or human merchant compound inside an elven or orc town |
| Fondaco dei Tedeschi, Venice | From 1228 | A building beside the Rialto where German merchants had to lodge and store goods, so Venice could watch and tax them. The word comes from the Arabic *funduq* (an inn and warehouse) | A "strangers' inn" where foreign races must stay |
| Galata (Pera), the Genoese colony across the harbour from Constantinople | 1267/1273–1453 | A self-governing colony with its own governor. The Genoese walled it despite a ban, and built the Galata Tower in 1348 | A foreign quarter that becomes a fortress |
| Caffa (Kaffa), the Genoese port in Crimea | 1266–1475 | Founded with the consent of the Golden Horde (the Mongol khanate that ruled the steppe). It traded grain, furs, slaves, silk and spices between the steppe and the Mediterranean. Some sources claim over 70,000 people, but figures for medieval Caffa are very uncertain and probably exaggerated; treat it as a large port town | A human port on the edge of orc or nomad lands |

Other kontors stood in London (the Steelyard), Bruges and Bergen (Bryggen, which closed in 1754).

### Frontier markets

- **China's frontier markets:** from the Song dynasty (960–1279), China traded tea for Tibetan horses at fixed markets in Sichuan and Gansu. The Ming briefly opened horse markets for the Mongols in 1551 but soon closed them again, and raids went on. In 1571 lasting border markets opened at Ming garrisons such as Datong and Xuanfu.
- **Silent trade:** for peoples who will not meet (see [Elves](#elves-forest-realms)).

Frontier markets sit **at a garrison or gate**, often open only on fixed days, with officials, interpreters and a strong guard.

### Mixed cities

Real mixed cities kept quarters. Toledo after its conquest by Castile in 1085 had Christians, Muslims and Jews. Norman Palermo (from 1072) had Latin, Greek and Arabic speakers. Many cities had a Jewish quarter and foreign merchant quarters, often walled or gated. In a fantasy city, give each people its own quarter with its own temple, market and gate, and put the shared market in the middle.

### Borders between peoples

The Anglo-Scottish border is a good model for a tense border between two peoples. In 1249, six English and six Scottish knights wrote down the "laws and customs of the march" (the border zone). Later agreements (1398) called for monthly "days of truce" where wardens met to settle crimes across the border. The meeting places were fixed: fords, streams and border villages such as Kershopefoot and Redden Burn. For border types in general, see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md#borders-and-frontiers).

> **Fantasy twist:** A shared magical language, or telepathy, removes the need for interpreters and silent trade. It does not remove the need for rules, guards and fixed meeting places. Distrust, not language, is what makes contact zones.

> **Map tip:** Along each border between peoples, mark: two or three frontier markets at gates or fords; one treaty or truce meeting place; a merchant compound in the largest town on each side; and a mixed town where the main trade route crosses the border.

## Gods, undead and dangerous seas

### Gods with real power

**Real baseline:** the medieval Church was already a huge landowner. In England in 1086, bishops and abbeys held about a quarter (25–26%) of the land, measured by value. The Peace of God movement (first at Charroux in 989) declared churches, clergy and peasants off-limits to fighting. Later councils added pilgrims and merchants. The Truce of God (first at Toulouges in 1027) at first banned fighting on Sundays. Later truces stretched the ban from Wednesday evening to Monday morning, and to holy seasons. These rules were often broken. A god who **enforced** them would change the map:

- **Sanctuaries become real safe zones.** Expect villages and markets clustered around temples, and roads under divine protection busier than other roads.
- **Temple towns grow at sacred sites,** even where trade does not favour them (see the "why is it here?" test in [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md)). They still need food and water.
- **Temples own more land.** If temples hold a third or half of the realm, draw temple estates and granges across the best farmland.
- **Theocracies (states ruled by priests or religious orders) appear.** Real church states existed: the Papal States, and the Teutonic Order's state in Prussia.

Pilgrimage roads, hospices and shrines are covered in [Religious, Cultural, Legal and Ancient Sites](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md).

### Undead, plagues and blights

A spreading undead curse or a magical blight works like the Black Death: 30–60% of people dead, towns too small for their walls, and, over the next 150 years, about 1,500 or more deserted villages in England alone. Real societies responded with isolation:

- **Quarantine:** in 1377 Ragusa (Dubrovnik) made travellers from plague areas spend 30 days on the islet of Mrkan or in the town of Cavtat before entering. The word "quarantine" comes from the later 40-day period (Italian *quaranta*, forty).
- **Plague hospitals on islands:** Venice opened its first *lazaretto* (plague hospital) on a lagoon island in 1423.
- **Outsiders outside:** leper houses stood outside town gates.

**What changes on the map:** if the dead can rise, graveyards move outside the walls and get their own walls and watch. Expect quarantine stations on roads and islands, guard posts at the edge of blighted land, burned or salted villages, and a "dead land" that slowly returns to forest.

### Dangerous seas

If sea monsters or storms make the sea too risky, trade goes inland, even though water transport is otherwise cheaper.

- **Ming China:** grain for Beijing and the northern armies once went partly by sea, a route that suffered from storms and pirates. In 1411–1415 the Ming state rebuilt the Grand Canal with about 165,000 workers. When it reopened in 1415, sea transport of grain to the north ended. Thousands of canal barges (about 15,000 ships, by one count) carried the grain instead. The canal was slower but safer and easier to control. A dangerous sea creates canals and river ports.
- **Convoys:** Venice sent its long-distance trade in state-organised galley convoys (*mude*) from 1315 to 1533. The ships left in spring and came back in autumn. The Flanders convoy carried spices and cotton to England and Flanders and brought wool cloth home. A dangerous sea creates convoy ports, escort fleets and fixed sailing seasons.
- **Coast-hugging:** ships stay near shore and stop each night. A galley averaged about 55 km (34 mi) a day. Expect harbours of refuge about a day's sail apart.

**What changes on the map:** more canals and portages (places where boats or goods are carried overland between two waterways); fortified harbours with chains across the mouth; coastal towns set back from the shore (as on the raided Mediterranean coasts; see [Coasts](01-settlement-placement.md#coasts)); lighthouses and watchtowers on headlands; and a few great convoy ports instead of many small ones.

> **Map tip:** For each of these three, mark the **new places** they create: shrines and sanctuary towns for gods; walled graveyards, quarantine islands and blighted zones for undead; canals, convoy ports and fortified harbours for dangerous seas. Then mark the **places they destroy**: ruins, empty coasts, abandoned villages.

## Consistency checklist

If something exists in your world, other things must exist or change too. Check each line that applies.

- **If monsters roam the countryside,** then there are fewer lone farms, more walls, watchtowers, ranger lodges and cleared roads. Total population is lower than on a safe map.
- **If the whole world is "points of light",** then most towns are small (about 1,500–4,000 people). Larger towns exist only where water brings food safely.
- **If fertility magic exists,** then density is higher and towns are bigger. Big cities still sit on water, unless magic also moves grain.
- **If healing magic exists,** then the frontier fills fast, there are more new towns and land wars, and fewer ruins from plagues.
- **If water magic makes desert cities,** then whoever makes the water holds power, and the city dies if the magic stops.
- **If portals exist,** then each portal has a town, a customs house and a guard. The capital is connected to them, or is in the wrong place.
- **If flying mounts exist,** then castles have roofed towers, lords keep aeries with food animals nearby, and messages are fast. Bulk freight still uses rivers.
- **If weather magic exists,** then someone controls it and takes payment, and famines are rare unless the weather-mages are at war.
- **If magic crystals exist,** then there are boom towns, mining law, mints and fortresses at the deposits, and ghost towns where deposits ran out.
- **If dwarves live under mountains,** then grain flows up to them through gate towns, and metal flows down.
- **If elves rule a forest,** then the forest has few roads, a wide border and a few fixed trading places.
- **If nomads raid farmland,** then there are frontier markets (or the reason they are closed), defence lines and burned villages near the steppe.
- **If dragons exist,** then there are lairs with empty land around them, tribute arrangements, and lost herds.
- **If gods enforce sanctuary,** then temples are rich and towns cluster around them.
- **If the dead can rise,** then graveyards are outside the walls and guarded, and there are quarantine places.
- **If the sea is deadly,** then canals and convoy ports replace many small harbours.
- **If two peoples meet,** then there are markets, strangers' inns or compounds, and agreed meeting places on the border.

> **Map tip:** After finishing the map, read down this list and check every "if" that applies to your world. Most fantasy maps fail one or two lines, usually the food supply of a magical city or the missing edge where two peoples meet. More common errors are in [Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them](11-common-mistakes.md).

## Quick summary

- Apply the real rules first. Then ask which input each fantasy element changes: water, food, defence, travel cost, threats or resources.
- Danger concentrates people: fewer lone farms, walled hilltop villages, refuge forts about a day's walk (24–32 km, 15–20 mi) apart, watchtowers, cleared roads.
- "Points of light" describes a frontier. Each point, if fields stay within 4–5 km (2.5–3 mi) of the walls, holds about 1,500–4,000 people.
- Fertility magic raises the population ceiling (treat a strongly blessed land like Flanders: 50–75 people per km²). Healing raises the growth rate, not the ceiling.
- Portals act like harbours and straits: hubs, tolls, warehouses, forts. Their capacity compared with a ship (a cog carried 30–200 tons) decides whether they change bulk trade.
- Flying mounts carry about a pack horse's load (100–120 kg). They change messages, travel and luxury trade, not bulk freight, and they force castles to defend against attack from above.
- Magic crystals are ore (boom towns, forts, mints). Ley lines are rivers (chains of towers, a big city at the nexus).
- Place academies like universities (big cities, a few per kingdom) and mage towers like monasteries (remote, but with farms).
- Model each non-human people on a real way of life: dwarves on mining towns, elves on Baltic forest peoples, halflings on free peasant lowlands, orcs on steppe nomads with real logistics, merfolk on sea nomads, dragons on tribute-taking raiders.
- Peoples meet at fixed places under fixed rules: kontors, strangers' inns, frontier markets, truce days.
- Gods, undead and deadly seas each create new places (sanctuaries, quarantine islands, canals) and destroy others (empty coasts, deserted villages).
