# Castles, Forts and Military Outposts

Castles are the most "fantasy" feature on a medieval map, and also the easiest to get wrong. Real castles were not scattered at random. Each one controlled something: a road, a river crossing, a town, a border or an estate. This chapter explains what fortifications were for, the main types and their dates, where they were built, how many there were and how far apart, how big their garrisons and armies were, how armies moved, camped and were fed, and how to draw all of this on your map.

## What fortifications are for

> **Rule of thumb:** Every fortification answers one question: "What does it control?" If you cannot name the road, crossing, town, border or estate it watches, move it or delete it.

A castle was not only a military building. It was the lord's house, his office, his court, his prison, his store for rents paid in grain, and a barracks. Many historians (for example Charles Coulson) stress that walls and battlements were also a **status symbol**. A castle said "a lord rules here".

| Purpose | What it looks like | Typical site | Real example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control a route | A castle or tower right beside a pass, ford, bridge or narrow river | The narrowest point of the route | Krak des Chevaliers (Syria) guarded the Homs Gap between the coast at Tripoli and inland Homs. Château Gaillard (1196–98) guarded the Seine valley road to Rouen. |
| Protect a town or port | Town walls and gates, often with a castle or citadel at one corner | Around the town; the castle on the highest point or by the river | Carcassonne (two walls, about 3 km / 1.9 mi); Ávila (about 2.5 km / 1.6 mi); Visby (3.4 km / 2.1 mi) |
| Hold a border | A chain of castles and towers, much denser than inland | Along the border, at every crossing | The Welsh March; the Arab–Byzantine frontier (the *thughur*); the Christian–Muslim frontier in Spain |
| Dominate conquered land | Big new castles in or beside the main towns, often with new settler towns | Inside existing towns, at ports, on rivers | William I's castles in English towns after 1066; Edward I's castles in North Wales (1277–1295); the Teutonic Order in Prussia (from 1231) |
| Lord's home and admin centre | A castle at the head manor (the *caput*) of a barony | Near the best farmland, a village and a church | Most of the 500–600 castles occupied at any one time in post-Conquest England |
| Refuge | Fortified churches, tower houses, walled villages, old hillforts | Inside the village | Over 150 Saxon villages in Transylvania with fortified churches (13th–16th centuries) |
| Watch and signal | Watchtowers, beacons, coastal towers | Hilltops and headlands with long views | The Byzantine beacon line across Asia Minor (9th century) |

> **Map tip:** Before you place a castle, write down its job in one word: *road*, *bridge*, *town*, *border*, *estate*, *refuge* or *watch*. The job tells you the site. A "road" castle sits on the road; an "estate" castle sits in the middle of good farmland; a "border" castle sits on the border.

## Types of fortification

### Castles: from earth to stone

| Type | Dates | What it is | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ringwork | 10th–12th centuries | A ring of earth bank and ditch with a timber fence (palisade) on top. No mound. | Common in south-west England and south Wales after 1066 |
| Motte-and-bailey | From c. 1000 in the Loire valley and Normandy; in England after 1066 | A **motte** (a man-made earth mound) with a timber tower on top, and a **bailey** (a fenced yard) beside it for halls, stables and stores | About 80% of Norman castles in England were of this type. Mottes were about 3–30 m (10–100 ft) high and 30–90 m (100–300 ft) across at the base. A bailey usually covered about 0.15–0.7 ha (0.4–1.7 acres): The Mount, Ilketshall St John (Suffolk), has a bailey platform of 53 × 30 m; Ewyas Harold (Herefordshire) has a bailey of about 0.7 ha. A large baronial castle such as Castle Acre (Norfolk) covered about 3 ha (7.5 acres) to the outer edge of its defences. See [Footprints](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md#footprints-how-big-to-draw-them-on-a-local-map). An average motte, 5 m (16 ft) high and 15 m (49 ft) across at the top, is estimated to have taken 50 people about 40 working days. The biggest mottes took up to about 24,000 working days. |
| Stone keep (great tower, *donjon*) | From c. 950 in France; in England from the 1070s | A massive stone tower holding the lord's hall and rooms | Doué-la-Fontaine (France, c. 950, often called the oldest known keep); the White Tower, London (c. 1075–1100); Dover's great tower (1180s) |
| Shell keep | Mostly 11th–12th centuries | A stone ring wall replacing the timber fence on top of a motte, because a mound cannot carry a heavy tower | Windsor (Henry II); Restormel (1270s–80s); Lewes; Launceston |
| Curtain-wall castle | Late 12th–13th centuries | A strong stone wall (the **curtain**) with towers along it that let defenders shoot along the wall face | Framlingham (late 1100s, finished by 1213; 13 wall towers and no keep); Château Gaillard (1196–98, taken 1204) |
| Concentric castle | Byzantine examples from the 11th century; crusader examples from the 1160s; Wales 1268–1295 | Two or more rings of walls. The inner wall is higher, so both rings can shoot at once. | Belvoir (Hospitallers, from 1168); Krak des Chevaliers (Hospitaller from 1142; its outer ring of walls was added in the early 1200s); Caerphilly (begun 1268); Harlech (1282–89, cost £8,190); Beaumaris (begun 1295, never finished) |

Stone castles were slow and costly. Stone needed skilled masons, and they had to be paid. A keep's walls usually rose no more than about 3.7 m (12 ft) a year, so a great tower took around ten years (the keep at Scarborough is a typical case). Edward I spent at least £80,000 on his Welsh castles between 1277 and 1304. For Beaumaris alone his master mason asked for 400 masons, 2,000 labourers, 100 carts, 60 wagons and 30 boats. Earth-and-timber castles, by contrast, could go up in a few weeks or months, within a single building season, using unpaid local labour. That is why invaders built them first.

### Small fortified homes

| Type | Dates | What it is | Real examples and numbers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tower house | 14th–17th centuries | A tall stone house of 3–6 storeys for one family and its servants, often with a walled yard (*barmkin* in Scotland, *bawn* in Ireland). Built where raiding was common and the king was far away. | Ireland: over 3,000 built (more than 2,000 still stand). In 1429 the English crown offered £10 to any man in the Pale (the English-ruled area around Dublin) who built one, though recent studies doubt this made much difference. Scotland about 800. England over 250, mostly near the Scottish border. Also common in the Basque lands, Italy and the Caucasus. |
| Peel tower and bastle house | Peels 14th–17th centuries; bastles mostly 16th–17th centuries | A **peel** is a small tower house on the Anglo-Scottish border. A **bastle** is a fortified farmhouse: animals below, family above. | Northumberland, Cumbria and the Scottish Borders |
| Fortified manor house | 13th–15th centuries | A manor house with a moat, gatehouse and battlements. Strong enough to stop a raid, not an army. | England has about 460 surviving **licences to crenellate** (royal permission to add battlements) for just over 500 sites, mostly for knights. Stokesay (1291), Cooling (1381), Bodiam (1385), Herstmonceux (1441). |

### Community defences

| Type | Dates | What it is | Real examples and numbers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Town walls and gates | All periods; a big wave in the 12th–14th centuries | Stone wall or earth bank and ditch, with towers and gatehouses. Gates also collected tolls. | Carcassonne about 3 km (1.9 mi); Ávila about 2.5 km (1.6 mi); Visby 3.4 km (2.1 mi). England paid for walls with **murage** (a toll on goods entering a town), first granted to Shrewsbury in 1220; about 1,470 murage grants are recorded in England, Wales, Scotland and France. |
| Citadel | All periods | A fortress inside or at the edge of a city. It defends the city, and also watches it. | Tower of London; Cairo Citadel (begun by Saladin in 1176); Aleppo Citadel (rebuilt c. 1200) |
| Fortified bridge | 12th–14th centuries | A bridge with a gate tower on it, to stop attackers and collect tolls | Pont Valentré, Cahors (1308–78; 138 m / 453 ft long, three towers); Monnow Bridge, Monmouth (bridge traditionally dated 1272, gate tower added c. 1300), the only British river bridge with its gate tower still standing on it |
| Fortified church or monastery | 13th–17th centuries | A church with thick walls, a strong tower and often a ring wall. Villagers took refuge inside. | Transylvania: over 150 villages (13th–16th centuries); Slovenia: about 300 churches fortified against Ottoman raids (late 15th–17th centuries); Albi Cathedral, a brick fortress-church (1282–1480) |
| Reused hillfort or Roman fort | Whenever old walls were handy | Old earthworks or Roman walls given a new castle inside | Old Sarum: an Iron Age hillfort about 400 × 360 m (1,300 × 1,180 ft), a Norman castle by 1069 and a cathedral in 1092, abandoned for New Salisbury from 1220. Pevensey and Portchester: Norman castles built inside Roman forts. |

### Frontier and special types

| Type | Dates | What it is | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crusader castle | 1100–1291 | Large stone castles on spurs and hills, held by small numbers of Western knights among a hostile population | Kerak (1142); Krak des Chevaliers (Hospitaller 1142–1271); Chastel Pèlerin at Atlit (Templar, 1218–1291); Montfort (Teutonic Order, 1220s–1271) |
| Islamic *ribat* | 8th–11th centuries | A fortified lodge for volunteer frontier fighters, with a watch and signal tower. Built in chains along coasts and borders. | Monastir (founded 796) and Sousse (rebuilt or enlarged 821) in Tunisia |
| Islamic frontier fortress | 8th–15th centuries | Big walled fortresses and fortified towns in the frontier zone | Gormaz, Spain (built or rebuilt 965–66 for the caliph of Córdoba; about 1 km / 0.6 mi of walls with 24–27 towers); Tarsus (main Arab base facing Byzantium, 4,000–5,000 troops in its garrison) |
| Military-order castle | 12th–15th centuries | A castle that is also a monastery for knight-monks (an *Ordensburg* in German) | Malbork/Marienburg (begun c. 1274, built over about 130 years; Teutonic Order headquarters from 1309; 21 ha / 52 acres inside its outer walls, the largest castle by area); Calatrava (Order of Calatrava, from 1158) |
| Siege castle (counter-castle) | All periods | A fort built to block an enemy castle or town: either a temporary siege fort close by, housing the besiegers, or a permanent counter-castle within a day's ride | Malregard at Antioch (1097); Mons Peregrinus at Tripoli (1102–03; the city fell only in 1109); four castles ringing Fatimid Ashkelon (Castrum Arnaldi, Beth Gibelin, Ibelin and Blanchegarde, 1133–42; the city fell in 1153). In 12th-century England, siege castles stood about 180–270 m (200–300 yd) from the target, just beyond bow range. |

> **Map tip:** Match the castle type to your map's date. Around 1100, almost every castle is earth and timber. By 1300, the important ones are stone and the small ones are either rebuilt in stone or abandoned. From 1350, add tower houses and fortified manors, and let old castles decay.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Cannon broke tall thin walls (the Ottomans breached Constantinople's walls in 1453). From the late 1400s Italian engineers built walls **lower and thicker**, often of earth faced with brick. They added angled **bastions** (projecting platforms for guns). This *trace italienne* ("Italian outline"), or "star fort", spread across Europe in the 1530s–1540s. A famous example is Palmanova (1593), a whole star-shaped new town. Star forts were very expensive: Siena bankrupted itself on its walls in 1544. Henry VIII's **Device Forts** (1539–47, total cost £376,000) were low, round artillery forts guarding harbours and anchorages, such as Deal and Pendennis. Their garrisons were small: from 9 men (West Tilbury) to 29 (Camber). See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

## Where to put a castle

> **Rule of thumb:** Put the castle where a few men can stop many: at the narrowest point of a route, on the strongest ground next to it, with water nearby.

| Site | Why it works | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| River crossing (ford or bridge) | Every road and every boat must pass | Stirling (lowest crossing of the Forth); Chepstow (begun 1067, on a cliff above the Wye) |
| Mountain pass or gap | The only easy way between two regions | Krak des Chevaliers (Homs Gap); Loulon at the northern exit of the Cilician Gates (Byzantine–Arab frontier) |
| Spur or promontory | A ridge end with cliffs on two or three sides. Cut one ditch across the neck and the castle is strong on all sides. | Château Gaillard above the Seine; Kerak; Montfort |
| River bend (meander) | Water on three sides, a short neck to defend | Durham (castle begun 1072 in a loop of the Wear); Toledo in a loop of the Tagus |
| Confluence of two rivers | Controls traffic on both rivers | Koblenz, where the Moselle meets the Rhine |
| Island or lake shore | Water as a moat; controls river or lakeside road | Pfalzgrafenstein, an island in the Rhine (1326–27); Chillon, on Lake Geneva beside the road to the Great St Bernard Pass |
| Coastal headland or harbour mouth | Sea on several sides; supply by ship; watches the harbour | Dunstanburgh (begun 1313); Harlech, with a water gate down to the shore |
| Hill above a town | Overlooks and protects the town, and watches it | Edinburgh; Prague; Carcassonne |
| Flat wet lowland | No hills, so dig a moat or flood the ground | Caerphilly (artificial lakes); Bodiam (moat); Muiderslot near Amsterdam (c. 1285). These are "water castles" (German *Wasserburg*). |

**Hills or lowlands?** It depends on why the castle was built.
- In the German lands about two-thirds of castles were on high ground (*Höhenburg*, "height castle"). Many lords there built for safety and display on wooded spurs.
- In England most castles stood on low ground, next to villages and towns. Norman castles were built to control people and farmland, so they sat where the people were.

> **Map tip:** Draw the terrain first, then look for the "choke points": fords, bridges, passes, narrow valleys, river bends and harbour mouths. Put a castle at the most important ones. Put estate castles in the middle of good farmland, beside a village. Do not put a castle on a remote peak far from any road or village. Nobody would pay for it.

> **Fantasy twist:** If your world has flying mounts or flying monsters, high spurs lose some value and roofs matter more. Expect covered courtyards, roofed towers, heavy crossbows aimed upward, and aeries (bases for flying mounts) inside the strongest castles. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

## How many castles and how far apart

### Counts and densities

The numbers below count **every castle site ever built** in the period, including small mottes that were used for only a few decades. The column "typical gap" is the square root of the area per castle. It is a rough guide to the distance from one castle to the next.

| Region | Castles | Area per castle | Typical gap to next castle |
|---|---|---|---|
| England and Wales overall | 1,761 sites (Gatehouse gazetteer) | ~86 km² (33 sq mi) | ~9 km (6 mi) |
| Peaceful eastern England (Norfolk, Essex, Kent, Suffolk, Lincolnshire) | 22–45 per county | 130–250 km² (50–95 sq mi) | 11–16 km (7–10 mi) |
| Welsh March (Herefordshire, Shropshire, Monmouthshire, Pembrokeshire, Glamorgan) | 60–127 per county | 17–28 km² (7–11 sq mi) | 4–6 km (2.5–4 mi) |
| North-west Wales (Caernarfonshire), conquered 1283 | 21 | ~71 km² (27 sq mi) | ~8 km (5 mi), but a few very large royal castles |
| Anglo-Scottish border (Northumberland, Cumberland) | 32–46 per county | 110–130 km² (42–50 sq mi) | ~11 km (7 mi), plus hundreds of tower houses not counted here |
| German-speaking lands | ~14,000 | ~35–40 km² (14–15 sq mi) (estimate) | ~6 km (4 mi) |
| Switzerland | ~4,000 (many very small) | ~10 km² (4 sq mi) | ~3 km (2 mi) |
| Teutonic Prussia | Several dozen brick convent castles plus smaller ones | ~370 km² (143 sq mi) (estimate) | ~19 km (12 mi) |
| Livonia (modern Latvia and Estonia) | — | ~790 km² (305 sq mi) (estimate) | ~28 km (17 mi) |
| Western Europe as a whole | 75,000–100,000 built | — | — |

Notes:
- County figures are from the Gatehouse gazetteer (England and Wales; its total area is about 151,000 km² / 58,000 sq mi). The German, Swiss and Western Europe totals follow the guide's shared baseline. The German area per castle assumes German-speaking lands of roughly 500,000–550,000 km², so it is only a rough guide. The Prussia and Livonia figures are uncited estimates quoted on Wikipedia (its Livonia figure also covers Courland and Estonia); treat them as rough.
- **Not all at once.** In England only 500–600 castles were in use at any one time after the Conquest. That is about one-third of all sites. Active castles were therefore about one per 250–300 km² (about 95–115 sq mi), or roughly **16–17 km (10–11 mi) apart**.
- **Castle booms happen in times of weak rule.** In Provence there were 12 castles in 950, 30 by 1000 and over 100 by 1030. England had a civil war in 1139–53 (the "Anarchy"). The chronicler Robert of Torigni claimed that 1,115 unlicensed castles were built during it, but elsewhere he gave a figure of only 126. Expect exaggeration in your own world's chronicles too.

### Control radius

- **Direct military reach:** only about 400 m (1,300 ft). This was a castle's immediate area of influence, roughly the reach of the defenders' bows and crossbows.
- **Practical control:** a castle's mounted men could ride out, patrol and return in one day. That gives a radius of about 15–30 km (10–20 mi). This is a working heuristic, not a measured figure.
- **Why it still matters:** an enemy cannot safely march past an untaken castle. Its garrison can cut his supply line, attack foragers and retake the land behind him. That is why so much medieval war was sieges.

### Borderlands and conquered land

Borders and newly conquered land have far more castles than the peaceful core of a realm. The Welsh March had roughly **5–10 times** as many castle sites per km² as eastern England. (A **march** is a border zone held by local lords with special powers to defend it.)

| Situation | Spacing | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Peaceful core of a kingdom | All sites 11–16 km (7–10 mi) apart; active castles about 20–30 km (12–19 mi) apart (estimate): one per county town plus scattered baronial seats | Eastern England (Norfolk, Essex, Suffolk) |
| March or contested border (many small lords) | 4–6 km (2.5–4 mi) apart; small castles, many short-lived | Welsh March, 1070s–1280s |
| Conquest chain along a coast or river | 20–40 km (12–25 mi) apart, about one day's march; each castle reachable by water | Edward I's coastal castles in Wales, from Flint to Aberystwyth: about 18–50 km (11–31 mi) apart in a straight line, most about 18–25 km (11–16 mi), all supplied by sea. The Teutonic Order's castle-towns along the Vistula (Toruń 1231, Chełmno, Grudziądz, Kwidzyn, Malbork, Elbląg 1237): 25–40 km (15–25 mi) apart. |
| Ring around a hostile city | About 25–50 km (15–30 mi) from the city and 10–25 km (6–15 mi) from each other, all within a day's ride of it | The four crusader castles around Fatimid Ashkelon (1133–42), which blocked its raiders until the city fell in 1153. Three of them stood about 25–32 km (15–20 mi) from the city; the fourth, Castrum Arnaldi, was nearer 45 km (28 mi) away. |
| Thinly settled frontier zone | Fortress towns 25–100 km (15–60 mi) apart, with open land between | The Arab *thughur* facing Byzantium (Tarsus, Adana, Mopsuestia, Malatya); the Duero frontier in 10th-century Spain |

> **Rule of thumb:** Peaceful core: a working castle every 20–30 km (12–19 mi). Contested border: one every 4–6 km (2.5–4 mi). Conquest chain: one every day's march (20–40 km, 12–25 mi) along the main road, river or coast.

> **Map tip:** Do not spread castles evenly. Cluster them along borders, at river crossings and around rich towns. Leave the safe middle of the kingdom fairly empty: a castle in each county town and a few baronial seats. On a frontier, draw a line of castles along the road or river the invaders used, about a day's march apart.

## Castles and towns

> **Rule of thumb:** Castles and towns come in pairs. A lord who builds a castle wants a market and rents beside it; a town wants protection. Where one exists, look for the other.

Three common patterns:

1. **Castle planted in an existing town.** After 1066 the Normans built most royal castles inside or beside English towns, to control them. They demolished houses to make room: 166 at Lincoln, 113 at Norwich, 27 at Cambridge (Domesday Book).
2. **New town planted beside a new castle.** This is the conqueror's method. Edward I built Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech and Beaumaris each with a new English borough beside it. At Conwy a royal charter of 1284 made the castle's constable (the officer in charge of the castle) also the town's mayor. The Teutonic Order did the same in Prussia: castle first, then a chartered town under its walls. Many of these towns used Kulm law, a model town charter first issued for Chełmno (Kulm) in 1233.
3. **Village gathered around a castle.** In Italy many villages moved into fortified hilltop sites (*incastellamento*). In south-west France nearly 700 planned new towns (*bastides*) were founded in 1222–1372, many walled later. See [Where Settlements Are Built](01-settlement-placement.md).

**How they sit together on the ground:**
- The castle is at the **edge** of the town, not the middle: usually at the highest point, at a corner of the town wall, or by the river or bridge.
- The castle has its own gate to the outside. The lord could bring in troops even if the town rebelled.
- The town grows along the road leading from the castle gate, with a market place in front of the gate.
- In a capital, the pattern is often "castle on the hill, merchant town below" (see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md)).

> **Map tip:** On a local map, draw the castle on the high ground or river bank at one corner of the town wall, with the market street running away from its gate. On a kingdom map, nearly every town of 2,000+ people should have a castle, a citadel or at least walls (see [Population and Settlement Sizes](02-population-and-sizes.md)).

## Garrisons and armies

### Garrisons

> **Rule of thumb:** Garrisons were tiny. The walls did the work. A castle with 30–60 men could often hold off an army ten or more times bigger for weeks or months, if it had food and water.

| Situation | Garrison | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| Peacetime caretaker | 5–20 | A constable (the officer in charge), a porter, watchmen and a few men-at-arms (armoured professional soldiers) |
| New frontier castle in peacetime | 30–60 | Harlech 1284: 36 (a constable, 30 soldiers of whom 10 crossbowmen, a chaplain, a smith, a carpenter and a mason). Conwy 1284: 30 soldiers, 15 of them crossbowmen, plus a chaplain and craftsmen. Caernarfon 1284: 40. |
| Small castle under siege | 20–150 | Caernarfon in 1403–04: a garrison of only about 30 men held out against Welsh besiegers of Owain Glyndŵr, who had French help. |
| Great castle in a major war | 150 to about 1,200 | Dover 1216: 140 knights plus many more soldiers held out against the French prince Louis. Kenilworth 1266: a garrison usually estimated at about 1,200 held out for about six months (June to December). |
| Great crusader fortress | 1,500–2,200 | Krak des Chevaliers: about 2,000 at its peak, but only about 60 knights. Safed (rebuilt by the Templars 1240–43, described c. 1260): 1,700 in peace and 2,200 in war, of whom only 50 were knights. |
| Frontier fortress city | 4,000–5,000 | Tarsus, the main Arab base facing Byzantium. In early Abbasid times (late 700s and early 800s) the whole Arab frontier zone held about 25,000 troops. |

Most people in a big garrison were not knights. They were crossbowmen, sergeants, servants, craftsmen and (in the East) slaves and workers.

### Field armies and population

| Campaign | Army (modern estimates) | Realm population | Share of population |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hastings, 1066 | William: 7,000–8,000 (some say 10,000–12,000). Harold: 5,000–13,000, most say 7,000–8,000. | England 1.5–2.0 million | ~0.4% (Harold) |
| Falkirk, 1298 | Edward I: about 15,000 (about 2,000–2,500 cavalry and about 12,000 paid foot soldiers, many of them Welsh). Some estimates are lower. | England and Wales ~5 million | ~0.3% |
| Crécy, 1346 | English: 7,000–15,000 (Ayton: ~14,000) | England ~4.8 million | ~0.3% |
| Siege of Calais, 1347 | English: a peak of about 32,000 in July 1347, the largest English army sent overseas before 1600 | England ~4.8 million | ~0.7%, for a few weeks |
| Agincourt, 1415 | English: about 6,000–9,000 (Curry: about 8,500–9,000). French: about 12,000 (Curry) or 14,000–15,000 (Rogers, Barker, Sumption); up to ~24,000 if armed servants are counted | England ~2–2.5 million; France well over 10 million | English ~0.3–0.4%; French under 0.3% |

> **Rule of thumb:** A medieval realm puts about 0.2–0.5% of its people into a field army. About 1% is an all-out effort that cannot last. A kingdom of 4 million people fields 10,000–20,000 soldiers, not 100,000. Chroniclers often claimed much bigger numbers; modern counts from pay records are usually several times smaller.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Armies grew fast. Gunpowder forts needed bigger garrisons, and states began to keep permanent troops in **garrison towns**. Field armies of 20,000–50,000 became common in major wars of the 1500s and 1600s.

> **Map tip:** You do not draw garrisons, but they tell you what a castle is. On a kingdom map, an ordinary castle is a post of a few dozen men, not a barracks for thousands. Put big troop numbers only in two or three great frontier fortresses and the capital's citadel. If you label armies or invasion routes, keep the sizes realistic: about 0.2–0.5% of the realm's people. A kingdom of 2 million fields about 4,000–10,000 soldiers.

## Military geography and logistics

### Speed and season

Army speeds follow the guide's shared baseline (details in [Trade Routes, Roads and Transport](05-trade-routes-and-transport.md)):

| Mode | Per day |
|---|---|
| Large army with baggage | 13–20 km (8–12 mi) |
| Army living off the land | 10–26 km (6–16 mi) |
| Forced march, a few days only | 30–55 km (20–35 mi). Harold's march north to Yorkshire in 1066 covered about 300 km (185 mi) in perhaps 5–7 days (often said to be only four), about 43–60 km (27–37 mi) a day, probably partly mounted. |
| Mounted force with spare horses | 55–65 km (35–40 mi) |

**Campaign season.** Most campaigns ran from late spring, when there was grass for the horses, until after the harvest in autumn. Winter campaigns were rare. A vassal usually owed only **40 days** of military service a year, so feudal armies melted away during long sieges. There were exceptions: the Teutonic Order often raided Lithuania in winter, because then the rivers and marshes were frozen and could be crossed. Galley fleets mostly stayed in port in winter.

### Food and fodder

- A soldier needs about 1–1.5 kg (2–3 lb) of food a day, mostly bread or grain.
- A horse needs about 4.5 kg (10 lb) of grain a day if it can also graze. Without grazing it needs roughly double that, about 9–10 kg (20–22 lb) of grain and hay. On summer grass it can graze for much of its needs. Otherwise, hay and oats must be carried.
- An army of 10,000 men and 4,000 horses therefore needs about 10–15 tonnes of food and 18–40 tonnes of fodder **every day**. That is very roughly 30–100 cartloads a day (an estimate).
- A moving army can take only about 5–15% of the food in the country it passes through and still keep moving (Devereaux's estimate). So armies **keep moving**. An army that stops, for example for a siege, eats out the surrounding land within weeks.

> **Rule of thumb:** Armies follow rivers and coasts. Bulk supply came by boat, so invasion routes, siege camps and frontier castles cluster along navigable water. Edward I placed every one of his royal castles in North Wales where ships could reach it. In 1294–95 Harlech was resupplied from Ireland by sea through its water gate.

### Military sites behind the front

| Site | What it is | Where to put it | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Muster point | Where the army gathers before a campaign | A royal town or castle near the frontier, on a main road or river, with grazing land around it | English armies gathered at Chester for North Wales, at Newcastle, Carlisle or Berwick for Scotland, and at Portsmouth or Southampton for France |
| Armoury and royal store | Weapons, armour, arrows, siege engines, tents | In the capital's citadel, or a major castle on the main route to the frontier | The Tower of London was the English crown's main armoury and store |
| Arsenal and naval base | A state shipyard that builds, stores and fits out war galleys | A sheltered harbour or river port in or next to the capital or main port | Venice Arsenal (traditionally founded in 1104, though the exact date is uncertain; enlarged from 1320 and again in 1473; about 45 ha / 110 acres, roughly 15% of the city; up to 16,000 workers claimed for the early 1500s). Barcelona's royal shipyard, the Drassanes (ordered in 1285, enlarged 1328–90). The French royal galley yard at Rouen, the Clos des Galées (founded by Philip IV from c. 1292–94). |
| Invasion port | Where a fleet gathers and sails, and where it lands | The nearest good harbour to the enemy coast, with roads behind it to bring troops and food | 1066: William's fleet sailed from Saint-Valery-sur-Somme and landed at Pevensey. 1346: Edward III sailed from Portsmouth and landed at Saint-Vaast-la-Hougue. 1415: Henry V sailed from Southampton to Harfleur. Calais, taken in 1347, stayed an English bridgehead until 1558. |

> **Map tip:** For each realm, mark one or two muster towns near each threatened border, one main armoury (usually the capital), and one naval base if it has a coast. Draw the obvious invasion route between two rival realms: the shortest sea crossing or the easiest river valley. Then fortify both ends of it.

> **Fantasy twist:** Magic that feeds armies (conjured food, bottomless wagons) removes the river rule. Armies could then march straight across dry land and besiege for months. That one change would make castles far less useful. If you allow it, make it rare and costly. See [Fantasy Variants](10-fantasy-variants.md).

## Camps and siege towns

> **Rule of thumb:** An army that stays in one place for more than a few weeks turns its camp into a town, with streets, a market, workshops and a defended edge, all fed by boat or by the main road. Most of these camps vanish when the war ends. A few become real towns.

**Why camps matter.** A field army of 10,000–30,000 people (see [Garrisons and armies](#garrisons-and-armies)) is bigger than almost every town in its own kingdom. When it stops for a long siege or for the winter, it needs shelter, food markets, smiths, carpenters and stables. It also needs a defended edge, because it can be attacked from the besieged town and by a relief army from outside. Merchants, servants and other camp followers come with it. So a long siege camp looks and works like a town of timber, thatch and canvas. Like the castles in [Military geography and logistics](#military-geography-and-logistics), it sits where boats or a main road can supply it.

### Kinds of camp

| Kind | How long it lasts | What it looks like | Size | Real examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overnight marching camp | One night to a few days | Roman armies: rows of tents inside a bank and ditch. Medieval armies: tents and rough shelters, usually with only light defences | Roman camps from under 1 ha to 67 ha (165 acres); most 10–18 ha (25–45 acres) | Roman camps in Scotland, a day's march apart along the campaign roads |
| Winter camp | One winter, several months | Naturally strong ground beside a river, with boats drawn up, workshops and trading | Torksey: at least 55 ha (136 acres) | The Viking "Great Army" at Torksey, England, winter 872–73 |
| Siege camp-town | Months, up to about a year | Timber houses and huts around the besieged town, a market, stables, a fenced or ditched edge, and the commander's own quarters | For an army of about 10,000–30,000 | Calais 1346–47; Neuss 1474–75 |
| Permanent siege town | Built to last | A small walled town on a planned grid, built to show the enemy that the besiegers will not leave | Santa Fe: about 335 × 260 m (1,100 × 850 ft), about 9 ha (22 acres) | Santa Fe near Granada, 1491 |

### Camps that became towns (or nearly did)

- **Torksey, 872–73 (England; earlier than this guide's baseline period).** The Viking Great Army spent the winter on higher ground beside the River Trent, partly surrounded by marsh. The camp covered at least 55 ha (136 acres), bigger than York at the time, and held several thousand people, including warriors, craftworkers and traders. They repaired boats, melted looted silver and gold into ingots, made and traded goods. After the army left, Torksey grew into an important Anglo-Saxon *burh* (fortified town) with a pottery industry. Archaeologists see the camp as a trigger for the later town.
- **Calais, 1346–47 (northern France).** Edward III besieged Calais for 11 months (4 September 1346 to 3 August 1347). His army built a temporary town on firm ground in the marsh, along the causeway road about 800 m (half a mile) from the walls. They called it *Villeneuve-la-Hardie* ("Bold New Town"). It had timber halls for the king and nobles, market halls, stables and thousands of huts of brushwood and thatch. Markets were held twice a week, and observers said its meat and cloth markets looked like those of Arras or Amiens. At its height it held more people than any English town except London. Supplies came by sea: 853 ships with 24,000 sailors served the siege at one time or another. It did not survive as a town: once Calais fell, Edward expelled the French townspeople and settled English colonists inside the captured city instead.
- **Neuss, 1474–75 (Rhineland, Germany).** Charles the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, surrounded Neuss, a fortified town of about 4,000 people, with an army of about 14,000 (estimates run from 13,000 to 20,000). He expected a siege of a few days or weeks. It lasted about ten months, from 29 July 1474 until a relief army sent by the Emperor and peace talks ended it in late May and June 1475. By mid-August 1474 the town was completely shut in. Charles kept his court in the camp in great luxury. His courtier Olivier de La Marche is quoted as calling the camp "like a city", with craftsmen, merchants, barbers, inns and taverns, and even windmills. Disease still spread through it.
- **Santa Fe, 1491 (near Granada, Spain).** In the last campaign against the Muslim kingdom of Granada, Ferdinand and Isabella set up their royal camp in April 1491 on the open plain (the *Vega*), about 10 km (6 mi) west of the city and within sight of its walls. They began building a real walled town almost at once. In July 1491 a fire destroyed much of the tent camp. Older accounts say the town was built only after the fire, but recent research shows building had already begun. Chroniclers say the work took about eighty days. In October 1491 the royal court's historian, Peter Martyr, described it as nearly rectangular, about 400 by 312 paces (about 335 × 260 m, 1,100 × 850 ft), with battlemented walls, moats and towers, a square in the middle and a gate in each of the four sides. Two main streets crossed at that square, and the moat was filled from the plain's irrigation channels. Granada agreed to surrender in November 1491. In 1492 the camp became a town (*villa*), and its land was shared out among about 200 settler households. Santa Fe still exists; some of its gates still stand, and the old grid can still be seen.

### Roman marching camps as old earthworks

Roman armies on campaign built a defended camp at the end of each day's march. On a medieval map these camps are part of the older layer of the landscape: low banks in moorland or marks in fields (see [Religious, Cultural, Legal and Ancient Sites](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md)).

- **How many:** nearly 500 are recorded in Britain, almost half of them in Scotland. No other Roman province has so many known, mostly because of aerial photography.
- **Shape:** the classic "playing-card" plan, a rectangle with rounded corners, made of one earth bank (**rampart**) and an outer ditch. Many real camps are less regular.
- **Gates:** most often 4 or 6, sometimes fewer (the Roman writer Hyginus describes a model camp with six). Each gate was a gap in the bank, usually guarded by a short bank and ditch set in front of it (a *titulus*), so attackers could not rush straight in.
- **Where:** beside Roman roads and forts, often just after a river crossing. Near some frontier forts, at river crossings and road junctions, are "gathering grounds" where armies camped again and again before a campaign.
- **Spacing:** camps built on the same campaign lie about a day's march apart: in Scotland mostly 11–24 km (7–15 mi), less across hills.

| Camp size | Area | Roughly how wide (estimate) | Examples (Scotland) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small | 1–5 ha (2.5–12 acres) | 100–220 m (330–720 ft) | Pennymuir II, 3.9 ha (9.7 acres) |
| Common | 10–18 ha (25–45 acres) | 320–420 m (1,050–1,400 ft) | Pennymuir I: almost 18 ha (44 acres), 520 × 343 m (1,710 × 1,125 ft), probably six gates |
| Large campaign series | 25 ha (63 acres) | about 500 m (1,650 ft) | About 20 camps in two lines north of the Forth, from the Stirling area towards Stracathro |
| Very large campaign series | 45–67 ha (110–165 acres) | 670–820 m (0.4–0.5 mi) | Camps north of the Forth (45–54 ha) and on Dere Street, the main Roman road north through southern Scotland (67 ha) |

**How many soldiers?** Pennymuir I is said to have had room for two full legions, roughly 8,000–12,000 men. That is very roughly 500–700 soldiers per hectare (estimate). So an army of 10,000 in a tight, ordered camp needs about 15–20 ha (35–50 acres). A looser medieval camp, with baggage, horses and camp followers, spread far wider: the Viking camp at Torksey covered at least 55 ha for several thousand people.

> **Map tip:** On a local map of a siege, draw the besieged town first. Then draw the camp as a rectangle or ring of huts and tents on dry ground beside the main road or the river landing, a few hundred metres to about 1 km (0.6 mi) from the walls, with a market square and the commander's quarters inside. Give it a name, as real soldiers did (*Villeneuve-la-Hardie*). For 10,000 soldiers, allow about 15–20 ha, roughly 400 × 450 m (1,300 × 1,500 ft), and more for horses and followers. On a kingdom map a siege camp is only a symbol (a closed dashed rectangle with rounded corners), and only on a map with a date. On a later map, leave old camps as the same outline drawn grey and labelled "Camp" or "Roman Camp", or, if the besiegers won and stayed, as a small grid-planned town with a religious or military name (*Santa Fe* means "Holy Faith").

> **Later era (1500s+):** Santa Fe's plan, two main streets crossing at a central square with a gate at each end, is often seen as a forerunner of the grid towns that Spain planted in the Americas. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** Where monsters roam the wilds, every army halt needs a defended camp, as the Romans built every evening. Draw chains of earthwork camps (closed dashed rectangles) a day's march apart along old campaign roads, and let a few of them grow into towns.

## Outposts in wild and contested land

### Frontier posts

Real frontiers were zones, not lines (see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md)). Their military layout was usually:

- **Forward line:** small castles, towers and fortified villages at fords, passes and wells, held by settlers or military orders.
- **Rear line:** big fortress towns a day or two behind, where the army and stores were kept. The Arabs facing Byzantium called the forward zone the *thughur* ("openings") and the rear zone the *awasim* ("the defences").
- **No man's land between:** thinly settled, raided every summer.

Examples:
- **Iberia:** the Christian–Muslim frontier moved south over centuries, from the Duero valley (10th century) to the Tagus (Toledo taken 1085) and finally Granada (1492). Castile's own name comes from its castles. Fortress towns, castles and military orders (Calatrava from 1158, Santiago from 1170) held each new frontier. The Granada frontier lasted about 250 years (1240s–1492). Both sides lined it with castles and *atalayas* (watchtowers).
- **Prussia and the Baltic:** the Teutonic Order advanced from 1231 along the Vistula and the coast, building a castle and a town together at each step.

> **Later era (1500s+):** The best-known early modern frontier belt is the Habsburg **Military Frontier** (German *Militärgrenze*, Croatian *Vojna krajina*), facing the Ottomans in Croatia and Hungary. Austrian troops began to garrison Croatian border forts in 1522. From the 1550s (1553 is the usual founding date) it became a permanent system, partly paid for by the Austrian and Bohemian lands. By the end of the 1560s the Habsburg border-fort system had about 100–120 forts of all sizes along a border of almost 1,000 km (620 mi), from the Adriatic to Transylvania. In the later 1500s about 20,000–22,000 paid soldiers held them. That is very roughly one fort every 8–10 km (5–6 mi) (estimate). Between the forts were small outposts and palisades at river crossings: in 1601–03 the local lord in Međimurje (northern Croatia), György Zrínyi, set up nine outposts along the Mura to guard its crossings, several of them with 100 guards each. Behind the forts the Habsburgs settled Christian refugees from Ottoman lands as **armed peasants**: free land and no feudal dues in return for military service. The first came in the 1530s (to Žumberak), a large wave came in 1597–1600, and the *Statuta Valachorum* ("Vlach Statutes") of 1630 obliged every man over 16 to serve. New star forts anchored the belt, such as Karlovac (begun 1579 as a six-pointed star), the headquarters of the Croatian frontier. The Military Frontier lasted until 1881. On a 1500s–1600s map, draw such a frontier as a line of forts and posts at the crossings, with a band of soldier-villages behind it. See [What Changes After 1500](09-later-era-1500-1650.md).

### Toll castles on rivers

- **Where:** narrow, busy stretches of navigable river, especially in gorges where ships cannot avoid the bank.
- **Numbers:** the Rhine Gorge (between Bingen/Rüdesheim and Koblenz) has about 40 castles in 65 km (40 mi), one every 1.5–2 km (about 1 mi). Many of them collected tolls. Along the whole navigable Rhine, recorded toll stations grew from 19 at the end of the 1100s to 44 by 1300 and 62 by 1400.
- **How it worked:** Pfalzgrafenstein (built 1326–27 by King Louis IV) stands on an island in the Rhine at Kaub. A chain could block the channel. Ships paid at the customs house in Kaub. Ships that refused were seized, and their crews were held in the castle's dungeon until a ransom was paid.
- **Who owned them:** the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne and Trier, the Count Palatine and noble families. Tolls were a pillar of their power.

### Robber-knight castles

When royal power was weak, small lords used their castles to rob travellers, demand illegal tolls and hold merchants for ransom. Germans later called them *Raubritter* ("robber knights").
- During the German Interregnum (1250–73), toll stations multiplied.
- In 1254 Rhine cities and princes formed the Rhenish League. It took and destroyed robber castles: about 10–12 strongholds within three years.
- In 1282 King Rudolf of Habsburg destroyed more robber castles, among them Sooneck.

### Military-order houses

- **Frontier castles:** the orders held the hardest frontier castles: Krak des Chevaliers, Safed, Montfort, Calatrava, Malbork.
- **Commanderies in the home countries:** most of the orders' houses in western Europe were not forts. A **commandery** (or **preceptory**) was a manor farm with a chapel and a small community, run to send money, horses and food to the frontier. The Templars had nearly 1,000 commanderies and fortifications at their height, and 15,000–20,000 members, only about one-tenth of them knights.

> **Map tip:** On a frontier, mark the march with hatching or a fading colour band, the guide's symbol for a frontier zone (see [Capitals, Realms and Borders](03-capitals-and-borders.md)). Put small forts at every ford, pass and well along its front edge, and two or three fortress towns a day or two's march behind them. The whole military zone, from the forward forts back to the fortress towns, is then about 30–80 km (20–50 mi) deep. Leave the land in front nearly empty. On a busy river gorge, crowd the cliffs with toll castles (small castle icons, with a toll bar and "T" across the river). Put one military-order commandery in the countryside of each inland county, drawn as a small church with a shield, not as a castle.

> **Fantasy twist:** A wilderness full of monsters works like a hostile neighbour. Its edge becomes a frontier zone even inside one realm, with forward posts, refuge towers and fortified villages, and a rear line of strong towns. Where dwarves or others hold mountain passes, the pass fort may belong to them, not to the human kingdoms on either side.

## Watchtowers, beacons and coastal warning

> **Rule of thumb:** Warning beats walls. A chain of fires on hilltops could carry a message hundreds of kilometres in an hour, faster than any rider.

**Beacons.** A beacon is a pile of fuel or an iron fire basket on a hill or tower, kept ready to light.
- **Byzantine beacon line (9th century, under Emperor Theophilos):** about 720 km (450 mi) from the fortress of Loulon near the Cilician Gates to Constantinople, with about nine main stations. Stations stood about 55–100 km (35–60 mi) apart on high peaks: closer in rugged country, farther in open country. Modern experiments suggest a message could cross the whole line in about one hour. Each of the 12 hours of the day stood for a set message. The hour at which the first fire was lit told the capital which message was meant.
- **Scottish Borders (Act of 1455):** one bale (fire) meant the English were coming; two bales together meant they were "coming indeed". Watches were kept at fords and passes.
- **Islamic coasts:** ribats were built at intervals along the North African shore so they could signal to each other. From Monastir's watchtower, signals were exchanged at night with neighbouring ribats. Sousse, the next big ribat up the coast, is about 20 km (12 mi) away.

**Spacing.** Beacon intervals depend on height and haze. In ordinary hilly country, plan for about **5–20 km (3–12 mi)** between beacons. Between high mountain peaks in dry air, 55–100 km (35–60 mi) is possible. These spacings are estimates.

**Watchtowers** stood at passes, fords, wells and frontier hilltops. They held a handful of men whose job was to see, signal and run, not to fight.

> **Later era (1500s+):** Dense chains of coastal towers are mostly a 16th-century answer to Barbary and Ottoman raids. Genoa built about 85–90 towers around Corsica in 1530–1620 (a 1617 list counts 86). Each was usually about 12 m (39 ft) high and held 2–6 guards. Smoke, fire or a conch horn could put the whole island on alert within a few hours. Spain and the Kingdom of Naples built similar chains.

> **Map tip:** Draw beacon chains as flame dots on hilltops leading from the border or coast to the nearest castle and on to the capital. Label a few hills "Beacon Hill" or "Watch Hill"; such names are common in Britain. On a raided coast, add a watchtower (a small triangle) on every headland that can see the next one.

## Where battles happen

> **Rule of thumb:** Battles happen where armies must pass or where one side must save a besieged place. Pitched battles were rare and risky. Sieges and raids were normal war.

Historians such as John Gillingham point out that many successful commanders avoided pitched battle almost entirely. When battles did happen, they clustered in a few kinds of place:

| Where | Why | Real examples |
|---|---|---|
| River crossings | The attacker must cross; the defender waits on the far bank | Stirling Bridge (1297); the fight at the Blanchetaque ford before Crécy (1346) |
| Near a besieged castle or town | The relieving army must come to the siege, and the besiegers must stop it | Lincoln (1141 and 1217); Muret (1213); Bannockburn (1314, near besieged Stirling Castle) |
| On the main road to a capital or a port | One army blocks the other's line of march | Hastings (1066, on the road to London); Agincourt (1415, on the road to Calais) |
| Near water in dry country | Armies must drink | Hattin (1187), where the crusader army ran out of water |

The defender usually picks ground with a slope in front and protected flanks: woods, villages, marsh or a river. At Crécy the English stood on a slope with their flanks resting on two villages. At Agincourt woods on either side narrowed the field.

> **Map tip:** Name a battlefield after the nearest village and mark it with crossed swords and the year. Put it near a ford, a bridge, a road junction or a besieged castle, never in the middle of nowhere. Three or four famous battlefields in a kingdom's history are plenty.

### Ruined and active castles

- In England only about one-third of all castle sites were in use at any one time. The rest were abandoned, never finished, or replaced.
- So a map dated 1300 should show many **abandoned mottes and ringworks** from the 1070s–1150s, especially in old border zones that are now peaceful.
- By 1450, many 12th-century castles were half-ruined. The crown kept up only the important ones.
- Old hillforts and Roman forts are often reused (Old Sarum, Pevensey). Others stay as grassy earthworks with names like "Castle Hill" or "Camp" (old army camps are covered in [Camps and siege towns](#camps-and-siege-towns)). Ancient ruins in general are covered in [Religious, Cultural, Legal and Ancient Sites](08-religious-cultural-and-ancient-sites.md).

> **Rule of thumb:** For every active castle, draw one or two ruins or earthworks in long-settled land. In a peaceful core, more ruins; in a newly conquered frontier, almost none (everything there is new).

### How many to draw

Per 1,000 km² (about 390 sq mi) of land, at about 1300:

| Zone | All sites ever built | Active castles | Show on a kingdom map | Show on a local map |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peaceful lowland core | 4–8 | 1–3 | The county town's castle, plus 1 major baronial castle per 2,000–3,000 km² | All active castles plus ruins |
| Average (England and Wales overall) | ~12 | ~4 | Royal and major castles only, about 1 per 1,000 km² | All |
| Hilly lands with many small lords (German type) | ~25 | ~8–12 (estimate) | Major castles and toll castles | All, clustered on spurs and river cliffs |
| March or contested border | 35–60 | 12–20 | All major castles; draw the border as a hatched band studded with castle icons | All, including mottes and towers |
| Raided border of the late Middle Ages | 8–10 castles plus many tower houses | Most of them | Castles and the main tower houses | Every tower house and bastle |
| Thin conquered frontier (Prussia type) | 1–3 | 1–3 | All of them, each paired with a town | All |

The "active" column assumes about one-third of sites are in use at once, as in England. It is an estimate for other regions.

**Map scale guide:**
- **Continent map:** show only capitals' citadels, the greatest frontier fortresses, famous fortress cities and naval arsenals. As a suggestion, about 1 per 20,000–50,000 km² (8,000–19,000 sq mi).
- **Kingdom map:** add royal castles, county-town castles, border fortresses and the main border chain.
- **Local map:** show everything: castles, ruins, mottes, tower houses, fortified churches, beacons and watchtowers.

> **Map tip:** Check your castles with three questions. (1) What does each one control? (2) Can it be supplied by water or from rich farmland nearby? (3) Is the density right for the zone: sparse in the core, dense on the border? Common errors are covered in [Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them](11-common-mistakes.md).

> **Fantasy twist:** If mages can break walls like cannon, castles should evolve early into low, thick, star-shaped forts. If enemies can teleport or fly over borders, strong citadels inside cities matter more than border chains. Long-lived races (elves, dwarves) may keep very old fortresses in use, so their lands show fewer ruins and older styles.

## Quick summary

- Every fortification controls something: a route, a crossing, a town, a border, an estate, a refuge or a lookout. If it controls nothing, delete it.
- Types change with time: earth-and-timber mottes and ringworks (c. 1000–1150), stone keeps and curtain walls (1150–1250), concentric castles and town walls (1250–1350), then tower houses and fortified manors (1350–1500). From the 1500s come star forts and artillery forts.
- Best sites: fords, bridges, passes, spurs, river bends, confluences, headlands, and hills above towns. German castles were mostly on high ground; English castles mostly beside villages and towns.
- England and Wales had about 1,761 castle sites (one per ~86 km², or 33 sq mi), but only 500–600 were in use at one time: an active castle every 16–17 km (10–11 mi).
- Border zones are roughly 5–10× denser: the Welsh March had one castle site per 17–28 km² (7–11 sq mi), a gap of 4–6 km (2.5–4 mi). In a peaceful core, working castles stand about 20–30 km (12–19 mi) apart. Conquest chains put a castle about every day's march (20–40 km, 12–25 mi) along a coast or river.
- Castles pair with towns: castle at the edge of town, on the high point or by the river, with the market at its gate.
- Garrisons were tiny: 5–20 in peace, 30–60 for a new frontier castle, a few hundred (rarely up to about 1,200) in a great siege. Only giant frontier fortresses held 2,000 or more.
- Field armies were about 0.2–0.5% of a realm's population: 10,000–15,000 for England in the 1300s, briefly 32,000 at most.
- Armies march 13–20 km (8–12 mi) a day, campaign from late spring to autumn, and follow rivers and coasts for supply. A stationary army eats out the land within weeks.
- Long sieges and winter quarters turn camps into temporary towns with markets (Calais 1346–47, Neuss 1474–75); a few became real towns (Torksey, Santa Fe 1491). Roman marching camps (nearly 500 known in Britain, mostly 10–25 ha, a day's march apart) survive as rectangular "camp" earthworks.
- Add muster towns near borders, an armoury in the capital, an arsenal at the main port, toll castles on river gorges and beacon chains from the border to the capital.
- Battles happen at crossings, near sieges and on roads to capitals and ports, and are named after the nearest village.
